# Chapter 69 — One Cleaning

Jo Verrick opened the housing at the first bell on the Thursday with a chisel she had borrowed from a house on the second level and a lamp set on the frame because the housing is under the floor and you cannot see into a thing you cannot see into, and nine people stood in the anteroom and nobody was on the corridor because the corridor was shut and the school was at the top of nine steps with a teacher and nine children who had been sent up and had nowhere to go.

"That is the first cost," Ilsa Norr said, "and I have had it since Saturday, and I am saying it now at the first bell so that it is said before the work and not after it. Nine children and a teacher and no corridor for a day. I will have it in the book and I will not be argued with about it, and I am not arguing with you about it either, Jo. I am just putting it in the same hour as the chisel."

"Noted, and I am reading it out now," said Dalla Sen, "in her words, with the time on it, and the time is before the work, and I am writing that down too, because in a year the order these two things happened in is the whole of what this day was."

---

The housing came off in pieces, and the first thing Jo Verrick saw was that the bolt-hole had opened about a quarter of an inch at the back, and the second thing was the seat.

She held it up into the lamp so that nine people who had never seen a pump's delivery seat could see a pump's delivery seat, and Dalla Sen entered, at Jo's request, that it was held up, and that nine people looked at it, and that it was not a thing anybody in the room had known what to look like.

"Everybody look," Jo said, "because I have been describing this thing to you in words for eleven weeks and there is not one of you who has seen it, and that is my fault and not yours, and it is about to be the end of what I can describe."

It was a ring of bronze about four inches across and three-quarters of an inch deep, and the inside of it was scored with grooves you could take a thumbnail into, and one edge of it was thin as a coin in a place about the size of a thumb, and the whole of it had a bright crescent worn into it on the delivery side, and the crescent was bright and the rest of it was black.

"Right," Jo said. "That is a seat that has been sitting in a jet of sump water five minutes out of every forty for eleven weeks, and the wear is on the delivery side and it is not even, and the thin place is where it is going to open, and I want somebody to write down that I showed it to nine people and that nine people saw it, because in four years somebody is going to ask me whether I looked."

She put it on the bench, and the room looked at it, and nobody spoke for a bit, and the anteroom heard the pump's neighbours through the floor.

There was a quarter of an inch in it that had not been there in the seventh week, and Marek Venn stood two feet from the bench with his left hand already half out of his pocket for the chalk, and he did not take it out.

That was the rule and he had never been able to say why it was the rule in a way that satisfied himself. It was not politeness. Jo Verrick had a hand on that housing and had a number on it in her own hand with a time on it, and the number was hers, and the quarter inch was inside the number; if he had walked up and chalked the bolt-hole and said a figure in a full room, then the figure would have become his in about nine seconds, and everybody would have said *the engineer measured it*, and in four years somebody would have written *measured by the engineer* on a form and the clerk would have read it and believed it, and the one person in that room who could say what the quarter inch meant was a woman holding a lamp above a floor she had opened with a borrowed chisel.

So the chalk stayed in his pocket, and he was, for the second time in three days, a man standing in a room where the best thing he could do was nothing, and it is not the first time in his life, and it was never going to get easier, and there was not one single thing in the room that he was qualified to say.

"Put it back," said Jo.

"Jo—"

"**Put it back.** And I am going to say the number, and I have not said it in eleven weeks, and I have said it out loud in this room every week for eleven weeks, and I am going to say it now and I am going to move it, and nobody in this room is allowed to say that it moved because of the look.

"Two or fewer. That is what it has said since the sixth week, in my hand, with a time on it, and it is the only number in this building that has not moved while everything else has, and I have been proud of it in a way I am not going to be proud of anything else this month.

"It is **one.** I have spent one. There is one left, and there is no third, and there is no bronze in this Hearthhold and there is no wood, and the housing is going back together tonight on a seat with a thumb's worth of it left in it and a crescent worn into it that I can see from the anteroom.

"And I am going to say the other half, because I would be able to and it would be easier. **The look cost a day of school and a day of a pot and it bought me the knowledge that I was right, and I am not going to pretend that is a fair trade, because a day of school is nine children and a day of a pot is a number on my board, and what I got was a thing I already believed.**

"Zero is a number I could write. I am not writing zero. Zero is a decision, and a decision gets made on a day with a man standing in front of you and a reason, and I am not making it on a Thursday with a borrowed chisel and nine children at the top of the steps, and if somebody in this room thinks that is cowardice then they can come and be at the frame with me on the day I write it and they can put their name under mine."

"Written," said Dalla Sen, "in her own hand, and read out twice because she has asked me to in the last fortnight, and I am putting under it in my own hand that the estimate moved from two or fewer to one on the Thursday of this week, on a named work, with the seat in her hand and nine witnesses, and that no person in this room objected to the number and one person objected to the leaving of it."

---

"It is one," said Tams Quill, from the bench. "So I am going to do the only thing I know how to do with a number like that, which is to put it where it can be argued with." He turned the board. "**Held, not spent.** One cleaning, on the seat, on the fourth day, on a named work, with the part in the housing that opened up a quarter inch at the back. And I am not putting it in *spent* and I want the room to hear why I am not putting it in *spent*, because I have put every screw in this book in *spent* for eleven weeks and I have been proud of that as well.

"It is not spent. It went into a thing and the thing is still there. **Held is the column for things that are still doing their job and I have had nothing in it but a shut until now.**"

---

The corridor came back at about the fourth bell and the school came down the nine steps and went through at a walk, and the anteroom cleared, and nobody said anything about the day because everybody had been in it.

Later that morning Mikka Hale said, flatly, from the frame, to nobody in particular:

"Two things and I am going to say them in the order they are going to matter to you.

"**One.** Sixteen went to fifteen this morning and I wrote it on my board at the first bell and nobody asked me to and nobody argued with me, and a day of a pot is a day and the three days in seven that the corridor is shut are not days of a pot either and I have stopped pretending those three are free. The number is going to keep going and it is going to keep going at me, and I want it read at the fifth bell every day, out loud, by a person, the way the throat is read. I am not going to be the only person in this building who knows what the number is.

"**Two.** And this is the one I have been carrying since Friday and I did not want to say it in front of the school, and the school has gone, so."

The anteroom went quiet in the particular way it had learned to go quiet.

"Leena Orr's litter is on this side of the shut waist, and the clinic is on the other side, and on the three days in seven when that corridor is shut she is in a room with me in it and the door to the rest of this boundary is corded. I can come up here. I can come up nine steps and I can do what I have to do up here, and I will, and it is not a tragedy and I am not asking anybody to make it one.

"But I am going to say the rest of it, and then I am going back to the frame. **I cannot stop a bleed up nine steps in a corridor that is two foot eight wide with a litter in it, and I have been in this building eleven weeks and that is the first thing I have not been able to do, and I have not said it out loud because saying it out loud makes it a thing this boundary is doing to a woman from above, and it is not. It is a shut I asked for. I asked for the corridor to shut. I would ask for it again this afternoon.** And I would like the words *I asked for it* in the ledger in my own hand next to the days of the school, because in a year this week is going to be read as a thing that happened to people, and most of the things that happened this week happened because people asked for them, and that is the entire difference and it is not a small one."

"Wrote," said Dalla Sen, "in her own words, and read out, and I have asked her whether she wants it with the time on it and she says yes, and it is the only entry in that book in eleven weeks that a person has asked me to put a time on their own cost."

---

And the woman from the wash rooms said her thing at the sixth bell, from the top of the three steps, and she had come up on the day she was not needed, which is the first time in eleven weeks that anybody has noticed her doing that.

"I have got a condition to say out loud," she said, "and I have had it since Saturday, and I have been waiting until the week was not full so that I could say it in a room that was not going to forget it by the second bell.

"Last week I shut this route, and I said out loud why, and the reason was that Jo's hiss had gone thin and I am not a pump hand and I do not know what a thin hiss is, and that is exactly why I shut it. That is the honestest thing I have said in this building and it is on a page and it is mine.

"And then on Friday the room arranged that I stand on a string at five in the morning and at the end of the day, three days a week, six crossings, with Jo Verrick on the lever and a pump I cannot hear. **So the thing I shut the route for is now the thing I am personally standing in six times a week, and nobody in this room arranged that on purpose and that is exactly what makes it unbearable.**

"I cannot be given what a thin hiss is. There is no thin and there is no full any more, the vocabulary is dead, Jo said so herself on Wednesday and she was right, and a person cannot be taught to read an instrument that has one note. So there is nothing to tell me and nobody to ask. All I have got is the same ear I have always had, and a rope, and the fact that I am the one who has to say the word.

"So I want one thing written, in my hand, and I have brought it written out because I did not want to do it in front of you.

"**When I say the word, it stays shut until somebody who knows looks at the reason. And nobody in this room is to argue with me at the moment I say it, and nobody is to argue with me afterwards, and if it turns out I was wrong then the cost of my being wrong is mine and not the cost of the next person.** That is the eleven words I said in the ninth week, and I have thought about them all week and I would not change one of them, and I am saying them again in a colder month because they are the only thing in this building I have ever been sure of."

Dalla Sen read it out, and the woman made her do the whole of it again from the top, and Renn Vale asked for it to be copied into Renn's hand as well, and the clerk said no, and Renn said why, and the clerk agreed with her.

"It is hers," Renn said. "It is not mine and it is not yours and it is not in a slate. If a boundary's refusals are written by the boundary they are policy, and hers is not policy, and if it ever stops being hers then it is a different sentence and it will have to be argued for again."

---

At the fifth bell, Tams Quill read the cold out of his board and made Pell Roke say the thing he had been turning over all week, which was that a count taken in the cold is not the same count as a count taken in the warm, and that nobody had been writing the temperature against anything.

"Six crossings on this stair this week," he said, "and I have got them in a column, and I have not got a temperature against one of them, and that is my fault and not a clerk's, and I have been counting in two rooms for eleven weeks without once asking what the room was.

"And a boy at the top of these nine steps has been telling me since Wednesday that a count is a photograph. So here is the photograph. **Six crossings, in the cold, at the first bell and the fifth, with the bar cold and the plinth cold and the check on one note, and on the third of those crossings a plinth reader believed a plinth for half a second because a cold thing under a cold hand does not move the way a warm thing does.** That is the week. And if somebody writes it in a book in a year without the word *cold* in it, then the book is wrong, and the book will be wrong in the way that is easiest to be wrong in, which is by looking correct."

Pell Roke said, from the bench, without moving, and Tams Quill wrote it down and read the line out twice, which is the first time in eleven weeks that the clerk has put a boy's words into the air without the boy asking for it:

"Six is a photograph of six cold mornings. It is not six of anything else. Nobody is to use it."

---

And the frame hissed all week, thin, and the seat went back in on the Thursday night with a bolt-hole a quarter inch open at the back, and the pump ran twenty-six strokes and six minutes off and nineteen strokes, and delivered, and came back to the boundary on the low note that is the only note it has now, and on the Friday the water in the dip was four inches from the chalk line Leena Orr had put on the frame, and the line had not moved, because a chalk line is a reference and does not move.
