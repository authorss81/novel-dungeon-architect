# Chapter 41 — The Floor Under the School

The grit had to go somewhere, and there was nowhere, and every person who had ever been in this room had known that before anybody opened the cover.

It was a day the school did not run, which was three days in seven, which meant the corridor was shut, which meant the anteroom's ten was the whole of the sheltered space in this boundary. Mikka had said that out loud in the thirty-fifth day and had not enjoyed saying it and said it anyway, and now here it was again, in a morning, with a cover coming up off a floor and four hundredweight of somebody else's century going into canvas sacks along a wall.

The second bell cover check had been done before the light was properly in the room. That was the rota, and the rota was Mikka's, and the rota was a list of people and not a list of jobs, and on this morning it was the woman from the wash rooms, because Mikka had asked her and she had said yes in one word, which is how she said everything, and had gone down on her knees at the kerb with a lamp and said *grit* in a voice nobody had to raise, and gone away again.

The school corner floor had a lid on it. Two boards, one each side of the channel, in a dressed kerb, with two lift holes and a bar that did not rattle. It had been six weeks since Ilsa Norr had reported grit coming up through the joint, three since the bar had been used to lift it and find a two-finger gap under the wall plate, and two since a stair-pressure turn had lifted the whole thing at the kerb on the wash-room side and put a line of white grit along a floor that had been swept that morning.

Jo Verrick had the bar. She worked the lift holes one at a time and she worked them slowly, and she counted under her breath, and the boards came up together and laid down on the kerb with their own weight and not her shoulder.

While the cover was off, the room did not speak. That was not a rule. It was what happened.

---

Ninety years of grit does not come out tidily. It came out in buckets, and the buckets were household things, and the canvas sacks were Mikka's, and the man who filled them was Oren Sill, because Oren filled whatever was put in front of him and did not ask what it was for, and Tams Quill tied the necks, because a man with a right hand that could not close could still hold a sack's throat open with his left and pull a cord round it with his teeth and a loop of that cord in his fingers, which is not easy and which nobody commented on.

Pell Roke had the water mark on the bench by the school door and called it out loud every half hour to a person, because the rota said so and the rota was people and not jobs, and Isel Ferrow read the mark beside him and was not to be first at anything for a month, and both of them were right, and Jo wrote down that both of them had been right.

They got two feet down in the first hour, which was the depth Mikka had said the void was, and then the grit changed.

"Listen to that," Iven said.

It was not a sound. It was the *absence* of the scrape. Below two feet the grit had packed, and packed grit does not come away, and it comes away in slabs, and the slab came up whole and about the size of a hearth stone, and it weighed what a hearth stone weighs, and two people took it.

"Hard packed," Jo said. "Hard packed, and dry, and that is forty years of a floor settling onto itself."

Marek knelt at the edge on his left knee and put the lamp down the hole on a length of its own handle, held at the top by Oren's other hand, and the light went down four feet and stopped, and came back off dressed stone.

He had expected to see the wall plate. He got the wall plate, two fingers of gap under it, and under that a floor he had never seen, and it was not grit and it was not a channel. It was cut stone, laid, and it was the same dressed work as the kerb at his knee, and it was not level.

"It runs," he said. "The floor runs. It goes off to the left, toward the door, and it goes down as it goes."

"Left is the school door," Nera said, from the doorway, and then stopped, because *left* was not a direction anybody in the room should have used yet, and she said it again in different words. "It runs toward the common landing, and it runs down."

Iven got on his knees beside the hole, which took a while and which he did not be helped with, and put his own face over the edge, and was not in it, and was asked by three people at once to be careful, and said he was.

"Two feet by eight," he said. "Give or take a hand, and I have never been able to give or take a hand, so take it as two by eight. Dressed floor, laid in courses, a joint every foot, and the joint is tight, which means it was laid and it was *meant* to be laid. And here—" and he put his thumb's nail against something down in the corner of the lamp's light, "—here. Look at the lamp, not at me. Down the left side. In the stone."

Jo put her head where his was and looked.

"A groove," she said.

"A groove the width of two fingers," Iven said, "and it is a hundred years deep, and it is polished, and it is not a drain, and it is not a water line, and everybody in this room knows what a two-finger running groove polished to a shine is."

Nobody had to say it out loud. It was the width of the groove in the hollow under the near landing, and Leena Orr had it in her survey notes from the thirty-third day in her own words, and it was the shape of the thing that had been stopped in that hollow with a piece of a wash door.

"It is the same run," Marek said.

"It is the same run," Iven agreed. "One line. It came up out of somewhere under the wash room floor and along here and under the school door and into the hollow under the stair, and it ran through the block, and it went on somewhere else, and it did all of that a hundred years ago. And three sections of it were taken out in the nineteenth week of the long cold year and given to a school, and one of those sections is a door in a wash room that a woman sleeps behind, and the third is a wedge-shaped piece that a woman drove into a hole by hand and packed with chippings, and it is still in the hole."

He sat back on his heels.

"Forty years," he said. "There has been a break in that run under this floor since before I could read, and you have all been breathing out of it."

---

The finding went to the room in the wrong order first, which Jo corrected, because Jo corrected the order of everything.

"Stop," she said. "That is a survey statement and it has not been said to the people who keep the floor. It goes to them first, and it goes to them in their own words, and I am not writing it down until it has been said, because a thing written down is a thing that has been decided, and nobody has decided anything yet."

"Nobody has," Ilsa said.

She was in the doorway. She had been in the doorway since the cover came off, with a slate under her arm, and she had not gone closer, and none of the crew had asked her to, and Jo had noticed that and had not said so, and said so afterward, to Dalla, and to nobody else.

"What have you found," Ilsa said. "Not in your words. In mine if you can manage it."

Marek tried four ways and got out of it. Leena Orr, on the frame at the service bend with her slate on her knees and her right arm in its sling, did it for him, and did it badly on purpose, in plain words, and Ilsa corrected two of them, and both corrections went in.

"Under the far end of my floor," Ilsa said, having it, "there is a room that is not a room. It is two feet deep in old dirt and it has dressed stone at the bottom of it and a groove in the stone the width of two fingers, and the groove is a hundred years old, and the line that made it went through the stair. And there has been a hole in that line under my floor since before the school was a school, and the air my children breathe in this room has been coming up out of that hole, and the grit my children put their hands in has been coming up out of the same hole."

"That is the whole of it," Nera said. "I would add one thing and it is not mine to add to a teacher's floor, so I will say it and Ilsa may leave it off."

She came into the room for the first time, which she did twice a day and did not make a thing of, and stood with her back to the valve's string.

"Stopping the air off that hole does not stop the pressure," she said. "It stops the grit. The grit is the smaller problem. The problem is that a hole has been breathing into a room with nine children in it for forty years, and the moment you shut it, the air that was coming up has to go somewhere, and I cannot tell you where, because I have never been down that void and I cannot see the bottom of it from a lamp held by a man on a floor."

"You want it left open," said somebody at the back.

"I want it shut," Nera said. "I want it shut and I want it shut properly and I want it shut with a floor over it and not a lid, because a lid is a thing that gets lifted. And I want it shut by the people who keep that floor, and I want to say the cost out loud, and the cost is that whatever is coming up through that hole today stops coming up today, and nobody in this room knows what it will do instead."

That was the argument, and it lasted forty minutes, and it was not held by the crew.

Ilsa refused first, and refused in three parts, which is how she refused.

"I will not have it left open. That is the first part and it is not negotiable and it does not depend on what anybody finds underneath. I have nine children on that floor and ten on market days, and one of them puts his hand flat on the joint to feel where the dirt is, and I have watched him do it, and I have stopped telling him to stop, because telling a child to stop touching a thing he can feel is a way of making him lie about it. So the mouth stops. Second part: I will not have it called a fill. A fill is a thing you do to get a lid onto. A floor is a thing you lay and then walk on and then have to be true for forty years, and I have watched this building lie to us for forty years through two fingers of daylight, and I am not having a floor over that hole that is only a thicker version of the same lie. Third part, and this is the one that matters: whoever lays it has to be a person I can name, and not a stranger, and not one of you six."

Jo wrote all three down. She wrote the third one twice, because Ilsa made her.

Then Mikka, and Mikka's was the practical one and it was about sacks.

"It does not go in the anteroom," she said. "I have four hundredweight of dirt coming up out of a floor and I have a room that holds ten people including a litter and it is the only room in this boundary that can be got into, and the day the corridor is shut it is the only room anybody can be sent to. It does not go in there. It does not go past the school door, because the waist is packed in fill that is gone and three wedges are what is holding what is left of it, and I am not putting four hundredweight on that floor to make a heap I then have to carry back out. So it stays on this floor, in sacks, against the wall, and it gets packed back into the hole in the same week and the cover goes over that. And the cost of that is that this floor is a builder's floor for two days and the school does not run and the nine of them come down the kitchen line with the pot on, in the arrangement we have had since the twenty-seventh day, and I will say that in my own words and I will not have it softened into a hardship by anybody who is not the one boiling."

"The two days are mine," Ilsa said. "Write them in the school column and not in yours."

"They are in both columns," Mikka said, "and I will not have mine rounded."

And then Renn, who had not come, and who sent a sentence by the woman from the wash rooms, who came to the door and said it and went away again.

"'It is under the wash room floor, and I know what that means, and you are not cutting my mother's floor, and the line under it is not yours to find. Find it where I can see you find it.' Renn Vale. And she says that if it takes four days she will find out on the fourth and not before."

Nobody had asked Renn to say anything. Jo wrote it down with the name attached to it, and the name attached to it was the reason it was worth writing down.

Renn's sentence made one change and the change was the plan.

The line could not be looked for under the wash room floor, because the wash room floor was refused, and it had been refused in front of nine people and could not be re-asked this week for the convenience of a survey. And that meant the load the line was putting through the void had to be taken somewhere the crew could see and a person could stand, and the only such place within reach was the **common landing**, which is where the boundary stair begins, and which is one floor under a landing where four people sleep, and which is the only floor in that room that goes down to stone instead of to fill.

It was not a design. It was the place the other options had left.

---

They told the people who keep the floor, and they told them badly the first time, and the second telling was the one on the page.

Marek had written it in nine words on a slate, in his left hand, with the letters uneven, and he read it out and Leena took it out of his hands and would not give it back, and said that the words *stair will be given something* were the words of a man who had already decided what the finding was for, and that a room that has just been told there is a hundred-year-old break in a load line under it does not need to be told what a stranger intends to do about a stair.

"Say what you found," she said. "Not what you are going to do about it. Those are two days' work and you have done one of them."

So he said what they found. And then Nera said what it meant for the air, in her own words, and Iven said what it was — a running groove, a hundred years, one line, cut in three and given to a school — and Ilsa wrote on her slate, in chalk, in the room, standing up, in her own hand and not a clerk's:

*There is dressed stone under my floor with a groove in it a hundred years old, and the air my children breathe has been coming up out of the break in that groove since before the school was a school. The crew did not put it there and cannot take it out. I am going to have a floor laid over it and I am going to know what is under that floor for as long as I keep it. Ilsa Norr.*

Mikka read it over her shoulder and said, "Say the pot."

"The children come down the kitchen line with the pot on," Ilsa said, "and it is my arrangement and my cost and I would like it in the record that I decided it and that nobody put it to me."

Then Jo read back everything that had been decided, in her own hand, in the order a person leaving would find it, and the room took it apart twice.

The first change came from Jo herself, and it was the word *shut*.

"It is not a shut," she said. "A shut is a thing a valve does. Write what it is: *the void under the school corner's far end is cleared to the dressed stone, packed hard in the void, and floored with the cover already on the kerb, and the floor is Ilsa Norr's and is not on the crew's list.*"

The second came from Nera, and it was one word in a different line.

"Put *one* on it. *The mouth is given a fall to the channel and a floor over the void, in that order, and the fall is cut first.*" She had the cloth in her hand and she was not using it. "Because if you lay a floor over that void and the air is still coming up, you have built a lid on a throat and the next turn will find the corner of it. The fall goes first, and the grit that comes up it goes where the grit from the fall already goes, and only then does the floor go on."

"And I want the fall cut before the floor," Ilsa said, "and I want it said that way, because the two of them are two days and the day the floor goes on is the day somebody starts calling this room finished."

Nobody marked anything. The page went to Dalla Sen at the anteroom bench and into the ledger, in Dalla's hand, with the finding in Ilsa's words above the plan in Jo's hand, and it was read back before it was copied, and the copy was read back after that.

And the review that had been named in the fortieth day came due at that moment, because it was named as the first work-bell after the school corner floor had been opened and traced and the people who kept it had been told what was under it in their own words, and all three of those things had now happened, in that order, in front of witnesses, and Dalla said so out loud before anybody could pretend otherwise.

"Sit down," she said, and nobody sat, and she read it standing, and it took a quarter of an hour, and Jo's copy went into the school store in a locked box that Ilsa Norr held the key to and would not let Marek Venn near.

And then they went and found Nera Oduya, because the school corner was shut and the stair was not touched, and the whole of the rest of it depended on where the air went instead.
