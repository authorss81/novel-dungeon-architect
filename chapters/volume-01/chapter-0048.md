# Chapter 48 — What the Route Could Carry

Oren Sill gave the lever to Jo Verrick at the fourth bell on the Thursday, in one sentence, and neither of them made anything of it, and it is in the ledger because Dalla Sen writes down every change to a thing a person is holding.

"You have run it since the twenty-first," he said.

"I have run it since the twenty-first," Jo said, and took the timber lever in both hands and set her feet, and neither of them said the other thing, and the other thing was in Dalla's ledger an hour later because Dalla writes down what a ledger is for.

It was a school day, which made the corridor open, which made the sixth watch at the waist a post and not a formality, and Renn Vale stood in it on a plank with her coat off, four days in seven, unchanged, and she had not been asked and would not have been. Nera at the valve. Sefu at the wheel. Mikka at the clinic return. Dalla at the anteroom bench with the ledger, which meant the threshold bell needed a deputy, and Sefu asked Ruk Sella at the door in front of the room and told her what she was being given and she said yes in one word and repeated it back, third time.

And the fourth of the four people who could stop that work was on the far side of a slab for the best part of the morning, because the mother from the wash rooms had gone and nobody had asked her whether she would be needed here. Renn said that out loud before the pump came on, and nobody had an answer, and it went into the ledger in Dalla's hand with a time on it, and it is the first line in nine weeks of that ledger that nobody has a reason for and everybody is going to remember.

Ilsa Norr counted at the head of the crossing, at the school door, four out and four in, and she did it because she is the person in this building who has counted nine children off a floor twice in one day and knows what the counting costs.

The pump was slow to draw that day. Everybody in the anteroom could hear it being slow — the housing knocking in a longer beat than it had beaten in a fortnight, and the water in the dip coming down about a third as fast as the notch marks said it should, and Isel Ferrow calling the water mark out loud every half hour to a person and not to a board, and getting it right, and saying so, and being told to keep going.

The count was thirty-one.

Not twenty-nine. Thirty-one, and there were two of them in the room that had not been in the room on the counting day, and both of them were named on the page before the second crossing started, and the reason was written under them in Dalla's hand, and the reason was not anybody's fault and was not nothing.

Renn's neighbour from the upper rooms had come down in the night, because the wheel had gone at about the second bell and the door of that household is the one that will not stay shut against the wheel, and the household had come down to the anteroom and had slept on the bench, and the second of the two was the fevered child out of the shutter household, who had been in the clinic and was well enough to be walked and whose people had decided in the morning that a well child on a route was better than a well child on a bench in a room with a wheel in it.

"A building does not announce its own population," Pell Roke said, from the bench at the top of the nine steps, with his ear against the stone and his crutch across his knees, "and a count is a photograph, and I said that on a slate a week ago and I have not stopped saying it since and I will keep saying it until somebody puts a bell on it."

Nobody rang a bell on it. That is in the record.

Renn Vale had said on the Wednesday night that she would count at the head of the crossing, and on the Thursday morning she was told that a school day made the corridor open and the waist a post, and she took the waist, and she said the whole of it out loud before she took it so that nobody could afterwards say she had swapped one for the other. "I will not stand a watch on a stair day. I said it in the thirty-eighth day in front of nine people and I said it was wider than the wet seam. The wet seam is the waist, and I hold it four days in seven and today is not one of them, and somebody said so last night, and they were right to say it. So the waist is mine and the count is Ilsa's, and neither of those is the stair." Nobody argued, and Dalla wrote both halves down with the time on them.

The school went first, nine children at a walk, Ilsa Norr's own order, four and four and then the ninth child in the third crossing with three adults, because four at a time is four at a time and no child in this building goes on a slab on her own. They were quick, and they came back quick, and by the end of the third crossing there was nothing left of the school and the fourth crossing had four more adults in it who did not know one another and all of whom apologised to each other for taking up room.

Then the middle.

Then the interval came off four minutes early, because a pump that takes eleven minutes to draw a sump down does not care whose count it is inside, and Jo Verrick came off the lever on the twenty-fourth stroke instead of the twenty-sixth, and the four minutes went out of the day, and nobody in the anteroom was angry about it, because nobody in the anteroom had any idea yet that they were the four minutes, and because two minutes of margin is not a thing anybody says out loud before it is gone.

Twenty people had run by then, and eleven were still on the near side of a school door for three crossings, and the arithmetic of Tams Quill's board said that one of those three was never going to fit in a day when the pump had lost four minutes out of the two it had to spare. Nobody had said that out loud, because nobody at the school door had ever watched a pump take eleven minutes to draw a sump down and had any reason to think the two minutes were a real number and not a figure on a board.

The six minutes went by with the stair empty, which was correct, and the pump came back on, and the nineteen strokes started, and the sixth crossing started, and the plan was that the slow group would be out and back inside the nineteen and that the fast ones would go last.

"Next," Renn said. "The slow ones, and me at the back of them, and the four of you on the slab, and I am going to say the order out loud so that it is in the air and not only on a page: the mother from the wash rooms, then the two off the clinic return, then the fevered child's people, and go on the word *now* and not before."

"She is not asked," someone said.

"She was not asked," Renn said. "She insisted. I wrote it in my own hand on the day and you may read it, and she is going, and I am not going to stand here and be asked twice."

The four went out at the word *now*, and the slab took them, and it is written in the record that the slab went out well and that it was a clean crossing and that nobody on the far side of it was hurried, and that seven people were still on the near side of a school door with a bearing hand under the lip, waiting for two crossings that the day could not pay for.

The whole of that plan was built on a number that Nera Oduya would not put on a page she did not hold.

She had said so in the thirty-sixth day, in front of a full room, and she had said it again in the forty-sixth day when the window got shorter, and she had said it a third time at the review on Wednesday night, and what she had said all three times was that her count of forty-one turns was a working number, and that thirty-four of them came between one and nine minutes *after* the lever came off, and that she would not publish a figure a person could plan a school day against, and that the plan therefore had to be a plan for stopping and not a plan for timing.

The plan was for stopping. It said: nobody on the stair in the six. It did not say what happened if a turn came while the lever was still moving, because that had happened twice in forty-one and both times had frightened her and she had not been willing to write down a rate for something she had seen twice.

A turn came while the lever was moving.

The turn came two minutes into the nineteenth strokes.

Nera Oduya's voice went up the string in two words, and Isel Ferrow called it off the plinth at about the same moment, and Pell Roke, on the bench at the top of the nine steps, with his ear on the stone, called it eight seconds before either of them, out loud, in the voice of a fourteen-year-old who has been listening to a wheel through a floor for nine weeks.

"Turn," Pell said. "Turn, now, turn, eight, seven—"

The party was off the near landing in thirty-eight seconds, which is the best figure in the record since the fortieth day, and that is not a good thing to have written in a ledger about the best forty seconds of Marek Venn's life.

The four on the far side had the slab taken away from under them. It took about a second, where it used to take a second and a half, because the work is being done properly now and properly is faster than stubbornly, and that third of a second is on the shore and it is the only good arithmetic anybody got that day, and it did them no good at all. They had eleven feet of shaft between them and the water, and a rail, and no floor behind the rail, and a woman who had not been asked standing on the lip with her hand flat on the rail.

The slab came back. It always comes back. That is the one thing this mechanism has done reliably for nine weeks and it did it then.

And the near landing's lip is one stone, the whole length of it, with the line-block set into its base, and a person has to stand at the edge of that stone to get onto a slab that has come back, and the line comes tight through the block at the same moment, up the open face of the wall, into the head of a shore that Oren Sill packed himself on the second afternoon with a piece of sized wood and his own knee, and the shore held, and the wall held, and the common landing's floor took it, and the four inches of bright stone in the hollow's face did not grow by a mark.

Oren Sill was under the lip.

He was there because he was the only bearing hand in the room and because the plan said somebody would be, and he had said on the second afternoon of the shore that a shore's head has to be held while it first comes tight and that a fraction at the head is a fraction at the block, and that is a thing about a shore and not about a floor. He was there because on the Wednesday night, at the review, in front of eleven people, Dalla Sen had read out Mikka Hale's sentence about the window coming early while the middle of a relay was out, and Oren Sill had said, "Then somebody is under the lip," and eleven people had written it down, and eleven people had known it all night and all morning, and none of them had said it out loud again because by then it was on the page and on the page it was a sentence.

He got his shoulder against the underside of the stone and his back into the angle where the wall met the floor and he put his weight into it the way a man puts weight into a joint he expects to move, and he held it, and four people came across the slab and off it and past him onto the three steps, and the last of the four was a woman of sixty who had not been asked and who went past him without touching him, because she had told Renn Vale on the step that she would not be helped.

It held for the length of that. Marek Venn, on the third step, with the line's slack in his left hand, because he had not been let go of the slack since the fortieth day, watched the lip of a stair take a man's weight and hold it, and then watched it not hold it, and the not-holding was not a collapse and was not a fall and was about two inches, which is the figure Oren Sill himself had produced in the thirty-sixth day with a stone and a line tied to the pump plinth, and which eleven people had written on a board and had then, in the ordinary way, stopped looking at.

Nobody was on the far side by then. That is the sentence that matters, and it is in the ledger, and it is in the ledger because it is the only good thing anybody did that day and Jo Verrick insisted it went down in those words.

Oren Sill went into the throat, and the shaft is eleven feet, and there is water at the bottom of it on some days, and the light goes in and comes off the water at the bottom, and the party was out of the step in thirty-eight seconds and the near side was as found and the cord held and the hatch stayed latched and the anteroom cleared to the outer step, because the anteroom holds ten including one litter and every one of the ten went out the kitchen line inside a minute and a half, and the litter went first and came back first, and the four minutes and six seconds it took was in the record and the record says four minutes and six and Dalla Sen wrote it in her own hand and did not write a cause beside it.

Nobody went down the shaft. Not because they were brave or not brave. Because the stair is corded, and the throat is eleven feet of a hole nobody has ever seen the bottom of, and the ladder that goes into that well is a ladder with six weeks of standing water on its stiles, and Oren Sill's own frayed nine-metre line is tied round it four times and it is the reason the ladder has not fallen over this week, and taking a lamp down it in the dark while a wheel is turning would have been the one thing since the collapse that every single person in that anteroom would have agreed on and none of them would have been able to live with.

The wheel turned twice. Then it stopped. Then the cloth hung still.

Mikka Hale said it, because Mikka was the one who had to say it, and she said it standing up, in the anteroom, with the anteroom holding rather more people than the anteroom holds and no pot on, and she said it in the plainest words she had and then she said the second half of it, which is the half that took the room.

"He is dead," she said. "And I am not going to write *missing*, and here is why I am not going to write *missing*, and I have thought about it for six minutes and I have not had a pot on this whole time, which anybody who knows me will understand. A missing man in a building like this one is a debt. It is a debt that somebody else pays for, and the somebody else is always a woman with a door who is already carrying four people and a lead line she cannot answer for. And Renn Vale is not going to be told that her neighbour's neighbour might come back. So the record says he was last seen at the near lip, bearing, with four people behind him and the stair turning, and it says he is not recovered, and it says he is dead, in that order, and in that order because the order is what a person reading it in a year will need."

Nobody argued with the clinic, and the clinic was not arguing, and the anteroom was very quiet for about as long as it takes a pump to come off a lever.

Then Nera Oduya said, "Stop the work," and it was over.

They took four hours to clear the near landing and they abandoned everything on it.

That is the sentence and it is the cost and it is in Tams Quill's column called *spent*, and it has four items in it and it is the largest single entry in nine weeks of columns.

The cracked lamp stayed on the near landing's step, because the party went back out with a lamp and there is one lamp and a lamp that has been on a step in the dark for nine weeks is a lamp that is not going to be carried up nine steps by a man who is carrying something else, and because a lamp standing on a stone is a thing a person can see the outside of this building from, in a year, when this building is somebody else's business.

Nera's draw cord came up last, and she took it up herself, and it went back onto the valve board, and it is on the board, and it is marked, and the mark says it was used twice more after the thirty-seventh day, on a plan for a flooded room, on a period whose terms have their own page.

The pry bar stayed in the haunch of the third step, because it had been driven in that morning and the man who had driven it was not going to be available for a week, and pulling it out of a haunch with a haunch in it is a job, and the job was not going to be done on that day by anybody.

The jack stayed under the shore's foot, wedged, packed, and holding, with its handle in a notch in the common landing's floor where Iven had cut it on the first afternoon so that nobody would kick it, and it is the crew's best tool and it is public-works equipment and it is the only mass in this building that is not somebody's wall, and it is a hundred feet from a flooded room with a length of timber on a shelf and a number chalked on it.

And the salvage was not taken, and Dalla Sen refused it in the same words twice, and the second refusal was to a different person.

The wedge-shaped piece of a dressed block in the hollow stayed where it was, because Renn Vale had said it would and nobody had asked her again.

The parapet slab stayed where it was, because Dalla Sen had said *removed for safety, not recovered, not claimed*, and had said it twice before this, and said it a third time on the day to Iven Tallo, who had asked whether a piece of it might be used to pack the lip, and who had put his hand to his own mouth afterwards like a man who has heard himself ask something.

"That slab is eleven feet long and a foot thick and it is a *stone*," Dalla said, "and the moment it carries a load in a Hearthhold work it is material in use under a guild salvage lien, and I have watched six people in this building learn a rule in nine weeks and I am not going to unlearn it at the end of the worst day we have had. Write it down. Write that it was asked for today, by name, and refused, and the reason, and that the person who asked will not remember asking in a month and will be glad in a year."

"Write that," Iven said. "And write that the man who asked is the man who built the shore."

And the working stopped, and the tools came up nine steps, and the six of them went to the kitchen line, and nobody in that boundary carried anything that belonged to anybody else.

At about the second hour Iven Tallo said the thing that has been in the ledger ever since, and he said it to nobody in particular, and it was not a consolation and it was not meant to be one.

"There needed to be a post under that lip."

Nobody stopped him.

"A second shore," he said, "foot on the common landing's floor, head under the near landing's floor, holding the lip up off the hole. That is the whole of it. That is a day's work and two lengths of timber, and it is what I should have said in the forty-fifth day when I said the first shore, and I did not say it because I was thinking about a line and a wall and I am a carpenter and I think about one thing at a time and I have spent nine weeks learning that this building will not."

"And the timber," said Tams.

"The timber is under six feet of water on a bottom shelf with a number chalked on it," Iven said. "Five foot nine. And there is nothing else. There has not been anything else since the twenty-eighth day. And the crew have four foot eleven and a bit of the three-foot-four in the anteroom, and the three of them is a scarf at the head of a shore, which is worth nothing to a shore, and Jo's basket has a mended handle and a flat file with a cord-bound grip."

"Then the route needs a man under the lip," said Dalla Sen, writing it down, "and there is no timber in this building for a post, and the timber for the post is in a flooded room under the inventory of an air engineer, and it is a length and not a bundle, and it is five foot nine, and the longest thing we brought up this side is three foot four. So the post wants nine foot and a bundle, and there is neither, and that is a problem for a month and for a room that has more than eleven people in it."

"Noted," Nera said, and then: "No. Do not write *noted*. Write it as what it is, because *noted* is what you write when you are going to do it and nobody in this building is going to do it this month and the page must not say that anybody is on notice."

Dalla wrote it again, and read it back, and this is what the ledger says:

*That the near landing's lip is one stone, carries the line-block at its base, and has no post under it. That the shore on the common landing took the line and did not move. That the lip moved about two inches with a man under it and four people behind him. That the material for a post under the lip is not in this building. Recorded on the day, in the clerk's hand, at the second hour, and read back to the room, and not softened and not deferred, and the word *noted* struck out in the clerk's own hand because the air engineer asked for it to be struck and the reason she gave was that a page which carries a warning in the language of a duty is a page that has already forgotten how to be frightened.*

*No stamp, no form, no name against a date.*
