# Chapter 67 — Over the Stair

The arithmetic was done in the anteroom on Friday morning by two people who did not agree about anything else, and it took forty minutes, and it produced a number that was a fifth of what Tams Quill had put on the board on Thursday night, and the difference between the two numbers was the whole of the week.

Mikka Hale did the pot. Tams Quill did the crossing.

"A pot is a pot and it is nine children three days, and it is a clinic, and it is a stove, and it is two people washing themselves in a trough, and that is what fourteen pails was on Wednesday and it is not fourteen pails on Friday because on three days in seven there are no nine children in this building coming through that corridor and there is no school to wash up after." She wrote the figures on her own board and turned it round. "**Twelve on the open days. Eight on the shut ones.** And I am not sorry about the four, and I want it said that I am not sorry about the four, because a day is a day and the four are gone and no arithmetic brings them back."

"Eight pails is two crossings," said Tams Quill, "and eight pails is two crossings on a day the school does not run, and that is legal and it is the first legal thing anybody has said about this stair since Tuesday." He had the board on his knee and the chalk in his left hand and the right one flat on the bench, and he did it slowly, and he read the terms out of his own memory in the flat voice he used for them, because he had been reading them out loud every day for a month and they were in him the way Oren's packing method was in him.

"Four at a time. Four minutes. Nobody on the stair in the six minutes after the lever comes off. No stair period on a day the school runs, or between the second bell and the fourth. **And the four treads behind the rail on the far side are not walked this month, which is the air engineer's second condition and the only one of the four that nobody in this room has ever thought was about safety.** The plinth reader's word is a warning and not a stop; the stop is the cloth, the cord, and any of the four people who keep a room. A bearer at the near lip for the whole of any crossing, named on the day's board, a person of this boundary.

"So: two crossings on a shut day, and they cannot be between the second bell and the fourth, and there is no school, so they can be at the first bell and after the fifth. **Two crossings. Three days. Six crossings a week.** And the record of crossings in a week on this stair is four, out of the ninth week, and I am not going to stand here and tell a room that six is a good number, because six is a number of times somebody stands on a lip with a knee on a piece of wood in the cold at five in the morning."

"That is four more than the record and it is half of what I wrote down last night," he said, "and I would like it recorded that the twenty-eight was my arithmetic and the six is the arithmetic, and the difference between them is a woman's mouth saying *I am not sorry about the four*."

---

Jo Verrick took the objection before anybody else could make it, and she made it against herself, which is the second time in three weeks and which the room had learned to be frightened of.

"Stop," she said. "There is a thing I said in this room in the ninth week and everybody in here has been quoting it at each other for a month, and it is a good rule and I am the one who made it, and I am going to say what it actually says and then I am going to apply it to myself, in front of you, in my own hand, because I have watched this room do everything else in public and I am not going to be the one person in it who only corrects her own mistakes in private.

"I said the stair is not for a cart, and I said every number in that route is a number about bodies, and that if you take the bodies out you have a more dangerous thing and not a bigger route. That is still true. That is not going anywhere. I have not withdrawn one syllable of it and I am not going to.

"And here is what I have been letting everybody get wrong, and I have been letting it for a month, and I would like it on the page. **A cart is not a person and pails are not a cart.** Every one of those six crossings is four people and a pail, and the pail is in a person's hands and the person is holding the line or holding the plinth or holding the cord, and a pail does not go through a doorway on its own. So the rule I gave does not forbid the water. The rule I gave forbids the water *being on the route instead of the people*, and it never did the other thing, and if I let a room believe it did for a month that is my fault and not the room's.

"So: **the water goes over the stair, and it goes as people, and every one of those six is a crossing of four people and not a load of eight pails.** And I want Dalla to read that back and I want it read back in the same breath as *it is not for a cart*, and I want both of them on one line, because in a year somebody is going to want to put a barrow on this stair and I want the line that stops him to be next to the line that stopped me."

---

Isel Ferrow took it at the top of nine steps at about half past four in the afternoon, and he was not the only person who volunteered, and Dalla Sen entered the fact that four people had offered before she entered any of the names, because the room had taught her that eleven weeks ago and she had never stopped.

"Four people offered and I am writing that down first, and the reason I am writing it first is that in a year somebody is going to read one name on this page and think it was the only person in this building who would have done it, and there were four, and one of them has not slept since Wednesday and one of them is a boy who cannot cross a two-foot-eight gap, and I am not going to have either of those things read out of this record later as the whole of who was willing."

"Boy," said Pell Roke, from the bench, without moving. "I am fourteen and I have a splinted ankle and I could not get down those three steps if somebody carried me, and I have said in this room that nobody is to build a plan on me, and I am not offended, I am just being accurate about the word."

"Corrected," said Dalla Sen, and struck the word, and read the correction back.

Renn Vale did the asking. She did it at the top of the three steps, on the common landing, in front of the woman and in front of the clerk, and she wrote the asking and the answer down in her own hand with the time on it, and the room did not remark on that either.

She asked her for the string, three days a week, twice on each of them, at the first bell and after the fifth bell, on the days the school does not run, and the woman from the wash rooms said yes, and then she said the rest of it, and it took about a minute.

"I will say yes to six crossings in a week. I will not say yes to twenty-eight, and I said so yesterday in front of all of you, and I am not taking yesterday back, I am adding to it." She had her hand on the rail and she was not looking at anybody. "On a shut day there is no school coming through that corridor, so the covers are not being lifted for the school. The covers are lifted at the second bell and the fifth and nobody else lifts them and that is Mikka's term and it is the only term in this building I have ever been sure of. Your first bell is before my second. Your after-the-fifth is after my fifth. So the six crossings fall in the two hours of a day that are not mine, and I have done that arithmetic myself, standing here, and I am not going to have it done for me by a man with a tally board, and I would like it read out in the room in my own words so that nobody can say later that a woman was talked into a string.

"And I am still not sorry about yesterday. Yesterday I was right. Today I have found a way round it that costs me nothing I had. That is not being talked out of anything. That is a woman who has been here a month."

"Read it," said Dalla Sen, and read it, twice, and the second time the woman from the wash rooms made her start at the beginning.

And Isel Ferrow took the near lip, six times in a week, and he said what he was being given before he said yes, which is a habit Bel Ordry put into this building and which has now spread.

"I am the bearer at the near lip," he said, "which means I stand on that stone with my own weight on it for the whole of a crossing and I do not leave it while there is anybody on the stone, and I have done that twice and I will do it six crossings in a week in the cold at five in the morning for as long as this arrangement stands, and I would like it on the day's board every morning and I would like the word *asked* next to my name, because I am the first reader of a stair in this Hearthhold and I would like to be the first person in it who can say that nobody put me there by being short of hands."

---

Bel Ordry took the threshold bell at half past four in the afternoon as well, and he did not volunteer for it, and everybody noticed, and Dalla Sen entered that too.

He stood in the middle of the anteroom with his hands open and said: "I want to say why I have not offered, because everybody has noticed and I would rather say it than have it written down that I did not notice.

"I have not offered because the bell is the easy one. A bell is a rope and you pull it and then you go and stand at the top of the steps. I held a bar against a wall for four mornings in the cold and came off it with no answer, and I have not been the same since, and I would rather hold the thing that cannot go wrong than the thing that can, and everybody in this room can see that and nobody has to say it.

"Except that the bell is the thing that can go wrong, because a bell is the only part of that stair that has to be *heard*, and it is the fifth of the morning in the cold, and a rope two floors down with a person on the top of nine steps, and I am the only man in this building who has never held anything and I would like to find out what a thing I cannot break feels like at five in the morning." He put his hands down. "Three days, six crossings, and I am not going to fix my mother's door this month, and that is the cost, and I said on Wednesday that the cost was a day, and it turns out the cost is a month, and I have found out about that on a Friday and I would like the month in the book and not the day."

"Wrote it," said Dalla Sen, "in your words, and I am adding, in my own hand, that this is the second time in three days that a person in this building has cost themselves a month of something in order to do a day's work, and that both of them said it out loud before they did it, which is a thing this room has only learned in the last eleven days, and which I am putting in the ledger in my own hand because in a year it is the only part of this week anybody will want to keep."

---

The first crossing went at the first bell on the Saturday and it went badly, and nobody died, and the record says the system worked and the cold did not.

Isel Ferrow was on the lip from five. The plinth was read by Ruk Sella, who had been asked and had repeated the terms back. The string was the woman from the wash rooms. The bell was Bel Ordry at the threshold. Jo Verrick was on the lever, and Tams Quill stood at the frame with nothing in his hands, because a man at a frame with a bar in his hand does not hear a hiss, and because in the cold there is no hiss to hear and he was there anyway.

They went out with the six pails in the pump's window and they were fifty-eight seconds off the near landing, which is the best figure in the record for a party with a load in its hands and it is worse than the thirty-eight, and Iven Tallo said out loud in the anteroom afterwards that he would rather have the fifty-eight and he would rather nobody built anything on it.

The turn came, and the cold had taken the plinth.

Ruk Sella felt it late. She said afterwards, and Dalla Sen entered it in her own words and made her say it twice, that she had her hand on the plinth and the plinth was cold and she had been told the cold was a condition and not a reading, and that a cold thing under a cold hand does not move the way a warm thing under a warm hand moves, and that she heard it and thought she had heard it, and there was a half-second in the middle of that where she believed it.

Pell Roke, on the bench at the top of the nine steps with his ear against the stone and his crutch across his knees, called it eight seconds early, in a voice that did not carry and did not need to, and said afterward that he had called it eight seconds early and that eight seconds is eight seconds and he was not going to be told he had been late or early by anybody who was not standing where he was sitting.

The woman on the string had the word out, and Isel Ferrow did not leave the lip, because a bearer at the near lip is there for the whole of a crossing and he knew it and stayed where he was, and the six pails went down onto the fixed first landing and the party was up the three steps in a second and a half, and there was no knee on the lip, and there was nobody under the stone.

What Dalla Sen wrote down afterwards, in the clerk's hand, and read out twice, was this: three of the four instruments on that stair had failed in the cold. The plinth had gone quiet under a cold hand. The check had gone to a single note. The bar against the throat's cheek had gone to a sound that could be a crack. And the one that had not failed was a person with her hand on a rope and a boot on a stone and a bad hand in a sling who had said yes at the top of three steps.

---

On the Sunday Mikka Hale's number was still sixteen, and the pot was on, and the water was in the kitchen, and Jo Verrick stood at the frame at the second bell with a cloth in her hand and said the last thing anybody said about the whole of it, and she said it to the room and not to the frame.

"Three days a week, two crossings, eight pails, four people at a time, a woman on a string, a man on a bell at five in the morning, and Isel Ferrow on that lip." She wiped the frame down. "That is how this Hearthhold is being kept in the cold, and there is not one hour of it in a guild file, and there is no name in it anywhere but ours.

"And on Monday morning I am going to open that housing. Because I have said that the seat goes before the cold and the cold is here, and I have no instrument, and I have no wood, and I would rather take the seat out of a housing and see what is under it with my own eyes on a Monday than find out in February that it was gone and that I did not look. **And I want it understood by every person in this room that I am going to open it with a chisel I am borrowing and my own hands and a half a day, and that if the water comes up while it is open then the corridor shuts and the school shuts and the number goes down, and that is the price of the look.**"

"And if there is nothing to look at," said Marek Venn, "then you have shut a corridor and a school and three days of a pot for a question."

"Then I will have looked," said Jo Verrick, "and I will be a woman who looked, and this building will be full of women who did not, and that is a thing I can live with and you, in about nine months, will not be able to."
