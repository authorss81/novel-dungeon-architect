# Chapter 70 — Whose Wood

Nera Oduya wrote the hardwood inventory in the anteroom on the last day of the twelfth week, and she wrote it in her own hand on the back of a card because her board was full, and she read it out line by line, and nine people took it apart, and it took an hour and forty minutes, and every line of it was a thing somebody did not have.

"A hardwood inventory," she said. "That is what it is. Every piece of wood over about four inches in this Hearthhold, with an owner, a length, a condition, and what it is doing, and a column at the end that says whether it is available, and I am telling you now that there is not one line on it with a *yes* on it, and I am not going to write one to make the page look better."

She put the card on the bench, which is the way a form is put on a bench in this building, and Jo Verrick came and read it, and then Renn Vale, and then Ilsa Norr, and then Bel Ordry, and it was taken apart in that order because that is the order they stand in.

---

"**Line one.** A scarfed head of three pieces, on the common landing, under the head of a shore. Boundary. Sound. In a shore. **No.**"

"That is mine," said Renn Vale, "in the sense that the shore is mine and the head is in it and I could not get the head out of the shore without taking the shore out. Correct. Move on."

"**Line two.** One length, five foot nine, public-works, with an inventory number chalked on the end, under that head. Air engineer's inventory line, closed. In a shore. **No.**"

"That is mine as well, and the number is on the end of it under a face, and I asked for the number to stay there, and it has stayed there through a bearing and a packing and a fortnight of frost." Nera looked at the card and then at the room. "It is the only line on this page that anybody asked for."

"**Line three.** A two foot ten and a handful, in a basket with a mended handle, in the anteroom. **Jo Verrick's basket. It was hers before any of us came up a shaft.** Packing, out of a shelf of the anteroom's workbench surface cut into sizes in the tenth week. Not sound, not seasoned, eleven weeks in a warm room. **No.**"

"That is the only piece of free timber in this building and it is mine, and I have been sitting on it for a fortnight looking at it every morning, and I am not going to cut a seat out of my own packing, and I would like line three to say *not sound* in capitals."

"It says not sound," Nera said, "in my hand, and I have underlined it, and I am not going to shout at a woman about a piece of wood in her own basket."

---

"**Line four.** Five public-works hardwood wedges, split between two shores on one landing. **No.** And **line five:** seven sound wedges and two dead sticks in this Hearthhold, three at the school-door head, three at the waist, one in a basket, and not one of them spare, and the head at the school door is one short of what it wants and has been refused its fourth by Renn Vale in her own words and the refusal is not withdrawn. **No, six times.**"

"Six," said Renn Vale, "and the seventh is a dead stick and a dead stick is not a no, it is a *no* with a reason, and I would like it entered as a dead stick, because in four years somebody is going to try to use one and I want the page to stop them."

"Entered as a dead stick," Nera said, and wrote it, and read the line out again with the new word in it.

"**Line six.** The anteroom's workbench surface. **It is not a line of wood, it is the absence of one.** Cut up in the seventh week for a reserve that was opened under a stone lintel that was coming down, and what is left of the surface is a stub and a bracket, and the stub is a no and the bracket is a no and the reason in both is *it is the only table in this building that a person can spread a plan out on.* **No.**"

"Put the bracket's length in," said Iven Tallo, from the wall, "because the bracket is nine inches and I have watched three people try to use it this week as a straight edge and I would rather they read it off a page than find out on a bearing face."

"Put in the length," Nera said, and put it in, and read it out.

---

"**Line seven.** The school store, in a locked box under Ilsa Norr's key. Two lengths of beech, sound, about five foot. A former door lintel. **Ilsa Norr's, and held by her, and in use — the props under the school store's dressed-stone shelf, which has dropped twice, and which carries nine register boxes, one of which has a corner off. No, and the reason is in her words and I am not summarising it.**"

"Read my words," said Ilsa Norr, and Nera read them, and they were exactly what she had said in the corridor a fortnight ago, and nobody in the room looked away while it was read, which was a thing that had not been true a fortnight ago.

"Correct as written," said Ilsa. "And I would like one word changed in the *owner* column."

"What word."

"It says *Ilsa Norr's*. It is in the school store, in a locked box, and the key has been in my apron since before the collapse, and there is a difference, and the difference is that a thing in a locked box in a school store belongs to a school and a thing in my apron belongs to a woman, and in about four years a clerk is going to ask this boundary who owns a beech lintel and I would like the answer to be a room and not a person."

Nera Oduya looked at the card for a moment, and then she drew a line through the word, and wrote *the school, held by Ilsa Norr*, and read it out twice.

"**Line eight.** Bel Ordry's two rooms above the clinic return. One piece of oak, sound, about five foot, a single board, a floor. **Bel Ordry's. Eleven inches of it marked out in pencil on the underside last week and not cut. Given. Not available this month, and the reason is the draught, and the cost to the man is a month of his own door.**"

"He asked for that line to be the one that is read out loud," Bel Ordry said, "and I would like to say the reason out loud as well, in my own words, because the reason in the column is the reason for the *no* and the reason I gave was a different one, and both of them are true and only one of them is the wood.

"The wood is the best sound thing in this Hearthhold and I would like it cut today. The reason is that a man who has given something to a place he lives in is a man who can be asked what else he has, and I have been in this building eleven weeks holding nothing and it has been the best eleven weeks of my life, and I am not going to trade that for a floor." He looked at the room. "And I am not going to be thanked for it either, and line eight says *given* and that is the whole of what I want out of that page, and if anybody thanks me they can do it in the corridor and not in a room with a clerk in it."

"Noted, in my own hand," said Dalla Sen, "that the giver has asked for the line to be read aloud and has asked not to be thanked for it, and that the reason in the *no* column and the reason the man gave are two different sentences and both of them are entered."

---

"**Line nine.** Under this floor. Two lengths of elm, about six foot, on two bearers, on the stone. Sound, seasoned, thirty years in standing water. **The pump's own bed. The boundary's. No, and the reason is not hardness: the housing bolts through the frame into the bed, and the bed takes the pump and the water on top of it, and take eleven inches out of it and the housing settles half an inch on one side the first time the pump comes on hard, and a seat in a crooked housing does not announce itself.**"

"That is mine and I said it in the anteroom and I am not going to say it again in a room," said Jo Verrick, "and I would like it noted that it is the best-conditioned wood in this Hearthhold and it is the pump."

"**Line ten.** The wash rooms."

The room changed, the way it changed when somebody said the word.

"There is a rack in that room," Nera said, "and on the rack there is a piece of wood that has been in a damp room for thirty years and it is the only piece of wood in this Hearthhold that a delivery seat could sit in and stay put. It is not this boundary's. It is not anybody's, so far as anybody in this room knows. **The floor of that room is refused and the refusal is older than any of us and it is not going to be lifted this month by a clerk with a card.**

"**No. The reason is not hardness and it is not the floor. The reason is that the only person who can answer for the room has not been asked, and Renn Vale has said in this room that she will go to her door and stand on her step while that woman says whatever she is going to say, including no, and she has not done it, and this page is not going to be the thing that makes her do it.**"

Nobody in the anteroom said anything for a moment.

"Put *no* and put the reason in my words as I said it," said Renn Vale, "and put the day on it when I go, and I am going to write that day down myself and not be given one by anybody, because the last time somebody in this room gave me a date for a thing I had refused to do, I did it in a hurry and it cost a person a morning."

"**Line eleven.** The middle gate. About four hundred paces of seasoned hardwood in a public-works store on a guild register, with a saw, and a water-lock keeper with a name. **Four days on a road, up the middle passage, through the school door, along a corridor with two places in it that are two foot eight wide, one of which is standing over an eleven-foot shaft on ground made of washed-out fill. No — and the reason is not distance. The reason is that the middle's water-lock is dry and the timber in that store is the timber that would put it right, and the middle knows that, and it is the same shortage as line ten and line nine and it is the third boundary's.**"

"**Line twelve.** The fallen parapet slab, eleven feet, a foot thick. Boundary's clerk has refused it three times and refused it once to a named man with his name under the refusal. Not wood. A sill and not a seat. Guild salvage lien, guild-frozen, and the carpenter who asked for it twice is a man whose bond is suspended. **No.**"

"Correct," said Dalla Sen, "and I would like the clerk's own three refusals read out, because a person in a year should know that a woman refused a stone three times and a fourth time to a man by name, and that is not a clerk being hard, that is a clerk doing a job, and this room has spent a fortnight being grateful to her and it took her three refusals to earn it."

"They are read out," Nera said, and read them out, and they were exactly what Dalla Sen had said they were.

Then she got to the last line and stopped for a moment with the card flat on the bench, which is the thing she does when she is about to say a thing she has decided to say.

"**Line thirteen. What the seat wants.** Eleven inches across the face, two and a quarter deep, set, proud at the top and hollow at the bottom, and a hand that has been a hand for a season, and somebody in this building to stand with the water line chalked on the frame.

"There is no line on this page for a person who has one. I have looked. I have written this card twice and there is a place for a *piece of wood* and there is a place for a *name* and there is nothing at all for a woman at a frame, and I could put a name in the name column and it would be the wrong column, and I would rather have a short page than a wrong one.

"**Not held. Available: no. And the reason is that this boundary cannot get a plank, and the reason it cannot get a plank is a corridor, and the reason there is a corridor is a building, and the reason the building is this shape is a question nobody in this room has answered since the tenth week.**"

---

Dalla Sen read the work out afterward, and it is not a long entry, and it took nine minutes to read because of the objection.

"**Named work: a delivery seat in hardwood, cut, dressed and set, in the pump under the anteroom floor, by Jo Verrick, on a day to be named, received by the boundary. Named by the keeper on the second day of the week. Struck in part by the keeper at the naming, in his own words, and the struck part read out with the rest.** Finished: no. Reason: the material does not exist. Price as named, in the worker's own hand with the time on it, and the price is a day of a shutter route, a day and a half of labour, a day of a corridor, a day of a school, and a day of a pot."

"Read the second-to-last word again," said Jo Verrick.

"**Finished: no. Reason: the material does not exist.**"

"That is not mine," Jo said. "That is yours, Dalla, and it is close and it is not right. It is not that the material does not exist. **The material exists and every piece of it is doing something, and one piece of it is a woman who will not be thanked for it.** I want that word changed and I want it changed in your hand and not mine, and I want it read out again, because in a year this is going to be the entry that a person reads when they want to know whether anybody in this building ever asked for wood, and the answer is that eleven people did, and every one of them was told no for a better reason than hardness, and that is the most careful week of work I have been part of in thirty-one years."

"It is changed," said Dalla Sen, "in my own hand, and the entry now reads: *the material exists and every piece of it is in use, and one piece of it has been offered and refused by its owner for a reason of his own, and the seat is not made.* Read out again, and it is the ninth time this entry has been read and it is the one I would like left in the book longest."

---

On the common landing that afternoon, Jo Verrick put a string on the lip and a chalk and a bent flat iron, and did the thing she has done every morning for a month, and it took four minutes, and she did not like the answer and gave it as a range.

"**A finger and a half to a finger and three-quarters.** Fourteen mornings ago on this stone it was a finger and three-quarters to two inches. After the shore it was about a finger and a half. It is now a finger and a half to a finger and three-quarters, and I am giving you a range and not a figure because Ilsa Norr made me give a range in the tenth week and it is the best thing anybody has done to my arithmetic in eleven weeks, and because in this cold a quarter of an inch is inside the noise.

"So: the shore took a quarter of an inch off the worst day of my life and gave a bit of it back in a fortnight of frost, and **that is not a fix, and the man is still under the lip, and it will be Jo Verrick if the slab turns while I am on the lever, and there are six crossings a week now and there were four in a fortnight and a half.**"

"And I would like that read out at the fifth bell," said Renn Vale, from the upper landing, "and I would like it read out with the seventh term under it, and I would like the two read out together, because the seventh term is mine and it is about a person and the number is Jo's and it is about a stone, and I would like a person in this building to have to look at both of them on the same line."

"Both," said Dalla Sen, "in Renn Vale's hand and in Jo Verrick's hand, and the clerk's between them, and read out at the fifth bell, and that is the end of the twelfth week."

---

At the top of nine steps, on the bench where a wrapped plate had lain and a boy sat with a splinted ankle and his ear against a stone, Pell Roke heard the wheel and called the interval eight seconds early in a voice that did not carry and did not need to, and then he said, to nobody, that it was eight seconds and not nine, and that he had checked, and that a count in the cold was a photograph of a cold morning and was not to be used for anything else.

And at the second bell the next morning a man with a cold bar put it against the north-east cheek of a stair's throat, about eleven feet down, and it ticked, three times, with a slide on the third, and it ticked in two other places on the same wall where he knew the stone, and he wrote down the shape of it and the cold and the three places and did not write a word about what it was, and he put the bar back and went to his own work with his hands white to the second knuckle.

And the check under the anteroom floor hissed on its one note, and the water in the dip was four inches from a chalk line that had not moved, and a pot of fifteen days stood in a kitchen, and four households in a second gate went on carrying water up four sets of steps, and a boundary twelve lines long sat in a clerk's book with *no* written against every one of them and a thirteenth line under it that said what the seat wanted and had no owner to put against it, and a schoolteacher in a school store took a key out of her apron and looked at a locked box and said, out loud, to nobody, that the key turned next week and the five of them would not be in the room.

The spring conversation happened on the second day, at the fourth bell, in one hour, in the anteroom, and it is in the book. Sefu Aran and Nera Oduya, with the five of them and the clerk, and nobody else, and the terms named on the day and not before, and the leaving not mentioned once in it by anybody, which Dalla Sen entered in her own hand because she had been braced for it.

It ran long by about ten minutes and it produced one thing, and the one thing is not in the ledger.

"Nera Oduya said a name," the clerk said, when it was asked for, "in her own words, in the air engineer's own hand, and I read it out once as she asked me to and I am not going to say it again in a room that does not have a reason. The person does not know. The air engineer went and stood at their door on the third day, which is her term and not mine, and whatever happens at that door belongs to the two of them and not to this book, and I entered *a person was named* in the ledger on the day, in her hand, with the time on it, and that is the whole of what I have got."

And on the bench at the fifth bell, the third item on the review page still had no name against it: *somebody stands at the low end of the corridor and watches the water in the six minutes after the lever comes off, and there is nobody standing there, and the line is chalked on the frame and the frame is in the anteroom.* The air engineer had put it there on the Wednesday and had said that she would not name a person, and nobody had named a person, and it was going to sit on that page with a blank against it for as long as it had a blank, and Dalla Sen read the line out at the fifth bell every day without comment, which is the only way anybody has ever found to look at a thing like that.

Marek Venn stood at the end of the tally board with the chalk in his left hand and thirty-one years of forms in his head, and understood that the whole of what he had to bring to a boundary in the twelfth week of the year was one sentence, fifteen words long, that he had never once in his life said out loud in a room.

The brass rule was in the pocket of the coat on its hook by the door, where it had been for six weeks, and he had not put his hand on it once in a fortnight, and neither had anybody else, and a thing in a coat on a hook is the quietest object in that building and it costs nothing to keep not touching it and it is the only decision in that anteroom he actually wanted to get right.

He picked up a slate instead, and turned it over, and found on the back of it in his own left hand a *null* from the seventh week and a set of figures from the ninth and a line of Leena Orr's chalk marks he had copied off a step because she does not go down three steps, and he looked at the whole of it for a while.

Then he put the slate down, face up, blank side out, on the bench at the end, and left it there. He did not write on it, because he does not write first on any room that people use, and because the only thing he had to record that day belonged to a woman at a frame, and because a man who has seven nulls in a drawer and a slate with nothing on it is a man who has run out of readings and has not yet run out of questions.

Then he went and stood in the corridor outside the pump room and asked Jo Verrick, out loud, who was going to stand at the low end of the corridor in the six minutes after the lever came off, and watched a woman who had said no out loud four times consider not answering a question that was not hers to refuse, and say a name, and then not say it, and then say it.
