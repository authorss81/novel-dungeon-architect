# Chapter 81 — The Road

The satchel went up the middle passage at the second bell on the first morning of the sixteenth week, and four people stood at the top of the nine steps and watched a stranger carry it out of the building, which had never happened before in the whole of the time any of them had been here.

Hesk Ammery was fifty-eight and had carried guild paper up and down that passage for nineteen years, and he had a coat that had been mended at the shoulder by somebody who was not him, and he took the satchel off the keeper with both hands and set it on his own shoulder and adjusted it the way a man adjusts a thing he has carried a thousand times.

"Four days to the middle gate," he said. "And then forty days on from the top of it to the file, if the file wants it, and the file always wants it."

"You know what is in it," said Sefu Aran.

"I know what is on the front of it, because the keeper read it to me at the threshold and I asked him to read it to me twice, and the second time he read it slower and that told me something about it." Ammery shifted the satchel. "A sheet of paper. Folded in three. Nothing else in the satchel at all, and I want that said in the room before I go up this road, because in nineteen years I have carried a satchel with a book in it and a satchel with a warrant in it and a satchel with a body in it, and I have never once carried a satchel with one sheet in it and nothing behind it."

"Nothing behind it," said Dalla Sen, from the top of the steps, with the ledger shut against her chest.

"Then I will say the thing I am going to say on the road and I would rather it was said here." Ammery took his hands off the strap and let them hang. "**I am not paid to carry a no.** I am paid to carry paper between a counter and a file, and that is the whole of my trade, and a *no* is not paper, and if I carry this up four days and put it down at a counter, then somebody at that counter is going to ask me what it means, and I am not going to stand there and be the man who invented an answer to it. So I will say what it says and I will say it exactly, and if I cannot remember it I will turn round and walk four days back down here and get it again."

Renn Vale, on the landing above, said, "That is the correct answer and it is the first correct answer anybody has given about a document in this building."

"It is not correct," said Ammery. "It is ordinary. Correct is for you people."

---

Then Sefu Aran gave him the terms, out loud, at the top of the nine steps, with the door open behind him and the pump going under the floor.

"You will not explain it. You will not argue it and you will not answer a question about it, because the moment a courier starts explaining, the notice becomes the courier's and the courier is a man with a satchel and not a boundary. If anybody at that gate asks you what it means, you say that it says what it says. If anybody asks you what it *costs*, you tell them the truth, and the truth is that it cost this Hearthhold four hundred paces of seasoned hardwood and a piece of a lintel, and that we had none of either before, and that is the whole of the price and there is no discount on it."

"And if they ask me what a Hearthhold is," said Ammery.

"Then you will have to say that too," said the keeper, "and I would rather you said it wrong than that you said nothing."

Ammery wrote that down. He took a stub of pencil out of his coat and wrote it on the back of his own hand, standing on the top step, in the cold, in nineteen-year-old shorthand that Marek Venn could not read and did not ask to.

"You are writing it on your hand," said Tams Quill, from the bench, with his right hand open in his lap.

"I have carried paper for nineteen years and I have never once lost a page," said Ammery, "and I have lost count of the number of hands I have written on. It goes up the road and the gate is four days away and if I cannot remember it at the counter then I have failed at a thing I could have done, and I do not intend to fail at a thing I could have done." He put the pencil away. "Right. I will go before the light goes off the top of the passage."

---

He went up the middle passage and nobody went with him, and that was Sefu Aran's decision and not Marek Venn's, and it was the first decision in fifteen weeks that Marek Venn would have argued with if anybody had let him.

It would have been four days of a wet road, and he could not have carried the satchel in his right hand and would have had to be told to change hands every two hundred yards, and at the service bend at the top, where the passage comes down to a shoulder a foot wide over a shaft nobody has ever sounded, he would have had to stop and be helped, and being helped on that bend is a two-person job and the second person would be a man of this boundary, and a man of this boundary on that road turns a courier into a party.

So Marek Venn stood at the top of the nine steps with his left hand in his coat and watched a fifty-eight-year-old man with a mended shoulder walk away from him up a road he would never see the end of, and understood that the first act of foreign policy this Hearthhold had ever performed had been carried out by a person whose name would be in no book in this building, and that the only part of it that was his had been agreeing with a sentence that somebody else had written.

*That is the whole of what I am good for now*, he thought, and the pump went round, and the check hissed on its one note, and he did not say any of it, and he went down the nine steps and sat on the bench with the slate in his left hand and put it down blank side up and left it there, which was the sixth time in three weeks and he did not know he was counting.

---

By the fourth bell the anteroom had worked out what it now was, and it took about nine minutes and eleven people, and the ninth thing said was the one that mattered.

"It is a boundary that has issued a notice," said Bel Ordry, from the side, "and has never issued one before, and the difference is that the notice is the first thing about this Hearthhold that is aimed at a person who is not in the room."

"It is aimed at a gate," said Jo Verrick.

"It arrived by a gate," Bel said. "The difference between the two is the difference between a shot and a rumour, and the shot is the one you can look at afterwards."

Renn Vale came down three steps, which she does, and stood in the middle of the floor of the anteroom with her slate against her hip, and said the thing that turned the morning over, and she said it flat, and she said it before anybody had argued for it.

"**We did not ask.** Read it out, Dalla. Read the whole of it, and then read the time it was written and the hour it went, and then somebody is going to tell this room whether we asked a room first."

Dalla Sen read it. She had read it out three times and she read it out a fourth, in the cold, at the fourth bell, in the anteroom, to eleven people, and then she read the time on it and the hour it had gone out, which was the second bell, and then she said:

"No. It was named in a room on the Friday of the fifteenth week by the keeper and it was read out by the keeper and by the clerk and by the man who carried it up the road, and it was read out three times, and nobody asked whether this boundary should be the kind of thing that issues notices, and I am the clerk and I did not ask, and I am putting that in the book in my own hand with the time on it before anybody else puts it in theirs."

"And I am not going to take it back," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel, "because it went up the road in the morning and it is a fact now and a fact on a road is worth more than a good argument in a room. **And I would like it recorded that we are now a thing that issues notices, and that it took us fifteen weeks, and that it happened in a morning because a twenty-two-year-old asked a question out loud that none of us had ever asked, and not because any of us decided to.**"

"And asking now would not bring it back," said Marek Venn.

"No," said Renn Vale. "**Asking now would not bring it back and would also not have stopped it, and I want the clerk to write down the second half of that, because in a month somebody in this room is going to want to know whether we were right, and the answer is that we were asked a question we could not have refused, and the reason is a dry water-lock four days up a road, and neither of those is a good enough reason and both of them are true.**"

---

Four days up that road, the far end of the notice got into the anteroom about half past the fourth bell, and it got in through Wen Pask, who had not been up the road and had not seen anybody and had been in this building for twelve days with a leg and a corner of a school store and no work.

"Corin Bexley," he said, and Dalla Sen wrote it down, because it had been on a page in a file for five weeks and it had not once been on this book's. "Water-lock keeper. Second gate of the middle gate. He has been on that lock since before the collapse and he is on it now, and I did not meet him and I am not going to pretend I did."

"Tell the room what he is," said Tams Quill.

"He is the man who put a hold on four hundred paces of seasoned hardwood in a public-works store because his lock has been dry since before I was born, and the timber is not his, it is held against the work, and every month it sits in that store the whole of that gate knows somebody is coming for it and plans around it." Wen Pask put his hand flat on the frame of the door he was leaning on, and it was the leg that made him put it there. "And a woman called **Nell Ferrin** stands behind the counter where the timber is counted, and I have been in that gate four days and I have talked to her more than I have talked to anybody since I came down a shaft, and she told me the rule about the pails twice without being asked to, and she is the one who said the sentence about this Hearthhold three times, and the third time she said it slower."

"Slower," said Bel Ordry.

"Like she was reading it out of a book and wanted to be sure the words were the ones in the book."

Nobody said anything for a moment, and the covers were lifted somewhere above them, and the pump came on, twenty-six strokes, and the check hissed thin, and a bar somewhere past the valve ticked once and stopped.

"Say the rest of it," said Dalla Sen. "Tams, say the rest of it, because I have been the clerk in this building for fifteen weeks and I have just found out that the reason a woman at a counter three times said the same eight words is that a man four days away has been holding a piece of wood against a dry lock, and I would like that in the book, in her words, and I would like somebody to tell me whether I have a right to write down what a woman at a counter said to a man at a table."

"You have a right to write down what a man in this building heard a woman at a counter say to him," said Renn Vale, "and that is a different sentence and it is the correct one, and it is the clerk's to say, and I would like it entered in that form and I would like the words *at a counter* kept in it, because that is where she was standing and this boundary has spent three weeks learning that where a person was standing is not a small thing."

---

At the fifth bell Dalla Sen read the arithmetic of the pot, which was **thirteen days**, and the lip's range, which was a finger and a half to a finger and three-quarters and worse on a cold morning, and Renn Vale's seventh term, and the ninth line, and the blank on the review page with the two sentences on it instead of a name, and the kitchen's book, and the wash rooms with *kept, and nobody's work* in them, and Wen Pask's forty pails in the column with no heading, and a line that was new and that she read twice, because it had been written in the morning by a man with a mended shoulder and copied into a clerk's hand and a count's hand and a slate.

*Carried up the middle passage at the second bell, on the first day of the sixteenth week, by a courier of the road and not by a man of this boundary: one sheet, folded in three, and nothing else in the satchel. The holder of it: Hesk Ammery, of no mark. The words on the other side of his hand are his and not ours and are not in this book, because they are his, and this boundary does not keep other people's hands.*

"Read it again," said Bel Ordry, and Dalla Sen read it again.

Then the room sat with the two facts together, which were the only two facts anybody in it had about the notice, and they were that it had gone out and that nobody in this building could say when it would arrive or what would happen when it did, and the second of those was not an oversight, and the man who had sent it had said so out loud at the wheel, and it was going to sit there in the room for the rest of the month like a chair with nothing in it.

Four hundred paces of seasoned hardwood were still in a store four days up a road.

This Hearthhold had no sound timber over six feet, no lath and no bronze, and five places where wood was held, and it had now in writing told a second boundary to stop waiting on it, and behind that wood there was a dry lock and four households carrying water up four sets of steps, and a woman at a counter who had said the same eight words three times and slower the third.

Nobody in the anteroom had ever watched a road leave. Everybody in the anteroom had read about roads.

The pump came on again, and the check hissed its one thin note, and the covers were lifted at the fifth bell by a woman in a room nobody had walked into, and the stair was corded and shut and stayed shut, and on the shelf above the bench there was a knotted bundle of work cloth with a thing wrapped inside it that had not been identified and had not been opened, and in the pocket of the coat on the hook by the door there was a brass survey rule that had said nothing for seven weeks, and a person was still under that lip.
