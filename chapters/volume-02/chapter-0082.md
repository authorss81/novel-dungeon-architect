# Chapter 82 — The Third Column

Nothing in the kitchen argued on Tuesday morning until the second bell, and what it argued about was a table, because Mikka Hale had cleared a table.

That is a thing you do not do in a kitchen of nine people and one litter. The trestle under the pump frame holds two pots, a board, a cloth, a crock of rendered fat, and the kind of arithmetic that only exists in the head of a woman who has been doing it since the seventh week. She cleared it, and she wiped it, and she set the book of favours on the left end of it and nothing else on the right end, and then she stood at the frame with her hands in her apron and waited for nine people to come and stand about in a room that was rated for ten including one litter, which was two hours, which was now.

"Right," she said. "Say it, then, and let me say the shape of it first, because I have had it since Sunday night and I have been carrying it about the kitchen like a pail I cannot put down.

"**I have been running this Hearthhold on a page I did not mean to write.** Six lines and three days and a third column with the word *water* in it forty-one times and a fourth line in my own hand that says *I have put a rota in a book and I did not mean to and the word when means boots.* And there is a man's board in the anteroom with a column on it that has nothing over the top of it. And there are five women in this building who unrolled a sheet on a dowel in a locked store on the first day of the thirteenth week and it was three columns and nine lines and not one name on it anywhere in nine hundred years.

"And not one of the three of us has ever been asked the question. So somebody is going to ask it, and I am asking it at the second bell on a cleared table, and I am asking it out loud so that the answer has to be in the room."

"What is the question," said Bel Ordry.

"Not *how* we keep it," said Mikka Hale. "We know how. Everybody in this room knows how, and the knowing is in a book and in a slate and in a clerk's head and in a cook's head and on a bell, and it has been getting done every day for fifteen weeks and nobody has ever written down what it is."

Dalla Sen said, "The boundary made a schedule once already."

"The boundary made a *work*," said Renn Vale, from the doorway, which was as far in as she came, "on Friday, and the work is the water, and it is a work and not a schedule, and there is a difference and the difference is that a work says who is on it. I have been thinking about that difference since Friday and I have not liked where it goes, and I am not going to let this table have it without me."

"Then say where it goes," said Sefu Aran, "because I asked you at the school door on Monday and you said you would think about it, and the keeper of a gate is allowed exactly one question before he starts answering his own."

---

"It goes like this," said Renn Vale. "A notice that says what a boundary will not do is half of a thing, and a boundary that has put half of a thing into the world has to be asked for the other half by somebody. And the somebody is four days up a road, and we do not know that yet, and it is going to arrive, and when it does it is going to be in the form of a question and the question is going to be *what do you do.*

"And to answer *what do you do* you have to write down what you do. And to write down what you do you have to say how many, and when, and what is done. And that is not a work. That is a **schedule.** And I have spent three weeks watching a cook's book turn into a rota in front of me, and a rota is a register with the words *person* left out, and the day this boundary writes a schedule is the day every person in it is in a column instead of a room."

She said the last of it with her slate against her hip and she did not sit down.

"So I will say the thing I have come to say and then I will go back up three steps and leave you to it. **I will not hold it.** Not because I do not think it should exist. Because I have been the keeper of this boundary's rota for four days in seven since the fourth day of the eleventh week and I can tell you exactly what happens to a woman in this building whose boots are in a book. It is not that somebody takes something from her. It is that she cannot say a room is hers any more, and there is a woman in this building who said those words in this room a week ago and had to take them back."

"It is me," said Ilsa Norr, from the school door, and she did not come further in either, and the two of them stood in the two doorways of a room that held nine people and neither of them would come into it.

"It is you," Renn agreed, "and it took four days, and you had been saying it for eleven weeks."

"Then let somebody else hold it," said Ilsa Norr. "Because I have thought about it for a week and I have got this. **You are all talking about who holds it, and nobody has said what is on it.** And the thing that is on it is the only thing I am frightened of, and it is not the stair and it is not the lip and it is not the wood. It is that the moment there is a schedule, the boundary has admitted in writing that there is a set of things that must be done whether anybody wants to do them or not, and from that day a person can be sent."

Nobody in the kitchen answered that, and the covers were being lifted somewhere above them, and the check under the floor hissed on its one note.

"Send who," said Bel Ordry.

"Anybody. A page does not know the difference between a chore and a punishment. That is the whole of what I have to say and I said it standing up because I am not saying it from a bed."

---

It was Nera Oduya, from the top of three steps, who turned it from a thing that was wrong into a thing that was going to be done, and she came down two steps and stopped, which is the farthest she has ever come down into an argument.

"Three columns," she said. "Not four, and I want it said in this room why not four, and then I want the three of them written on that table, and I want them in the shape the dowel has got them in, because that sheet has been in a locked room for nine hundred years with a count and a condition and a column of work on it and it has not one name on it, and that is the only piece of evidence anybody in this building has ever got, and we are going to use it and we are going to use it honestly and we are not going to be embarrassed about using it.

"**How many people.** A number. And *no names anywhere, not one, not an initial, not a surname* — and I am aware of how that sounds coming from the woman who has spent fifteen weeks on that landing insisting that a room with nobody's name on it is a room with a hole in it. But that hole is on *our* side. Ours is the page a stranger can pick up. So: a count, and a count is not a person, and an office can read a count all day long and it cannot do anything at all with it.

"**When.** A condition. *Wet. Dark. Cold. First. Last.* Longhand. And **not one number and not one day of a week anywhere in that column, ever** — and I have put my own objection to that in the book and the objection stands and I am not withdrawing it, because the wet days in this building are Renn Vale's boots and a condition that has stopped meaning the weather has started meaning a rota. So the condition is allowed and the person is not. That is the whole of the compromise and I do not like it and I am not going to lose the argument about it in a kitchen.

"**And what is done.**"

She stopped there, and looked at the cleared table, and the room waited.

"That is the third column," she said, "and that is where it stops, and I want the room to understand that it does not stop because we are frightened. It stops because in fifteen weeks this boundary has not written down one piece of work."

---

"She is right," said Dalla Sen, and she put her ledger on the table and did not open it. "And I have been the clerk of this building for fifteen weeks and I can tell you exactly what is in my book and it is *who.* Nine names, twenty-nine times, in their own writing, with the time on each one, and a keeper's calendar, and a cook's rota, and a man's estimate of what is left in a pump, and four mornings of a bar, and a boy's ten days and ten nights with no heading.

"**There is not one line in this book that says what was done.** Not one. I have kept a record of a boundary and I have kept a record of the people in it, and if you put the two together in a year you would know every person in this Hearthhold and you would not know one single thing about a single thing that got done."

"That is not your fault," said Tams Quill. "You asked the wrong question every day for fifteen weeks and I want to say that in your hearing, Dalla, because you are going to hear it from somebody else inside a month and it will be worse."

"Thank you," said Dalla Sen. "Write it down."

Jo Verrick came down the frame side with her hand still flat on the wood. She had been on the pump since the second bell and the pump was in the six minutes and she had not left the frame, and she said her piece from there without turning round.

"Third column, then. **The pump is run attended, two people, second bell to the fifth, stopped on a chalk line, and the check is one note and the two-note vocabulary is withdrawn.** Twenty-six strokes, six minutes off, nineteen, and the housing shut, and five cleanings paid, and the estimate is *one*, in my own hand, and I have not written zero and I am not going to. That is a line of work and you can have it and it is mine and I am giving it because I have been waiting fifteen weeks for somebody to ask me for a line of work that is not a number of cleanings."

"Give them the whole of it," said Sefu Aran.

"**You can have the whole of it and I want the estimate read out on the same line as the running of it, because the day somebody in a hearing puts a pump that works next to a pump that has one cleaning left in it, I want the answer to already be on the page.**"

And then Leena Orr, from the litter at the side of the room where it had been set down for the hour and not two, said the sentence that stopped the table, and she said it from flat on her back with her right arm in the sling, and nobody in the kitchen had expected it from that direction.

"**A list is not a location.** Ask it to a scale. It is not to a scale, and it is not to anything, and it is not going to be, and I want that said out loud in the room that starts it, so that nobody in four years uses it to find a room. Because that is what the other one does. The dowel is to a scale, and that is the only reason anybody in this building knows where the ninth line is, and the ninth line is four steps below a landing behind a rail, and there is no door to it on this level, and if you hand that sheet to anybody they will find a room on it that they cannot get to, and they will assume there is a way in, and there is not."

"Nobody is going down those four treads," said Renn Vale, from the doorway, and it was not an answer to Leena and it was not nothing.

"Everybody knows that," said Leena Orr. "I am not frightened of you going down them. I am frightened of a document."

---

They wrote the three headings on the back of a butcher's sheet from the school store, in Ilsa Norr's hand because the clerk's hand is the one a stranger could match, which was the first rule anybody proposed and the only rule the room did not argue about, and Mikka Hale's handwriting on nine sheets of the back of it is not going to look like a clerk's handwriting to anybody who has not seen it, and if it does then a stranger can go and find the clerk, and that is a thing a room gets to decide about itself.

Two columns were filled in the first hour. The counts went in as numbers without a struggle: the anteroom ten including one litter; the corridor four; the pump two; the school nine children and her; the wash rooms *not a count, a person* and Ilsa wrote the word *one* in the count and then struck it and wrote *—* and said out loud that a dash was not a number and that she was going to leave the dash there for as long as it took somebody to say why a room with a person in it has no count.

Conditions went in as words, and it took an hour and a half to argue three of them into the room, and every one of the three that got in cost somebody something.

*Wet*, for the ground at the waist, and the objection was Renn Vale's and it stood and the entry reads that the wet days in this Hearthhold are the days a particular person is on a particular seam, and the entry does not say her name, and Dalla Sen entered that as a decision and not as an omission, and Renn Vale said, "Read that sentence back to me twice, because in a year I want to be certain the only thing standing between this page and a rota is a dash."

*Cold*, for the bar in the throat, and Bel Ordry gave it, and gave the condition with it: **cold, and at the second bell, and in three places on the north-east cheek, and the count is one man and a bar and a lamp.** "And if the count is a man, then this page has got a man in it, and I have spent thirteen weeks refusing to be thanked for holding a floor, so somebody had better say the difference now."

"It is the difference between a count and a name," said Nera Oduya, "and it is the whole of what we have learned, and it is the difference between a rota and a register, and I will not have it described as *nearly* the same thing. One is a number. The other one is a person you can be sent to."

"Write it," said Bel Ordry. "Write *one man, a bar and a lamp* in the count and write my name nowhere, and if that is cheating then it is the best cheating in this building."

And then Mikka Hale stood at her cleared table with a pencil in her hand for about four minutes and did not write anything in the third column at all, and the room let her, because a room in this Hearthhold had learned in fifteen weeks to tell the difference between a woman thinking and a woman stalling.

"I am not going to fill that column in," she said, "and I am not going to fill it in out of modesty, and I want it written that I said that, because I know what I look like.

"I filled one in. In three days, and I counted it twice and it was forty-one both times and I have still not got round to believing the second count. And what came out the other end was a woman who keeps a schedule and did not know she was keeping one, and a word in my own book that means boots. **And the third column is the one that made me.** If I fill it in now, in a cold month, on a cleared table, in a room where everybody has been frightened of paper for a fortnight, then I will fill it in with the things I am doing this morning, and those are the things I am doing because I have been asked to, and not the things that need doing, and in a year somebody will read it and it will be a true record of a Tuesday in the sixteenth week and it will look like a schedule of a kitchen, and it will be the truth about a Tuesday, and that is the danger. A column you fill in before you know is a promise you have not made yet."

Sefu Aran stood at the kitchen door and looked at a table with two columns full and a third one empty and a fourth one that did not exist.

"Entered," said Dalla Sen, from the doorway, with her ledger shut. "**The boundary has begun a public maintenance schedule on the back of a butcher's sheet in the schoolteacher's hand, with three columns, of which two are filled and one is empty, and the empty one is empty on purpose, in the cook's own words, and no charter of anything has been agreed to by anybody, and no person has been asked to hold it, and the person it is for has not been named, and I am going to read that sentence at the fifth bell every day until somebody in this building stops me.**"

On the cleared table, in a teacher's hand, the third column of a public document that eleven people had started and nobody had finished was nine tenths of an inch of wiped paper.

Marek Venn was the man who would have written it, and he sat on a bench in an anteroom with a slate he could not hold flat and a right hand that would not close, and it was the first time in thirty-two years that being the right man for a piece of work had turned out to be the wrong thing to be, and he sat with it for about a minute and then went and stood under the pump, where the check hissed on its one note, and put his left hand flat on the frame of it the way a man does when he has decided to be a person who notices things.
