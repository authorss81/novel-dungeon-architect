# Chapter 131 — A Room With Nobody's Condition On It

**Shut for the corridor on the first day of the twenty-sixth week, a Monday, and the school did not run, and a room at the far end of a corridor that is not open stood open with nine hearth stones in it and a packed sill and nothing at all written about what it is allowed to hold.**

**A girl of nine was on the fourth step with her foot across the clerk's knee in a strip of crate board bound with a piece of an old apron, and she was not going to be carried, and nobody had asked her twice.**

Renn Vale put it in a room at the second bell, standing on the landing with the pump's check off and the pump running underneath her, in the flat voice she uses for a term.

"**I lifted a condition on Monday morning in this building and I said out loud that lifting it was worth exactly not being the one a doctor looks at, and I have had a week to think about what I own now, and what I own is a room with nothing on it."

"**There is a slate in this building that has a condition on it about clay under a sill, and a condition on it about a mark on a jamb, and a condition on it about a room that runs water, and there are ten rows on the plan and every one of the ten has a condition in the second column with no number and no day on it. And the room at the kitchen-line end of this corridor has a row and the second column of that row says what it says, and the condition it names was lifted, and the slate it lives on has nothing on it about that room this morning."

"**So that room is open. That is the good news and it is the whole of the good news. It is open with nobody's condition on it, and a room in this Hearthhold with nobody's condition on it is not a safe room. It is an unwatched one.**"

"**Say the difference out loud,**" said Bel Ordry, "**because this boundary has spent a fortnight learning that the difference is the whole of what it knows.**"

"**The difference is that a watched room is a room somebody has agreed to look at, and when it changes the person who agreed to look at it is the one who says so, and nobody can be told they did not know. I have been not looking at that room for nine days. It is not a criticism of anybody. It is what happens when a woman with a slate lifts a condition on a child's foot and then goes and stands on a landing for a week. It is what happens. And the next time that stone moves, which it will, there is nobody whose condition it moves against.**"

---

**What anybody in that room asked about first was a stove, and it was not a warm room anybody was asking about.**

Mikka Hale had the fire-frame off and her back to the anteroom, and she turned round when she was asked and said the thing she has said for seven weeks and has asked not to be praised for.

"**No. I will not light that hearth and I am not going to change my mind today and I do not want to be argued with about it by a child."

"**Nine children sit on those stones in the morning and the fire is in the corner on nine hearths of that kind and it is the only heat in that room, and the light in that room in the morning is the light coming off nine stones nine children are sitting on, and the bed of one of those stones has a hole under it the width of a hand and a half. I have said that. It is in the book in two hands."

"**And if I light that hearth the floor of that room is warmer, and a warm floor under nine children and a lime bed under a stone is the exact thing that was under the ninth stone when it went over a hand's width. I am not going to be the woman who put the fire back in there to be kind. Kind is what I would be calling it.**"

"**Then the room is cold,**" said Ilsa Norr, from the fourth step, "**and I want the record to say that I said that and that I said it before anything else, because the record is going to be written this week by somebody and I would like the cold in it and not left out of it."

"**The children are cold. They have been cold for seven weeks because I would not have a cook light a fire on a bed I do not trust, and I would rather they were cold than warm on a stone that is going. That is the trade I have made and I made it and I am not asking anybody to make it for me.**"

Marek Venn said the ordinary fact, because that is what he is for in a room, and he said it slowly, and he was on the anteroom step with his left hand open on the rail and his right one shut and useless, and he said it to the room and not to a book.

"**A room with a hole under one of nine stones, and no fire, and a sill packed in stone in clay that is still shrinking, and a packed sill that is the only thing in this Hearthhold holding that floor up — that is a room that is standing on a repair. I said the two available actions a fortnight ago in a room and there were two and there are two."

"**Lose the room, or leave it open and watch it. There is no third and there has not been a third since the first day of the twenty-fourth week, and I am not going to be the man who finds a third one in a bad month because we would all like one."

"**And the thing nobody has said out loud in this room yet, which I am going to say because it is an engineering fact and not an opinion: a room with nobody's condition on it is a room nobody is watching, and a room nobody is watching is a room that finds out about itself from a stranger. In this building a room found out about by a stranger is a row on a form with a space in it. In this building a room watched by a person with a slate is a room that a person can be asked about by another person and can say no."

"**That is the whole of the difference and it is worth a stove. It is worth more than a stove. I would like it written down that I said that, because I have spent a fortnight being the man who was right about a floor, and I am now in a room saying that the most valuable thing in this building is not a repair and not a fire and not a figure, and that it is a woman being allowed to say no to a room she looks after.**"

"**Then say the rest of it,**" said Nera Oduya, from three steps up, in the voice she uses when she has found a hole in a sentence and is going to make a person finish it. "**Say what happens to the word *watch* when the person who watches the room is a woman who cannot get to the room.**"

"**The low end of this corridor is shut four days in seven and the fill at the waist has moved three times and is not measured, by three people, on purpose. Renn Vale stands on a landing. That is not a criticism either. It is a distance.**"

"**It is a distance and it is also the reason the ninth stone went, and I have said that sentence in a corridor a week ago and a clerk has it in her hand, and I am not going to stop saying it because it has been said once.**"

---

**Then it was argued, in a room, with the pump running, whether the boundary had any business asking anybody anything, and the argument had a beginning and the beginning was a slate nobody's name was on.**

Bel Ordry put it at the fourth bell and he put it in the shape of a keeper with a page in his coat, which is his shape.

"**I am going to say the thing I have been carrying since Friday and I am going to say it badly and quickly, because this boundary has spent five weeks being careful and being careful was not enough, and I would like one ugly thing said in a room before the end of the day."

"**For five weeks this boundary has asked rooms to accept things. It asked a school to cross a fill. It asked a door to be shut. It asked a grate to be left off. It asked a woman to write a condition with no number and no day on it. And nobody in this building wrote down what any of those rooms agreed to, or what happens when it stops agreeing, or who is allowed to say it has stopped."

"**So the question is whether we write it down. And if we write it down, the question after that is who signs it.**"

"**No,**" said Renn Vale.

"**Say why, and not the small reason.**"

"**Because a signature is a name, and a name on a page about a room is a handle. This boundary has spent twenty-six weeks learning that and it has learned it by being cut, not by reasoning, and I am not going to be talked out of it by a man in a good mood on a Monday."

"**There is a form on a road with two lines in it for a name, and a field book four days up this passage with a title in it, and a form my own office put face up on a table that a woman from another office read in four seconds. If this boundary starts putting its people's names against its rooms, then in a year somebody comes up that passage with a piece of paper and says *the person who signs the condition on this room*, and this time there is a person to send for. There is not one now. That is not an accident. I have been protecting that on purpose for twenty-five weeks and I have never once said it out loud in a room until now.**"

"**So where does the agreement go,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**Into the person.**" She put her thumb flat on the rail. "**It goes into the woman who keeps the room, and it goes in as a condition, and the condition has no number and no day on it, and the reason it has no number and no day is the reason it cannot be satisfied on a schedule by a man with a book. And then the only way it comes off is a person, in a room, saying the thing has changed."

"**You have just asked me the question *for how long*. And the answer is: as long as the person who is able to say no is able to say it. That is not a duration. A duration is a thing you can run out of. A woman can be tired, and out, and on a bed, and in a room nine steps down that she cannot cross. That is what you are asking me to write down and I am telling you the shape of it and I am not going to be pressed for a number.**"

For about four seconds nobody said anything, and Marek Venn understood that the sentence he had been carrying since the second bell was not going to be the one that closed this, and that the woman on the landing had said in nine lines what he had been failing to say in a week.

"**Say it back,**" he said, "**and I will hold it, and I want the clerk to write it and I want it read against the original, because this building checks a condition by reading it out twice and I am not going to be the first man in a month to get a sentence of hers wrong.**"

---

**A physician was asked the question the whole of this week turns on, and he was asked it in a room with nine people in it, and he did not answer it, and everybody in the room had to sit with that.**

Mero Senn had been in this building eleven days and had not been in a room more than twice in that time, on the grounds he gave out loud on the first of them, which is that he is not here to be forgiven and would rather be useful than liked.

Bel Ordry asked it at the fifth bell, and he asked it in the plainest words anybody in the building had used in a fortnight.

"**A room's own page has a number in it that says how many the room is allowed to hold, and it is not a count, and every person in this room knows it is not a count, and there is a room at the far end of a shut corridor with nine children in it and a ten in the first column. Is a plan with a number like that in it acceptable?**"

Mero Senn was quiet for long enough that the pump got three strokes in.

"**I am not going to answer that with a feeling, and I want that entered before I say anything else, and I want the clerk to enter that a man was asked a question in a room and did not answer it and said out loud in advance that he was not going to."

"**A plan with a number in it is not acceptable or unacceptable. It is incomplete, and I have watched two buildings die of incomplete plans and I am not going to be the man who says the number is acceptable and I am not going to be the man who says it is not, because the number is not a person and you cannot hold a person with a figure."

"**A plan with a number in it and a name against it is a claim about a person. That is worse and it is worse in a way I can show you, and the show is that the moment a name is on that page, the number stops being a permission and becomes a quota, and a quota is a thing a person in a bad month can be held to, and the man who holds them to it will be right about the number.**"

"**What would you sign,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**A condition. Two people, one who can stop the room and one who cannot be moved by anybody who stops it. And I will not sign the page. I will sign a condition, and I will not sign a number, and I would like it entered that a physician came down a road to look at one foot and has now been asked to sign a plan and has refused, and that the refusal is not modesty and I am not going to be flattered out of it.**"

---

**At the sixth bell the clerk read the six things, and the day's number, and the count in the column with nothing over the top of it, and the room did not fill in after any of it.**

"**Three today and a hundred and nine since the keeper signed. The boundary is at two hundred and twenty-six and a quarter, and it adds by the day the work was done, and the two columns reconcile, and they have reconciled for twelve weeks. The crew are at fifty-six and a half and have not moved since Thursday of the twenty-fourth week."

"**The pot is ten. It has not gone up since the fifth day of the nineteenth week and I am not going to say that sentence in a new way this week, I said it every way I had last week and a clerk who keeps improving her own arithmetic about a cook's hardship is enjoying herself."

"**The count."

"**Wen Pask is sixty-six days and sixty-six nights into a count that is his own, and I said on Friday that I would write it and I am writing it, and nobody in this room said anything to me about it and I have entered that nobody said anything, because I have been clerk for twenty-six weeks and I have spent most of them in a room where somebody always says something.**"

Orla Kern was still on the fourth step when the bell went and she had not moved from where she was put, and Mero Senn came down and took her ankle in both hands without asking the clerk, and turned it a little under the light, and let go of it.

"**The bone is where I put it and it has not moved and I am not going to be pleased about that, because it is being held in place by a strip of crate board and a piece of an apron and a nine-year-old who is not putting weight on it because I asked her not to, and there is not one part of that sentence I am happy with and all three parts are true."

"**She will not put weight on it while that bone is where it is, and it is where it is until two people have said in a room that it has not moved, and it has moved a little every day this week. I am not going to put a figure on how long that is, and the reason is that the only figure in this building about a body is a number in a column, and I would like it kept on the page where it is and off my tongue.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**in a physician's words, and entered that he has refused to put a figure on it, and entered that the reason he gave is this building's own first column, and entered that I have not put those two things next to each other with a reason beside them, and entered that I noticed I wanted to and did not.**"

---

**At the fourth bell, before any of the rest of it, Jo Verrick had gone down the nine steps to the dip on this side of the waist with a lamp in her hand and had come back up them wet to the shoulder, and the room had to be told what she had in front of her, and what she had in front of her was three places and one water.**

She stood on the anteroom step and did not come in and put the lamp down where everybody in the room could see the wet on it.

"**I have been in three places in the last hour and I have put my hand in all three of them and I am going to say the thing I can say and I am not going to say the thing I cannot."

"**Under the bed joint in the school corner there is water. That room has been shut as a room since the fourth day of the twentieth week and it was opened as a room on the fifth day of the twenty-second, and it is dry as a bone and it has a bed joint in the floor with water standing in it, and I did not go in there to look, I stood in the doorway with the lamp because it is a room nobody is using and I am not going to be the person who starts using it."

"**And between the second and third hearth stones of the room at the kitchen-line end there is water again, and it came up on the first stroke this morning and it is four threads and it is warm."

"**And the channel at the low end of this corridor is full to a mark I scratched in the stone at the side of it, and the mark I am talking about is the new one. I scratched the old one in the sixteenth week and it is under the water. I have scratched a new one above it and I want the room to understand that a scratch in a stone is the only instrument this Hearthhold owns that says anything about how high water gets."

"**And here is what I can say. I cannot tell you they are the same joint and I have said that in this room a fortnight ago and I am not going to stop saying it. What I can say is that I have had my hand in all three of them and they are the same warmth, and I have been under this building for twenty-six weeks, and I have never once felt the same warmth in two places at once that were four rooms apart.**"

"**Say what you are not saying,**" said Renn Vale.

"**I am not saying it is one water. I am saying that a man who is honest about the difference is more useful to a room than a man who is not, and if I am wrong about the warmth then somebody in this room who is cleverer than me will find out and I would like to be told.**"

"**The three are one subject now,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and I am not going to let that be a sentence. Dalla, put them down with a reason beside them and nothing between them, and I want the reason to be the reason a woman with a rule gave, and I want the page to say that it is three findings and not one conclusion.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**three findings and one clerk's reason, and the reason is the reason a woman gave for not walking into a room, and I have read the three back twice.**"

**A room with a packed sill and nine hearth stones and a fire that would not be lit stood open at the far end of a shut corridor with nothing at all written on it, and a girl of nine was on the fourth step, and the anteroom above her was the room it had been for twenty-six weeks — ten including one litter, no more than two hours, a frame packed in stone and clay a finger and a bit out of true, and a second way out that was a word.**

**And nobody in this building had decided yet who decides, or for how long, and the slate was in Renn Vale's hand and it was empty.**
