# Chapter 23 — The Upper Gallery

Fourteen treads, and the eleventh one had a hollow note that Pell found before anyone asked him to look for anything.

He was at the top of the fixed flight with his crutch across his knees and his splinted ankle propped on the folded blanket from his cot, and he had stopped three steps from the gallery door because the sound came up through the stone rather than down from above.

"That one is not the same as the others," he said.

Marek came back down two treads and put his left hand on the riser. The wood was old joinwood gone the color of weak tea, and the eleventh tread had a split running from the stringer into the board that was hidden at the near edge and open at the far one. The stair had been repaired once, badly, with a length of timber let into the side of the stringer and pegged rather than bolted.

"Can you go up on the stringer?" Marek asked. "Not the tread. Along the wall side, one tread at a time, with Oren's hand on the rail and nobody below you."

Pell considered it seriously, which was the thing nobody had taught him to do and everybody had to learn from him.

"Yes," he said. "Not the other side. And not if a board moves while I am standing on it."

"Then that is the survey. You do not cross the deck."

The gallery opened in front of them, and it was the biggest dry space Marek had seen since the collapse.

Twenty-two paces, roughly, of a room about nine wide, with a stone floor at the near end that gave way after the fourth pace to an old timber deck laid on heavy joists. The proportions were wrong for a shelter and exactly right for a school: the ceiling was too high, the window slots were too small and too regular, and along the outer wall four iron hooks had been set into the stone at the height of a grown adult's shoulder, spaced seven feet apart, each one with a socket worn pale around it. Ropes, benches, or slates had hung there. The hooks had no purpose now except the one they had always had, which was to be useful to somebody in a hurry.

At the far end a slate shelf was cut into the stone, and on it sat three boxes, closed, with a fourth space left empty where a fourth box had stood for a long time. Old painted marks still showed on the wall near the shelf: a white arrow, and beneath it a second arrow in a different hand, repainted over the first, both pointing at the high grille.

The floor told the rest. Along the middle of the deck ran two pale paths a foot wide, worn through the black of old use, curving to avoid a joist that had been replaced in somebody's grandfather's time and never matched. Nine smaller paths fanned out from the wall at the shelf side, where children had knelt in rows. The paths were not decoration. They were the shape of a hundred years of the same forty minutes.

Pell looked at them for a long time from the doorway.

"They stood here," he said. "Nine of them. And one stood here who was taller, because the path is deeper."

Nobody asked him which one was the teacher.

Leena had come up in the litter as far as the stair head and no further, because the turn at the top was a quarter-turn and her shoulder could not take a corner being set down. Oren and Tams had carried her; it had cost them both, and it had cost the litter-bearers' future, because the survey had now written down that this room, if it were ever used, would have to be entered by people who could carry somebody out of it.

"Write that down," Leena said, before Marek could. "The stair is a fixed flight of fourteen treads with a quarter-turn at the head. A litter comes up and a litter goes down. It takes two bearers, and one of them has to walk backwards. If the room above this stair is ever a room people wait in, then this stair is part of its escape."

"Is it part of its escape or part of its throat?" Iven asked.

Leena looked at him. "It is both, and I would like the record to say both, because a room that has one door is not a refuge and a room whose only door is a stair with a turn on it is a room with a throat on top of a throat."

Nera had gone straight to the high grille.

It was a hole cut in the end wall about the height of a tall man's shoulder, the size of two hands together, with a wooden louvre gone grey and half its slats missing. Beyond it, the shaft rose into darkness. She held the dyed cloth under its lower lip and it did nothing at all.

"This tells me the shaft is still open," she said. "It tells me nothing about where the air goes when a room needs to breathe out."

She went back down the stair without hurrying, crossed the corridor, and came back with a smooth hearth-stone wrapped in a folded cloth. Mikka had sent it, warmed and then cooled in a basin so it could be carried; it was hot enough to blister a careless hand and Nera held it by the cloth with the flat of her fingers.

"Everyone stays off the low slots," she said.

There were four low slots in the gallery's inner wall, cut low down and covered with a wooden grille, one every five paces, opposite the four iron hooks. They were the reason the room could probably work. They sat below the level of the deck.

Nera set the warm stone on the deck at the room's center and crouched beside the first slot. After a while the cloth at the slot's edge lifted very slightly, outward, and the dye on it held its color.

"Air is coming up out of the corridor and out here," she said. "The gallery has a supply. Now watch what happens to it when the room is full."

Iven went up onto the deck. He was the heaviest man available, which made him a poor substitute for thirty people and a good first approximation. He walked to the second slot and stood on the boards directly over it, one foot on each side, and put his whole weight down.

The cloth at the slot stopped moving.

Not gradually. It stopped.

"That is the floor closing the room's own air supply," Nera said. "Every person who stands on a deck board stops the vent beneath it. Thirty people on that deck is a sealed floor with a hole in the wall at one end."

Iven stepped off. The cloth lifted again.

"And the hole?" Marek asked.

"That is what I brought the stone for." Nera set the warm stone beneath the high grille and watched the air in the room move toward it. The thermal pulled a lazy current across the deck. The cloth at the high grille lifted, drawn inward and up, and Nera put a second strip of dyed cloth at the mouth of the shaft, higher, where the air was going. "The supply goes up that shaft. Where it comes out is the question. If it comes out, the room breathes. If it does not, the room fills with what thirty people have breathed and the only relief is the low slots, which thirty people will be standing on."

"Could not someone open a slot?" Iven said.

"Someone could. While thirty people are behind them, in the dark, on a stair with a turn, in a room where the air has already gone wrong. That is not a design. That is a hope with a handle."

Marek had been standing very still, because the demonstration had taken his proposal apart in about ninety seconds, and he had had the answer before the evidence, which was the thing Leena had stopped him doing in the corridor the day before — *say it in words and not as a plan* — and which he apparently could not stop doing.

"Nera," he said carefully, "if the deck comes up and the void beneath it is opened, the low slots are no longer beneath a walkable floor. Then the room has a real supply and the high grille may be its only relief."

"The high grille is its only relief," Nera agreed, "and we do not know where that shaft goes. I will not certify a room on a shaft I have not followed. Not for thirty people."

That was the end of the air plan, and it happened before Marek had drawn anything, which was exactly the order he should have insisted on from the beginning.

He moved to the structure.

The joists were the good part. Six of them, each about nine inches deep, laid across the narrow direction, seated at the outer wall in a stone pocket and at the inner side on a row of four timber posts. The posts stood inside a stone kerb that ran the length of the gallery, and the kerb sat on the upper wall of the lower corridor, on the corridor side. Marek had followed that line with his eyes and then with the brass rule, bound to a folded strip of cloth, before Nera came back up.

"Those posts are the whole building," he said. "Everything in this room goes through them, into the kerb, into the corridor wall."

"And the corridor wall is the later one," Iven said. "Look at the coursing. Old stone, then a course of thin stuff, then the wall you are looking at. Somebody built the corridor narrower than the room above and did not care what happened over the top of it."

Marek put his left palm flat against the wall. For a moment there was that shallow pull under the stone, a relative change travelling from the post line toward the corridor, and then nothing at all — a door closing on him. He had felt four of these. He had never once learned to predict one, and the temptation to try was the same every time: that this time, with the hand on the stone, it would finally mean something specific.

It did not.

"I have nothing to add," he said. "The stone did not tell me anything I did not already have from the marks."

"Write that down too," Jo said from the doorway, where she had come up once to see the room and had then gone back down, because a floor she had been told not to trust was not a floor she was going to admire. "That is going to be the most useful line on your page."

Marek wrote it.

Then the disagreement started, and it started about a beam.

The old gallery had one visible structural member inside the room, a squared timber let into the wall above the high grille and running the full length of the outer wall, with its ends in stone pockets. It was oak, or something the color of it, and its lower edge carried a shallow curve that told Marek it had been under load for a very long time. It was, as far as anyone could see, sound.

Iven agreed. That was the difficulty.

"Sound and useful," he said, standing under it with his hands behind his back. "That is a good beam. If you build a second deck off it, you are building off a hundred years of somebody else's good work, and you are not cutting it."

"And if we do not use it, we have to replace it."

"Then replace it. The question is with what."

Marek had the stores list in his head, because he had asked for it at the review. The Hearthhold repair timber was a named quantity: a six-piece standing stock of sound short lengths, a bundle of split lath, forty feet of good black cord, and a set of hardwood wedges. The standing stock had been committed to the kitchen brace and the clinic sill. The longest sound length in the stores was six feet.

The joists spanned the nine feet of the room's width, outer pocket to post.

"Six feet," Marek said. "With six-foot stock, a nine-foot joist is two lengths and a scarf. A bolted scarf joint, two through-bolts, staggered, iron straps top and bottom. That is a stronger joint than anything the old carpenters would have used, and it is inspectable from the floor."

"It is not inspectable," Iven said. "Not the joint. You can see the straps. You cannot see the ends of the old joists, because they are already inside the posts, and you cannot see the bolt holes because the bolt goes through four hundred years of oak in the dark. What you have built is the one joint in the room whose condition no living person will ever know, and you have put it over a post line, in the middle of a floor where thirty people will be walking back and forth all day."

"Then the splice goes where there is no post."

"Then the splice goes where the load has to cross from one joist to the next, and there is no point in the middle of a joist where a splice can live." Iven turned around. He was not angry; he was doing the thing he did when a plan was about to be built on a fact nobody had checked. "Marek, the crowd does not stand still. It walks. It goes to a door and comes back. In a school gallery the whole class moves at once, every time, for forty minutes at a time, in the same direction, because the teacher says so. You are proposing to lay a floor for a moving load and then splice it in the middle of the length. Either there is no splice or the floor is not for a moving load, and the second one means the room is not a refuge."

Marek wanted to answer with a calculation. He had the numbers: the crowd load, the joist span, the strap capacity, the bolt spacing. He had done this kind of arithmetic ten thousand times. What he did not have was the state of the inside of a timber in a stone pocket in a room nobody had opened in a living memory, and no arithmetic had ever solved that problem. It had only ever been possible to solve it by not cutting the timber, or by having a better timber, and the Hearthholds did not have better timber.

"Six feet," he said. "That is the real limit. I am not going to pretend I have timber."

"No," Iven said. "You are going to ask for timber, and when they say no, you are going to build anyway with a joint you cannot see, because the alternative is admitting the room is too big. And that is the same sentence that made the lintel in the first room look solid."

Marek took the grease pencil out and put it away a second time.

The survey closed with a number of written refusals and one declared design, which was not yet a plan and would not become one without a cost nobody had yet agreed to pay.

Dalla read the page back to the room at the stair head.

"The design is declared," she said. "Two levels. The lower corridor is the shared throat, the clinic return, the stretcher route, and the way out. The upper gallery is the room that holds the people. That is what the six outsiders propose. It is not agreed. It is not built."

She turned the page.

"Before a full-room test is even discussed, the cost is four things. One: the deck has no sound timber at this length, and either splices are accepted or the room is not built. Two: the low slots have to be opened into a void, which means the old deck comes up, which means the school floor is gone. Three: a test with thirty people and one litter is not a laboratory. It is a school morning, a kitchen shift, and a clinic half-day, all interrupted on the same day. Four: the test has to be allowed to fail in front of the people it is for, and nobody here has ever been given that before."

Ilsa took the slate from under her arm and looked at Dalla's list without softening.

"You are asking me for the school floor," she said. "I will say yes to that when the design says what happens to the children on the day the floor is not there. Until then the answer is no, and it is not mine to soften."

"Noted," Dalla said, and wrote it down in the same hand as everything else, and looked up at Marek.

"The cost is payable," Marek said. "We are not paying it tonight. We are naming it so that when somebody asks whether the room is safe, the answer is a list and not a promise."

Nobody agreed out loud. But nobody took the list off the board either, and at the bottom of it, in Dalla's hand, there was a line for the day, and the day was still empty.
