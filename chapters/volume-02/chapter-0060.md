# Chapter 60 — A Threshold That Is Not the Last One

Renn Vale wrote the shore onto her slate later in the eleventh week, in the anteroom, in her own hand, with the room reading over her shoulder, and it took her longer than the other six terms had taken because she had to write a seventh and she had to argue with the room about whether it was one.

"Here is what is on the common landing," she said. "A shore, cut in a face by Iven Tallo, standing four foot two back from where the near landing's stone beds, with its head packed up against the underside of that stone in sized wood and a knee, and its number chalked on the end and its number under its head. It is five foot nine of public-works timber and the air engineer has the inventory line and it is closed, and the keeper received it, and the crew moved it and do not own it, and there is not one sound wedge in it, and I am writing *that* on the slate because in a year somebody will offer to wedge it and they will not be able to."

"Wedge it with what," said Tams Quill. "There is nothing."

"That is the point and it goes on the slate," Renn said, "and Jo reads it out when the tally is read, and Dalla puts it under the material."

She wrote for a while, and then she stopped with the chalk up, and the room waited.

"Here is the seventh term and I am going to say it before I write it, because in the thirty-eighth day I put a *not yet* on this slate with my name under it and I meant it every day since.

"**The lip is not held. The lip is packed.** It goes about a finger and a half with a man under it and it went about two inches before, and the shore took a quarter of an inch off the worst day of Jo Verrick's life and it did not take the man out of it. The next time that slab turns, somebody will be under that lip with a knee on a piece of wood and their whole weight on it, and it will be Jo Verrick, and if she is on the lever it will be somebody whose name we do not have yet, and I want that on this slate in Renn Vale's hand, in ink, with a date, so that it is not a thing people find out."

"You are writing down that a person will be under a stone," said Sefu Aran.

"I am writing down that a person will be under a stone *and that this room knows it*, and that is not the same as writing down that a person will be under a stone," Renn said. "One of those is a fact. The other one is a plan. I have got a road and a line and a shore and I am not writing anybody a plan."

She wrote it, and read it out, and Dalla Sen took it down in her own hand and read it back, and it went into the ledger with the slate's number against it, and the slate is Renn Vale's and stays hers and no person may add a name to it, and nobody has.

---

The ledger was read at the fifth bell on the last morning, and it took an hour and ten minutes, and it is the only hour in a fortnight that anybody in that building was frightened of, and it happened because Talla Roke asked to be there.

"I have been in this room nine days," she said, "and I have listened to a very careful woman read out figures, and I am a courier and I do not know how to read a ledger, and I would like somebody to read me the whole of it. All of it. Because I am going to walk out of here at first light, and I would like to be able to say to the middle gate that I know what this boundary has and does not have, and I would rather know it from the book than from a person."

Dalla Sen read it, and she did not read the whole of it, and the not-reading was the interesting part and it was argued about for twenty minutes.

"You are going to skip the stores," Talla said.

"The stores are the ninth week's," Dalla said. "They were read out in the ninth week and the reading is on the page and the page does not change because a courier has arrived. I am going to read you what is **new since the fourth of the month**, and there is a great deal of it, and if you want the stores as well then I will read them and you will be here until dark and you will not have listened to a word of it."

"I want the ones that are new," said Talla Roke, "and I want somebody to write down that I said that, because in four days I am going to be in a room where somebody will tell me a Hearthhold has stores."

"Written," Dalla Sen said, "in her words, with the time on it."

So the clerk read the new entries, and she read them the way she reads everything, which is that you can tell where she got them.

*The pump. The delivery check stopped chattering at the second interval of the second draw in the tenth week and is now a flat hiss, and it is a thing read by ear and not a thing looked at. The vocabulary is two notes. A hiss that stays a hiss, and a hiss that gets thinner, and the thinner one is the seat, and the seat is going. The estimate on the seat is two or fewer, in Jo Verrick's own hand with a time on it, and it has not been moved in eleven weeks. There is no spare check and no bronze.*

"Read that line again," said Jo Verrick.

"*A hiss that gets thinner, and the thinner one is the seat, and the seat is going.*"

"I did not write *and the seat is going*," Jo said. "I wrote *a thinner hiss is the seat*. That is a different sentence. Mine is a rule. Hers is a finding, and hers is the correct one, and I want both and I want them marked as two people's, because in a year somebody is going to ask me whether I thought the seat was going in the tenth week and the answer is no, I thought it in the eleventh, out loud, at a frame, in a room."

"It is marked," said Dalla Sen, and read the two lines back as two, in two hands, and Jo Verrick said *that is right* in the voice she uses when a thing has been done properly.

*The air documents for the lower pump gallery are four, all in the air engineer's own hand. The first carries the word *once* on purpose and carries one line added under it: *first descent, this card, and it did not finish*. The second cuts the time under the six from eleven minutes to five. The third was written on the morning of the fourth trip. The fourth is a method for one work — the recovery of one length of five foot nine from the bottom shelf at the cache mark — it carries the word **spent** and the number three, and it survives for nobody.*

*Recovered and received: one length, five foot nine, public-works, inventory number chalked on the end, condition as found, sound, no decay and no split, received by the boundary's keeper with a time on it, the inventory line closed in the air engineer's hand, and the crew took no possession and made no claim.*

*The route, on Renn Vale's slate, in Renn Vale's hand: six terms and a seventh, and no person may add a name to it, and the page that says what the crew did is the crew's and the page that says what the route is is the boundary's, and the two pages are not the same page.*

*The closure. The route was shut once in the tenth week by the woman from the wash rooms, on her own hand on the cord, and by her own word, and the reason in her hand, and the clerk read it back twice at her request. It cost a day of Ilsa Norr's school and the pump interval it was shut for, and it was right. The earlier closure of the ninth week, which cost a half-day of a shutter route that pays Jo Verrick and a day of the same school, is a separate entry above it and stays a separate entry above it.*

"That is a correction," said Dalla Sen, without being asked, "and I have made it in my own hand because both numbers are mine and one of them was wrong in the direction of the ledger sounding more careful than it is. The half-day belongs to the ninth week. It has been read out twice this fortnight as though it belonged to both."

*And this is the first entry in nine weeks with a stranger's name in it, and it is entered on the clerk's own authority and not anybody else's: a Cartwright route-custody clerk of the Marrowgate clearance office attended at this boundary as an inventory witness, is not a person of this boundary, cannot hold a watch, cannot be on a rota, and wrote in the margin of her own form that a route in this Hearthhold is maintained by the people whose rooms it is under and held by nobody, and that she was told so by the keeper and understood it. A resident keeper refused that clerk permission to observe a crossing, gave reasons, and did not withdraw them, and both halves of that answer are entered because both halves were given on purpose.*

*And: an order from the route-council clerk of Hearthhold Middle, carried thirty-one days by a courier, for the release of the fallen parapet slab and the bulk public-works stores to the Middle water-lock, was withdrawn in the anteroom by the courier who carried it, in the courier's own hand, with the time on it, and the clerk records the distinction she was asked to record: a refusal is the clerk's and stands and can be appealed; a withdrawal is the courier's and can be made again by anybody with a mark. The cost of the withdrawal is four households in the second gate carrying water up four sets of steps this winter, and the clerk records that in her own hand as well.*

*And: a question asked in the tenth week, in this room, by Ilsa Norr, and not answered. **Who keeps the Hearthhold rooms.** It is on this page with the week and the room and the people in it, and there is no answer under it, and there is no word *pending* on it, because *pending* is a word that makes a thing look like it is being worked on.*

*And: in a school store, in a locked box, under one key, are nine register boxes, and one plan rolled on a dowel with a tape round it, and the key is Ilsa Norr's, and the boxes were refused in front of the room, and it is agreed out loud by the room and recorded with a date that the key turns in three weeks, and the terms will be said by the person who keeps it, and the crew will not be in the room when it is done.*

*And: on the second bell, by a person, by hand, with a bar, the north-east cheek of the stair's throat is sounded, and the answer is written down whatever it is, including nothing. The air engineer heard a knock from about eleven feet down at the second minute of the nineteen strokes on a Sunday in the tenth week, and did not go, and the bar has been against that wall every morning since. The first morning the bar was put there the answer was nothing. The second morning it was nothing. The third was a knock, with a slide on the end of it, and the time of it, and the person who heard it, and the air engineer present, and no person went down, and the record says that in that order.*

That last one went into the room twice, because Talla Roke asked what a knock was, and Dalla Sen read it again, and then read it a third time with the two knocks kept separate — the one the air engineer heard at the lip and the one the bar found against a wall — because they are two events and the second is not the first and a record that runs them together is a record that will be wrong about something that matters.

"It is not the same knock," Bel Ordry said.

"It is not the same knock," Dalla Sen said, "and it is on the page as two, in two places, with two sets of hands, and the second one is in mine and the first one is in the air engineer's, and neither of us is going to be asked to explain either of them again."

---

Sefu Aran read his own paragraph separately, and had it marked separately, and read out the sentence that he had insisted upon in the ninth week, and this time it was not new, and this time everybody in the room knew exactly which use it was going to be put to.

"**That the day this route is first used for anything other than air and its own testing, it is on the clerk's page, with the name of the person who shut it and the name of the person who opened it, and the reason, and the date.**"

"Sefu," said Dalla Sen.

"It has been used. A fortnight ago, on the shore. Not for air and not for its own testing. **For work.** Three people from above went down three steps onto the fixed first landing, and a woman of this boundary packed the head of a length of timber with her own knee and her own weight for a day and a half, and a woman from this boundary stood at the lip for the whole of it as a bearer, and another read the plinth, and another held the string, and a boy on a bench at the top of the nine steps called the interval eight seconds early and was right.

That is not air. That is not testing. That is a boundary doing maintenance on a route with the people it lives with, and the name of the person who opened it is the person who untied the cord, which was Renn Vale, and Sefu Aran, and nobody else may untie that cord, and the name of the person who shut it is the same two, at the end, and the reason is in their words and I am not going to improve it.

"So it goes on your page, Dalla. Today. With the date. And I want it read out in the anteroom, and I want the room to hear what it sounds like, because in nine weeks that sentence was a piece of a keeper's policy and in a minute it is going to be the first line in a record that says **a Hearthhold worked on its own route, and shut it, and the whole of it was done by people who live here.**"

And Dalla Sen wrote it, and read it back, and the room heard it, and nobody in that anteroom said one word about it for about as long as it takes to boil a pot, and then Mikka Hale said, "Well," and Ilsa Norr said, "About time," and Renn Vale went down the three steps to the service bend and put the back of her hand on the foot of a shore that was not hers, and did not say anything about that either.

---

The tally was read at the fifth bell and Tams Quill read the two columns last.

"Thirty-eight and a half, and fifty-eight," he said. "And I am not going to divide it and I am not going to stop saying it, and the sentence is the same sentence it was in the ninth week and it is one item longer.

"**The half that gets counted is the half that is ours.**"

"And the obligation," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel, because he had asked for it since the seventh week and had never once been refused.

"Which has not ended," said Marek Venn, "because the end of it is that the boundary can do it without us and a person named by the boundary has said so out loud, in this room, with the reason, and nobody has said it, and there is a woman in this room who has said it out loud for eleven weeks that she will not be on that stair and will not be on that landing, and there is a woman who has a dead mother in a hole and will not be asked, and those are two of the four people who could say it, and I am the third, and none of the three of us is going to say it this month."

"Not me," said Renn Vale, from the service bend, without turning round. "Not this month, and not because of the work. Because I am not going to say a thing like that about a boundary I have only just started believing I am allowed to have."

---

Talla Roke went out at first light with a courier's satchel and a drawing of the second gate's inlet folded inside it, and a copy of the withdrawn order in her own hand, and nothing else, because everything in that anteroom belonged to somebody.

She went up the nine steps past the bench where her brother was sitting with his ear against the stone, and she stopped, and she did not kneel down, and she did not put a hand on him, and she said:

"Your hand opens in the eleventh week and it has not come yet and it will be a hand and not a fist, and I am going to be four hundred miles from here, and I want you to be careful with it in a way that I would be if I had one."

"You have a hand," Pell Roke said.

"I have a hand," she agreed, "and I have been told nothing about it, because nobody in this room says anything to anybody in a way they did not ask to hear first, and that is the single strangest thing anybody has ever said to me, and I have been a courier for four years."

Then she went up, and Dalla Sen wrote down the time and the words, because in this boundary a thing said at the top of nine steps is a thing said, and a thing said is a thing the book can hold.

---

And on the last morning, at the second bell, a person sounded the throat with a bar.

It was Bel Ordry, and he had asked for it, and Nera Oduya had said yes, and he was frightened and did it anyway, and he wrote the answer down afterwards in his own hand, and the answer was *nothing*, and Bel Ordry said, out loud, in the anteroom, to nobody, "That is a real thing that I have written down. That is me, and a bar, and a wall, and nothing. That is going to be on the page tomorrow and it is going to be on the page the day after that, and the day somebody hears something, that is going to be the day somebody knows I have been here every morning doing nothing and doing it properly."

And the review line was named at the fourth bell, and it was not the long one, and Dalla Sen read it out before she wrote it and the room took it apart twice, and it came back with two changes, and it went in the way review lines go in, with a name and a time and Jo Verrick's hand under Dalla Sen's.

*Due immediately if the pump stops, if the air cloth moves, if the water reaches the sill, if any watch is empty, if the cord moves, if the seat goes — and the hiss has been thin since the Thursday, so the seat is going now and not in the cold — if the guild's door opens, if anybody proposes reopening the old school gallery, if anybody in this boundary writes the word *out* on that slate, and if anybody at the throat hears anything at all.*

And the unresolved list did not go away. It got its eleventh item and kept the other ten, and it is written in the clerk's hand and in a hand that is not the clerk's, and the second half of it is on a teacher's slate, and neither of them has an answer under it.

*The bronze delivery seat, and the check, and no bronze, and a whole hand, and not Tams Quill's.* The timber for the school-door head, which is a wedge short and a length short, and the refusal is Renn Vale's and is not withdrawn. The carpenter's wage for a man a guild will not pay. Nineteen days of meals, given and not waived. A sealed litre of verified condensation in a stoppered jar. Iven Tallo's bond, suspended and named. A form unsigned in a Marrowgate office, to which six people had added a school floor, a lip with no post under it, and a dead man on a stair, and to which a clerk has now added a page in her own margin about a route held by nobody. The guild's door, locked, with an order on it that has never once opened anything, and a clearance office forty days of walking away that has now been told, in writing, by a competent person, in a form it understands.

And: **a post under the near landing's lip, which wants nine foot and a bundle, and which is not built, and which is the tenth item on that list, and which is the reason there is a woman under a stone at all.**

And the two questions the page still carries, in the clerk's hand, with the week on them and the people in the room, and no answer:

*Who keeps the Hearthhold rooms, and why do the oldest plans in this building show homes and not treasure?*

*And if a route like this one is built by a rescue crew and kept by the people who sleep under it, is it a public thing — or is it a road with a name on it, and a page in a file, and a lock on the end?*

The plate did not go anywhere, because nobody went anywhere. It is in its bundle of work cloth with the corners still knotted off by Mikka Hale's hand, on a shelf above the anteroom bench where the bench was needed, and the shortest entry in that ledger is still the shortest, and it was read out once this morning in the middle of a long reading and nobody in the room said a word about it, which is the correct amount of words.

*Unexplained. Not identified, not used, not opened, not claimed.*

And under it, in the crew's own keeping, in a pocket that belongs to Marek Venn and not to the boundary: one brass survey rule, damaged, belonging to a man who went into the Sable Cut fourteen years ago and did not come out of it. It has not said a word in eleven weeks and it did not say one this morning, and Marek did not put his hand on it, and there is nobody left in the building who would be able to tell him whether that was restraint or luck.

And the whole of the boundary went on being lived in. The wheel turned twice under the floor and went on going under the conversation in the kitchen line the way weather goes on under a conversation. The pot was on. The school corner's floor was laid and the grit had not come up once since the floor went over. The corridor was shut on the days six people agreed to shut it, and open on the days the school ran, and the waist was a post on the open days and not a post on the shut ones, and everybody knew which they were standing in. The school door was two foot eight at the head under a cracked lintel with three wedges in it and one missing, and the missing one was not going to arrive, and a woman had said in her own words that she would rather it came down on her, because that is where the door she keeps is.

And in three weeks a schoolteacher was going to turn a key in a locked box in a store and unroll a plan off a dowel in front of the people who lived there, and not in front of the five who had found their way down a shaft, and nobody in that anteroom knew what was on it, and one of them had said out loud that she would not be trusted with it, and one of them had put his hands on the cloth either side of a bundle he has not opened and got nothing, and wrote it down nowhere, because there was nothing to write it on and he knew what it would say.

It was the first useful thing that Hearthhold had put under the place where a man had died, and it was five foot nine of somebody else's timber with a number on the end of it, and it was kept by a woman, and it was not enough, and the page said so in Renn Vale's hand with a date on it. And on the fourth morning of the eleventh week, at the second bell, a man nobody had trained put a bar against a wall eleven feet up and wrote down *nothing*, and put the bar back, and went to his own work.
