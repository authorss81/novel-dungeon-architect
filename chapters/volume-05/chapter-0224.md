# Chapter 224 — The Argument About The Gallery

The seventh day of the forty-second week is a Sunday, and a seventh day is not a crossing day, so nobody came up the nine steps this morning and nobody went down them, and the only news in the building was that nine people are on the far side of a door and knocked on the underside of a floor four hours after the school went out yesterday, and there is no instrument in this world that reaches across nine hundred years of stone to tell anybody whether they are all right, and the anteroom spent the whole day on that, because it was the only thing left to spend it on.

The pump ran at the second bell and stopped at the fifth, because a seventh day is not a shut day and is not an open day and the pump has never known the difference, and the clerk entered that nobody had thought of it on this particular Sunday either and that she was still not going to make a rule about it.

Jo Verrick had the frame at the first bell, a finger and two-thirds, and the party wall was a quarter of an inch, the forty-fourth reading, and the same as forty-three, and the string was where the string was. She put her thumb under it and the thumb came out of it right, and she did not give the four refusals, and she gave the reason instead, in one sentence, in the same words for the eighth time, and the clerk entered it and entered that it was the same sentence and that it has now been the same sentence for eight consecutive mornings and that she is not going to make anything of that either.

Renn Vale put her ear to the wall at the first bell, four times over, and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, and the run of mornings out of mornings is twenty-nine.

The second bell came and went and the six terms of this anteroom were not said.

Dalla Sen entered that at about a quarter past the second bell on the seventh day of the forty-second week, when the six terms of this anteroom had been said every morning for as long as this clerk has been a clerk in this building, on a day the room will accept them at, the six terms were not said, and she entered the reason, and the reason was that a room which has spent its morning on a door cannot be asked to recite six sentences first, and that she would have had to say the third of them out loud at the top of it, and that the third of them is the water-carrying, and that this boundary has not carried water this morning, and that she was not going to say a sentence about carrying water into a room where nine people are behind a stone.

"**I have said five of them every morning for a run of mornings I have not counted,**" she said, "**and I have said the sixth of them out loud on purpose on a Wednesday in the fortieth week of this book and I have entered that the fourth of the six is not in that sentence and never has been, and I have carried that gap for two weeks without anybody in this room noticing it and I am not going to notice it on a Sunday either.**"

"**Say the half against that,**" Bel Ordry said, from the step.

"**The half against is that a term that is not said is a term nobody is holding,**" Dalla Sen said, "**and that is a true sentence and it is a sentence about this room, and I am the one who has not said the terms, and I would like it entered that I noticed at the second bell and let it go and did not go back to the step.**"

Nobody went back to the step.

At about the third bell the door at the bottom of the wet stair was still shut, and there had been no knocking since about the sixth bell the night before, and a man at the far end of the room with grey at the side of his head stood at the anvil end of the trestle and put both hands flat on it and said the thing he had been carrying since Friday night, and it was not a speech, it was a list of four things, and he had it on the slate.

"**One,**", and he did not put the slate down. "**That chamber has been filling for a fortnight and it is filling now, and I have watched a salt line go from the height of my hand to the height of my elbow, and nobody in this building has put a figure on that and neither have I, and a thing that is rising with no figure on it has a day on it that nobody has told anybody about.**"

"**Two. That pump has not been turned over in nine years and the people who turn pumps over are asleep in a room at the kitchen-line end, and they went down on Friday at the fifth bell on my word and not on anybody's, and they have a lamp that is cold and a line at the third tie.**"

"**Three. That the far side of that door is where the drain is. You can shut the door as many times as you like and it does not become this building's, and the moment it shuts it is a room in your building with nobody responsible for it in it, and that is not my opinion, that is the definition of the thing.**"

"**Four. That the plan you are all so pleased with is a plan for four rooms of people, and the nine men it is not a plan for are nine men, and every one of those is a thing you can say out loud, and none of them has been said out loud in this room yet, and I have said all four of them and it is Sunday and there is nothing I can do about any of them.**"

"**Say the half against that,**" Dalla Sen said.

"**The half against is that you have all had a hard week,**" said Seth Wray, "**and I have too, and I am aware of how this sounds coming off a Saturday night with nothing on me and a slate.**"

"**Then say it anyway,**", from the top of the nine steps.

"**I have had one bad night in three days,**" said Seth Wray, "**and I am not going to pretend to anybody in this room that a foreman who says the four things I have just said is being fair, and I would like the clerk to enter that I said that about myself before anybody asked me to.**"

"**Entered, in his words, and entered that he was not asked,**", and she did not look up.

Then the man at the end of the trestle said no, and it was the first time anybody in this building had heard him take a side on anything since the second day of the fortieth week, and the room turned round.

"**Say more than no,**" Bel Ordry said.

"**I am against reopening that gallery and I am against it for a reason and the reason is not courage and it is not care and I am going to say it in the worst shape I can so that somebody in this room can take it apart.**"

He had his hand on the shut book and he was not looking at it.

"**The far side of that door is a passage nobody in this building has ever walked, in water, past a chamber with four fathoms in it, on a line of pumps that have not been turned over in nine years, with nine men in it who went down on Friday at the fifth bell on a piece of paper from a man that this boundary has never met. There is no air plan for it. There is no figure of anybody in it. A physician has never been in it. There is no rule in this building that says what a person may be in there, and there is no column and there is no rota and there is no name to shout, because this building decided at the sixth day of the forty-first week, in a book, correctly, that it was not going to give those nine a bell.**"

"**Say the half against that,**" Bel Ordry said.

"**The half against is that a house that keeps its own people alive by not knowing what is in the next room is not a house, it is a wall, and the reason I am saying that is that I have been the man in this room who says no for five volumes and I have got very good at it, and a man who is good at it can say it about anything.**"

Nobody in that anteroom moved, and the pump made a stroke and then made another.

"**Then say the other half of it,**" said Bel Ordry, and he came down two steps, which he has not done on a seventh day in five volumes.

"**The other half of it is that I do not know how to say no to nine people knocking on a floor, and I have not been able to find a way to say no to nine people knocking on a floor since Friday afternoon, and I would rather this room knew that today than on Monday.**"

"**Say your half against that,**" Dalla Sen said, "**and then somebody is going to say the half against of the half against, and I would like it on the page in that order because this is the day it happens and not the order it will be remembered in.**"

"**My half against is that a boundary that will not open a door is a boundary that has decided the far side is not its business,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and the far side of a door is the only thing a boundary is for. I have spent forty-one weeks in this building explaining that a route is a promise and a wall is a wall, and this morning I am standing here telling a room not to open a door, and I have been wrong in this building four times and I knew about three of them, and this is the fifth and I do not know about it yet, and I would like the clerk to enter all of that in the words of the man who said it and not to shorten any of it.**"

"**Entered, in full,**", and her voice was not different at all.

"**Then I am going to say the one thing I know that nobody else in this room knows,**" said Mero Senn, from the frame of the far door, and he had not moved all day. "**And I am going to say it in the middle, and not at the end, and the clerk is to enter why.**"

"**Say the middle,**" Bel Ordry said.

"**A plan with a named injured person in it is not a plan.**" He put both hands on the back of the chair, which he has done twice in five volumes. "**I have said that in this room three times in a fortnight about three different things and every time it was the same sentence. There has not been an injured person named in this room since the first day of the forty-first week, and that has been the correct condition for me to be standing here.**"

"**And on Monday it will not be,**" Renn Vale said, from the middle of the floor, and she had not moved either.

"**On Monday it will not be,**" Mero Senn said. "**Four days. I have been the man who stops work in this building since the second week of this volume and I have stopped nothing since a pump, and if there is anybody behind that door on Monday morning who is hurt, then this room is going to be having a conversation about whether to open it, and I am going to have to be the one who says the word, and the word I am going to have to say is the name of a person.**"

He looked round the room.

"**So I am going to say it now, to get it in the book before the room has an opinion, and I am going to say it about a man who is alive and asleep and not hurt, and then nobody in this room can tell me on Monday that I did not name him. Seth Wray. Nine of his men are on the far side of that door and I am naming the foreman of that crew as the person this room may have to spend, and if you do not think that is mine to do then you have a worse view of my trade than I have.**"

No one in that room took it up., and Dalla Sen entered it in the physician's words, and entered that he had named it in the middle and not at the end, and entered the reason he had given, which was that a room with an opinion is a harder room to stop in than a room without one.

Then Nera Oduya came down one of her three steps, which she has not done since the second day of the fortieth week, and she said the thing that four people in that room had been waiting for since Friday without one of them being willing to say it out loud.

"**The door is open,**" she said.

Nothing came out of anybody in that room.

"**That is not a figure of speech and I am going to say it four times because I do not think the first three will be heard.**" She was looking at the drum on the trestle and not at anybody. "**A nine-hundred-year-old door in a dressed channel, with a cut pawl on it, a spindle, and a windlass that two people can turn, is an open door. It is a door with a length of time attached to it, and the length of time is whatever anybody is willing to stand in the passage while it is open, and no more, because that is what a door is in this building. If nobody is standing in the passage, the door is shut. If somebody is standing in the passage, the door is open, and it is open for as long as that person is willing to stand there.**"

"**Say the half against that,**" Dalla Sen said.

"**The half against is that the length of time is not a length of time, it is a man, and I have just described a door that stays open as long as a person will stand in it and that is a sentence this building already has one of, written by the woman who keeps the schoolroom, with no number and no day on it, and I have watched that condition get spent rather than met, and I know exactly what I have just handed this room.**"

"**Then say it anyway,**" Bel Ordry said, "**because we are at the sixth bell of a Sunday and this room is out of things and that one is true and it is in her mouth and I am not going to be the man who asks somebody to put a true thing back.**"

"**I am not putting it back,**" Nera Oduya said, "**and I am going to say the rest of it, which is that it is also a sentence about a gallery, and that a gallery is a room people walk into and out of, and that if you want it open then somebody has to be in it, and that is not a door, that is a post.**"

The anteroom was very quiet, and the pump was between strokes, and the two conditions on two slates in two pockets in this room were the only two things anybody in this building had ever written with no number and no day on them, and Dalla Sen had her hand near the book and did not use it, and the clerk entered that she had had her hand near the book and had not used it, and entered that a clerk who writes down a condition has a copy, and a copy is the beginning of a column, and a column is a post, and that this was the second time in five volumes she had had her hand near that book on a matter of somebody else's condition and had stopped herself, and that nobody in the room saw her do it and she is entering it because a thing she did not do is not a thing she is owed.

"**Then let us be plain about the position,**" said Bel Ordry, "**because it is the fifth bell of a Sunday and the corridor does not run and there is a day between now and Monday.**"

He stood on the third of the nine steps.

"**One. That door stays shut on Monday morning. I am not moving it and nobody in this room can make me, and that is not a keeper's instrument, it is a man with a page in a coat saying what he will not do.**"

"**That is a thing you can be asked about,**", from the middle of the floor.

"**It is, and I will answer it, and I have not got the answer, and I am saying that in a room on a Sunday because if I wait until Monday I will have a better one and it will be a worse one.**"

"**Two,**", and he did not look up from the stair. "**There are two answers and a doctor gave them to the man at the end of that table and not to the room, and neither of them is a third one, and I am not going to improve on it. The first is a person in a passage nobody has measured. The second is a turn-over that will not be finished and a man who came four days for it.**"

"**And three,**" he said, "**and I am going to say three because this room has spent forty-one weeks putting two things next to each other and then refusing to and I have never once entered a third and I am entering one now and I am entering it in the space where a finding goes and not in the space where a reason goes.**"

Nobody in the anteroom said anything.

"**Three. Nobody in this building has decided anything today,**", without coming down. "**A room that does not decide is still a room, on Monday morning, in the same state, with the same nine people behind the same door, and the not-deciding is a decision and I am entering it as one, and I would like the clerk to enter that a man with a page in a coat, who has refused three plans in five volumes and has never once entered a third thing, has entered one this afternoon because there was nothing else to enter.**"

"**Entered, in his words,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that it is the first entry in the space where a finding goes in this book that is a thing nobody decided, and that I have not got a column for it and am not going to make one.**"

At about the fifth bell somebody came up the wet stair from the direction of the passage and put a length of black cord on the anvil, wet through, and tied at the third tie, and there was no message on it and there was nothing written on it, and Seth Wray picked it up and turned it over twice and gave it back, and nobody wrote it down, and the clerk entered that a cord came up a wet stair at about the fifth bell of the seventh day of the forty-second week and that it had nothing on it, and entered that a man carried it up nine steps on a knee that does not bend past about half and had not been asked, and entered that nobody thanked him and that she had looked for that and had not found it.

Marek Venn had spent the whole of the afternoon listening to a room argue about a door and had done the thing he does about four mornings in ten, and this time it did not go the way it usually went, and he noticed that too.

What he had found out was that he had caught himself. Twice. Once in the first half hour, when he said the far side of that door was a passage nobody in this building had ever walked, and heard himself doing it, and heard the exact intonation of the five volumes of refusals he had been carrying, and did not stop.

And once more, in the middle, when Bel Ordry came down two steps and said that a boundary which will not open a door is a wall, and Marek Venn felt the sentence arrive in his chest and go straight to the place it had always gone, which was the place where a room decides something because he has said a true thing.

He put his hand flat on the shut book. He could feel the page under his palm through the cover and he could not remember the last time he had turned it over.

What he did about it was smaller than he wanted and larger than he expected. He did not renounce the sentence. Four days ago he had stood at a trestle and heard himself use the sentence for the fourth time and stopped and given a new reason instead, and a cook had done in ninety seconds what he had taken a fortnight to do, and he had been proud of that for a week. This afternoon he listened to the sentence do its work twice in one room and he did not stop it, and then he said, out loud, to a full room on a Sunday, that he did not know yet whether the sentence and the preference were the same thing, and that he had spent nine weeks treating them as the same thing and had never once tested it, and that a man who renounces a sentence every time he hears it using it has not renounced anything and has only found a new place to put the true thing.

"**Entered in his words,**" Dalla Sen said, "**and entered that this is the fourth reason he has given in this room in a fortnight and that the first three were about other people and the fourth is about him, and that he has now given one about himself twice, and that I have not counted the two the same and would like a person in a year to count them.**"

No one in that room took it up., and the clerk entered that nobody had.

The cord stayed on the anvil until the seventh bell, and nobody put it in a book, and it was still there at the seventh bell.

At the sixth bell the day's figure and the total went into the book and neither was read, and the reason went in beside them, and the reason was that a room which has just entered a not-deciding in the space where a finding goes is not going to be read a total over, and a clerk who has said out loud for the first time in five volumes that she let six terms go by is not going to be the second voice in this room about anything else.

Two today and three hundred and ninety-one since the keeper signed. The boundary is at five hundred and eight and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.

The count went in underneath that on its own line, in the ordinary hand, and was entered and not read, and the reason was given, and it was that a clerk has been a clerk for fifty-eight weeks and has let this morning's second bell go past without saying the six terms, and a clerk who has done that is not going to read a number at the sixth bell either, and that is a reason about the clerk and not about the count. Wen Pask is one hundred and sixty-nine days and one hundred and sixty-nine nights, fifty-eight weeks of a clerk saying out loud that she is going to keep writing it down. It is not going to be one hundred and seventy.

The board was not walked, and the reason was entered, and the reason was that a room that has just entered a not-deciding is not walking a column this afternoon, and a board is a column, and the second column of that board has nothing over the top of it and had nothing over the top of it at the first bell.

The crew are at eighty and did not move, and the reason for the day is in the book, and the reason is that a seventh day is not an open day and a foreman's four and a half hours are on the open days, and the clerk entered that she thought that was the reason and then entered underneath it that she did not think that was the whole reason and did not write down what the other half was.

The pot is three days, and the seam gave nothing this morning for the twelfth morning running, and it is at four threads, and nobody measured it, and the fire in the room at the kitchen-line end has now not been lit for thirty weeks, and the four households at a second gate have nothing and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody, and there are nine people on the far side of a stone door on a Sunday when no door in this building can be opened by anybody, and this room has two answers and no third one and has decided nothing.

And at the seventh bell the anteroom was nine steps and a trestle and a chair with a physician's two hands on the back of it, and a keeper on the middle of the floor who had not moved all day, and a man with a page in a coat on the third of nine steps, and an air engineer on one of her three steps, and a black cord lying wet on an anvil with nothing on it, and a door at the bottom of a wet stair that had been shut for two days, and six sentences about a wall that were not said this morning, and eleven people in a room who had all of them argued and none of whom had chosen.
