# Chapter 113 — Four Marks And A Carpenter

**The third day of the twenty-second week was a Wednesday and the corridor was open and the school ran at the far end of it, and the whole length of that corridor was between nine children and the room they had been moved out of, and nobody in this building pretended any more that those were two facts about different things.**

**The crossing took nine minutes and fifty seconds,** which is two parties and a fifth at a walk in the strokes with Isel Ferrow at the school door and Mikka Hale at the kitchen line, and Renn Vale was the first thing over and the last thing back and she did not put a hand on the fill and did not need to say so, because by the third week a room has stopped saying things it has said once.

Wen Pask carried four register boxes in the first party and the fifth with somebody else's, and put the lamp down in the kitchen-line room when he was told to and went away, and Dalla Sen entered that he did it without being told twice and that she had been about to tell him twice.

A man of twenty-two was forty-eight days and forty-eight nights into a count that was his own.

At the first bell, before any of it, Jo Verrick put her three-foot rule against the jamb of the anteroom's outer service door and looked at a line of chalk that Renn Vale had put there the day before.

**The anteroom had not been watched overnight, and it never is.** That has been true since the eighteenth week and it is the plainest fact in this building.

"**It has not moved,**" she said.

"**Say how you know, and say it in a way I can write down,**" said Renn Vale.

"**I have the rule marks on the jamb from three weeks of Tuesdays and the new one is the same width as the last one to within a bit of the edge of my rule, and I have put a hair from one of the nine's heads where the nine has one and it is still across the line, and a hair is not a measurement and I am not going to call it one.**"

Then she stood up off the sill and said the thing that made the day, and she said it to the room and not to Marek Venn.

"**I do not like that.**"

"**You have been waiting twenty-two weeks for something to change and it has not changed.**"

"**I have been waiting twenty-two weeks for a thing to change slowly, and slow is what I know, and I know it because I have watched it go out a finger and a bit in a fortnight and a finger a week in the cold. And a thing that has gone out a finger and a bit and then has not moved a hair overnight is not a thing that has stopped. It is a thing that has stopped for a reason, and I do not know the reason, and I am not going to walk past a thing I do not understand and say good morning to it.**"

Dalla Sen wrote all of that down and then wrote underneath it, in her own hand: *and the clerk is entering that the woman's objection is entered and that nothing was done about it, and that the man whose hand is on the stone was in the room when she said it.*

---

**Then they found out what it has actually done, and it was not the frame, and it was three feet away from the frame, under a shut door, in a room nobody is in.**

Jo Verrick went down the nine steps and along the corridor on her knees with the lamp because the lamp is what she uses, and there is no reason a woman with a three-foot rule and a bad knee should go into a shut room with a wet floor, so **she did not go in.** She put her hand flat on the flags through the door's gap, at the low corner, the way she put her hand on the seam in the haunch, and then she took a slate and wrote on it and slid it under the door.

Dalla Sen said it out in the anteroom, twice, and then said it in her own words because the clerk does that.

"**Found, located, tested, recorded, and not before, because the room is shut and nobody has opened it and it is not being opened for this. The dark at the low corner is the same dark as it was on the first day of the twentieth week and not the dark it was on the fifth day of the twenty-first. The line of it across the joint has not moved. The bed joint under that floor is wet to the same place it was wet to and it has stopped being wetter.**"

Then Iven Tallo said the only sentence in this building that has ever made nine people go quiet, and he said it without standing up off the bench.

"**Then the water has stopped coming up.**

"**And the water comes up that joint from the patch in the course at the haunch, and the patch is in the haunch of that door frame, and the frame is the thing with the chalk on it. So the four threads and the leaf are still there — she will have looked — and they are still giving two jars a day, maybe three, and the water is finding the same low side of that floor it found a fortnight ago, and it is arriving slower, or it is arriving the same and the floor is drinking it and the floor is not a drain.**"

"**Say which of those two it is,**" said Marek Venn.

"**I cannot and I am a carpenter and not a drain, and I have been asked for a piece of work and I have given the piece of work and the piece of work is a frame.**"

Iven Tallo put his hand flat on the bench.

"**And I want it written that I said that out loud in a room and did not soften it, because there is going to be a month where somebody in this building says the school corner dried because a man put grease on a jamb, and that is not a thing anybody should say, and if it is said it should be said with the sentence about the drain in it that I have just given.**"

---

**And the argument that the whole morning turned on was not about the frame at all, and it was about a number.**

Renn Vale wanted the mark's condition on her slate, in her hand, next to the other terms, because a condition that lives on a loose page is a condition that gets lost. She had the slate under her arm and the chalk out and she was about to write and she stopped with the chalk an inch off the stone.

"**I have to say *further out of true*, and there is no rule in this building for how much is further, and Jo has a rule with a hair across a line and a hair is not a measurement. So somebody is going to come back to this in a month and say *further than what*, and I am not writing a condition I cannot be held to.**"

"**Then we measure it,**" said Mikka Hale. "**We put a figure on it.**"

"**No,**" said Renn Vale, and then, before anybody had argued: "**And no, and here is why, and it is the same reason I have not written a number on anything since the seventh term. A number on a condition is a date, and a date is something that can be satisfied on a day, and this is not that.**"

Nera Oduya came down the four steps and did not go further, and said the thing that the room had been circling for a week.

"**There is a number for that room. There has been one for twenty-two weeks and it is the only figure anybody in this Hearthhold has for that room, and it is a ten, and it is a permission.**"

The room let it stand, which is a thing rooms in this building do about four hundred times a day.

"**A ten is not a distance. A ten is not a thickness, and it is not a load, and it is not an angle on a door frame, and it is a number somebody set about how many bodies may be in a room with one door and a word for an exit. It is the only number we have and it is the wrong shape entirely, and if it goes on that slate next to *further out of true* then in six weeks a clerk is going to read a condition about a man and a condition about a crowd and not be able to tell which is which, and the room will have made a page that means two things in one column.**"

"Then write it in words and not in a figure," said Dalla Sen, with the chalk up, "**and write the reason in your hand, and the reason is that a permission is not a measurement, and I am going to read it back and you are going to tell me if I have got it wrong.**"

She said it out a second time. Nera Oduya said: "**Right. And write that I have not said the next half of it, and that the next half of it is about something else, and that nobody in this room is going to ask me for it.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen.

---

**A man who had been sitting on a bench for an hour was then asked the question only a carpenter can be asked,** and he was asked it properly, and he was asked it in a room, and he said no.

It was Bel Ordry who asked it, which was right, and Dalla Sen wrote the terms of the asking out before he asked, which is now the way this room asks anybody for anything.

"**Iven Tallo is asked one question about the four marks and the groove on the lintel above the shut school corner's mouth, and about the line of chalk and the grease on the jamb of the anteroom's outer service door, and he is not asked to read them and he is not asked what anybody meant, and he may say no, and the person who can be told no is the woman who keeps the anteroom's terms, and if he says no then nothing is entered and it stands as unread.**"

"**There is a line of grease on a jamb in this building that is four hours old,**" said Iven Tallo, "**and I have already had one piece of work in this room and it went in the crew's column, and I am going to say yes to this one and I am going to say what it costs first.**

"**I am not going to read them. I have not read them. I have looked at that lintel every day for three weeks and I have not read it, and I am not going to now, and nobody should write down that I did.**

"**What I will give the room is one thing about the groove and it is a craft thing and you can check it. That groove is two inches and it has had something put in it and taken out more than once and the edges are not chipped, and stone does not do that without a tool. So somebody braced something off that lintel. Not a post. A brace, a short one, from the lintel back into the jamb, and it went in and came out and went in and came out, and that is not a mark, and it is not a message, and it is a hole somebody made for a piece of wood a thousand years ago and there is no wood in it now and there is not going to be any.**"

"**Then the four marks are what is left of the answer,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**The four marks are what is left of everything,**" said Iven Tallo, "**and I have been in this building twenty-two weeks and there are five marks in it now, four of them cut into stone and one of them mine by marriage only, and the four that were cut are older than any charter and the one that is four hours old is on a jamb, and in fifty years there is going to be a woman standing in front of that jamb who can read four ancient marks and cannot read a line of chalk and a smear of grease at all, and she will not know it was ever meant to be read, and I do not know one single thing anybody can do about that, and nobody in this building has ever stopped anything by deciding it would not happen.**"

He stopped. Then he said the last part, more quietly, and it was not for the room.

"**I do not want the mark off. I want to be wrong about this. I have watched a door frame go out a finger and a bit in a fortnight and I would rather be an old man being wrong about grease than be right about clay.**"

It was not entered, and Dalla Sen had been asked by Renn Vale not to enter it, and that was the whole of the transaction.

---

**At the fourth bell a courier came up the middle passage with a sheet that was not for this boundary and that the clerk read standing in the anteroom and entered in full.**

He was a road hand going on to a survey camp and he was in this building for about four minutes, and he came up the nine steps to the anteroom because that is where the boundary's own people are, and the outer service door of that room was standing open with the room emptied and a lamp in it, and there was a line of chalk and a smear of gland grease on the dressed face of the jamb at the height of a hand.

He was not asked about it. He did not stop. He did not say one word about it, and he did not look at it twice, and he gave Dalla Sen the sheet and took the clerk's mark off it and went back down the nine steps at a walk without being asked to and without being thanked.

Dalla Sen entered the whole of it afterwards, in her own hand, and read it back, and nobody found a word wrong in it, and what she entered was: *a man stood in a doorway for four seconds on the third day of the twenty-second week and saw a mark on a stone and did not stop and did not ask and is going up the middle passage this afternoon to a survey camp, and this boundary will not hear from it, and I am entering it now because in a year somebody is going to be surprised and I would like the surprise to be in a clerk's hand and not in a hole.*

It was a store order from the middle gate, and it was a surveyor's notice of the kind that goes up on a board, and the first thing in it was that **a route-clearing crew is working a survey of this boundary's sector under a clearance**, and the second thing was that the timber the halfpenny sheet promised for the month after next **is not coming**, because the timber is in the same store and the same store is now a survey camp, and the third thing was that the survey's finding would be the sector's own, and that the sector's own record would be what it was compared against.

"**Say that last part again,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**A route-clearing crew is working a survey of this boundary's sector. Its finding will be the sector's own condition. The boundary's own record is what that finding is compared against.**"

There was no argument with it. Bel Ordry made one, in a small way, and Dalla Sen entered that he had, and the clerk's entry is the whole of the objection: *the keeper of the plan says that a record written by the people who live in the sector is not a survey, and says it once and does not say it twice, and does not offer the record.*

Then they went in, and the school ran at the far end of the corridor for two bells and a bit, and Marek Venn sat down on the anteroom step with his left hand open in his lap, and it did not close.

It had not closed that night and it did not close that morning either, and the count was in the room under the pump's check, low and uneven and counted, like a thumb run along the edge of a plate, and he knew what it was and knowing it did nothing at all.

On the jamb, at the height of a hand, there was a line of chalk. **Four marks and a groove over a shut door at the far end of the corridor, and one line of chalk and a smear of grease on a jamb in a room that is full of people, and Jo Verrick did not like it, and the woman who wrote the condition did not like it either, and the only person in this building who had said he did not want the mark off was a carpenter, and he had said it under his breath.**
