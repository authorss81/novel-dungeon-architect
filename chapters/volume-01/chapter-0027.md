# Chapter 27 — Who Pays for the Room

Marek brought a clean solution to a dirty room, which was his habit, and Nera took it apart before he had finished setting it down.

They were in the anteroom on a wet afternoon with the corridor in use, which meant the sixth watch was filled and the bell line was doubled and nobody could leave the room without somebody counting them out. The pump ran its nineteen strokes, came off, and the corridor emptied through the kitchen line while Dalla wrote the time. Then the room got very quiet, and in the quiet the arithmetic started.

"Put everything in one place," Marek said. He had a board with a column on it. "Jo's route, Iven's hours, the timber, the meals, Renn and Mikka's watches, the kitchen's pot, the clinic's wash water, the six hundred hours the Hearthholds have spent on a pump belonging to nobody and a room belonging to six strangers. One fund. One column. Then anybody can look at it and see what it costs and what it is worth, and nobody has to keep a private tally of what they are owed."

"Paid to whom?" Dalla asked.

"Split it. The Hearthholds have paid most of it in labor. The crew has paid in labor too. Whatever cannot be paid in labor, we put in the same column and let the room decide what it is for."

"The room cannot decide," Nera said. "The room is nine people and a school corner, and two of those nine are standing in this room right now holding a watch that nobody has paid them for in a year. You are proposing to create a fund in a place with no one appointed to hold it, no one authorized to spend it, and no legal existence at all. Where does it sit? On whose ledger? Under whose seal?"

"Somewhere Dalla can write it down."

"Dalla can write anything down. Writing is not holding." Nera put the dyed cloth down on the board, on top of his column, which was a thing she had never done before and which stopped him dead. "Listen to what you have actually said. You have said that a fund would be a fair way to pay people. Fine. Then notice that I am not one of the people to be paid. I am the air works of a Hearthhold, and the air works are maintained by this neighborhood because the neighborhood breathes it. If you pay me for the air watch, then either the neighborhood stops maintaining its own air and hires an outsider, or the neighborhood maintains its air and is paying twice for a thing it already owns. And if you pay me as the crew's air engineer, then your six people have bought an air engineer, and an air engineer's word starts to sound like a property. I am not a guild fee. I am not your architect's fee. I am not available to be bought by a fund that a fund raised by a corridor does not have the standing to raise."

The cloth sat on his column, which was the most effective argument anybody had made in six weeks.

Marek took it off and set it aside, and sat down, which he had not planned to do.

"Go on," Dalla said. "You are not wrong about the arithmetic. You are wrong about the container."

The argument about meals had been building since the first work period and it came out now, because Dalla had put *meals* in the column and Mikka had walked in to collect the bowls.

"That is my pot," Mikka said, reading the entry upside down from the doorway. "Forty-one strokes. Nineteen days. Do not put my pot in a fund."

"It is a cost," Dalla said. "It belongs in the same column as everything else."

"It belongs in my column, which is the kitchen's, and the kitchen's column is not a debt." She set the bowls down. "I will tell you the difference and then you can argue with it. If I feed a crew because they are working here, that is hospitality, and hospitality can be stopped at any moment by any person in that kitchen without anyone owing anybody anything. If I feed them because they are owed, then I have made myself a creditor of six people who cannot leave, and I would be able to say so out loud, and so could they, and that is not a thing you put on a person who has nowhere else to go. And if it is a wage, then it is wage work and I have a rate and a wage book and I am a creditor, and I am not that either. So it is a meal. Write *meals, given, at the kitchen's cost, and the kitchen has not waived them.*"

"You have fed us nineteen days," Leena said. "You should be able to stop."

"I can stop," Mikka said. "Any cook can stop. That is why it is not a wage. And I am not going to stop, and I want that written down too, because a gift that is not recorded becomes a claim in about four months, and I have watched it happen to two households in this building."

Dalla wrote it exactly as Mikka said it, in Mikka's words, which she had not done for anyone.

The refusal came from Renn, and she made it standing in the doorway of the anteroom with her coat already on.

"You want a sixth watch on the wet seam while the corridor is in use," she said. "You want somebody at the waist, at the channel, and at the clinic return line, and the clinic return is Mikka, and the waist is Jo, and now you want a sixth. I am the door and I am the landing. I will not be the sixth."

The room went very careful. Nobody told her she had already agreed to a watch, because she had not: she had agreed to the door, and the door was a wall, and the sixth watch was a person standing in a wet place for an hour watching stone.

"I will not," Renn said again, more slowly. "I have four people in my rooms and one of them is not walking. If I am here for an hour in the wet, one of them is alone, and I have done that arithmetic before and I did not like the answer the first time either."

"Then the watch is empty," Jo said, before Marek could.

"Then the watch is empty," Jo said again, to make sure. "And what happens with an empty watch?"

"Nothing happens with it," Nera said. "The corridor does not get used. The room empties. Nobody is shortening the shift and nobody is doing the work with a hole in it, because the hole is the whole point of the watch."

"Then the corridor is not usable on the days I cannot stand it," Renn said. "Write that down in your own words, not mine, because I am not a route keeper and I am not speaking for the door tonight."

"Recorded," Dalla said, and wrote: *corridor use depends on six watches. The sixth is unfilled on some days. On those days the corridor is not used. The people who would stand the watch decide whether it is stood, and nobody outside this boundary can decide it for them.*

"And if Marek says the work cannot stop?" Leena asked. "Not as a question. As the thing that will actually happen."

Marek looked at the floor, because she was right to ask it that way.

"I can stop structural work," he said. "I cannot turn my stop into anybody's consent, and I cannot turn anybody's stop into a delay I get to overrule in an hour. If a watch is empty, the corridor is not used, and my part of that is to be somewhere else."

Sefu had been standing at the wheel the entire time, which was his habit, and which meant he had been listening to a fund being invented and dismantled in his own boundary.

"One more thing, and then I will let you have the afternoon," he said. "You have written down nine names and six of them are people who came here from above. Every cost in that column is a cost a person agreed to. If the Hearthholds wanted to bind the six of you to a room, they cannot. I cannot. Jo cannot. The kitchen cannot. There is nobody in this neighborhood with the standing to make a stranger carry a maintenance obligation, and the moment somebody tries, this stops being a place people volunteer to work in and becomes a place people are kept in. Which means every hour any of you puts in here is an hour you are giving, and the record has to keep saying so."

"Then what do we call it?" Marek asked. "A fund that is not a fund. Hours that are given. A ledger that never closes."

"Call it the maintenance obligation," Dalla said, and then stopped, because she had said it before in a document and a document had cost them a week.

They wrote it in the end as two pages, and Dalla wrote them both.

The first page was what the Hearthholds had spent and what the crew had spent, with no money in either column, because there was no money in this building. The Hearthholds: the standing timber, all six lengths and the bundle of lath, released to a project they did not own; Jo's route, three half-days of pump cleaning and one shutter route closed for the season; the boundary workbench's surface; the pump seat, still scored, still without a spare; nineteen days of kitchen provision, recorded as given and not waived; the clinic's wash water carried past a narrowed corridor; Mikka's and Renn's and Dalla's hours, entered as hours.

The crew: sixty-one person-hours of labor, no wage, no claim, no ownership of the pump, the corridor, the room, the plate, or the air. Iven Tallo's instruction, twenty-six hours of it, uncompensated, with a suspended Cartwright witness bond attached, which meant the Hearthholds could not pay him for it without turning local craft into guild work, and would not do that.

The second page was the list of things nobody could pay for from where they were standing: a bronze delivery seat that nobody had released; timber long enough to reset a lintel, which did not exist in the stores; a carpenter's wage for a man the guild would not pay; nineteen days of meals; a sealed liter of verified condensation in a stoppered jar; and a form sitting unsigned in a Marrowgate office that named who answers for a tunnel collapse and that six people had now added a second failure to.

Jo read both pages and put her thumb on her own line.

"Do not soften it," she said. "Say that the route I do all year is shut because of a floor I asked for. Then nobody can tell me later that it was always going to close."

"It was going to close anyway," Mikka said. "That shutter has been failing since the second month."

"That is not the point," Jo said. "The point is that I have to be able to point at something."

The second page ended with a line that Dalla had not been asked to write and wrote anyway, in her own hand, at the bottom, with a space left after it.

*Unresolved and not payable from this room:* the seat, the lintel, the carpenter, the meals, the sealed reserve, the surface record, and the only physically continuous way down, which is restricted and which nobody here may open.

Marek looked at that last item for a long time. The service bypass behind them was continuous stone all the way to the flooded gallery and the failing lower chamber. It was also the only way out that did not pass the stair that repeated, and the stair was corded and named and the record said it stayed corded until a load test on its lower side that no room above it had to pay for.

"So we cannot even leave cleanly," he said. "That is on the list now."

"That is on the list now," Dalla agreed, "and it was on the list before, and the reason it is written down is that you have spent a week building things and not once written it down."

The pump came off the lever at the fourth bell. The corridor emptied through the kitchen line. Two people went out and eleven came in, and Dalla wrote the numbers down, and the afternoon went on being an afternoon.

Oren and Tams put the wedge set back on the board and counted it, because a store that is not counted is a store that gets borrowed from. Nine, and Jo made them count out loud so the number would be a thing a person had heard rather than a thing a person had seen. Pell read the number off the tally and wrote it on his slate, and then asked, reasonably, who checked it on a day when nobody was bored enough to look. Nobody answered, and Dalla wrote *nobody, yet* at the end of the line, which was the most honest thing on either page.
