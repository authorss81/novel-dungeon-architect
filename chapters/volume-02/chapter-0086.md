# Chapter 86 — What the Middle Gate Asks

A courier came down the middle passage on the first morning of the seventeenth week and got as far as the bottom of the nine steps, and the anteroom emptied itself of eleven people in about ninety seconds, because a Hearthhold has learned in fifteen weeks that a stranger on that landing is a thing that happens at the first bell and not at the fifth.

He was not Hesk Ammery. He was a young man of about thirty with a bad cold and a satchel he was clearly sorry about, and Dalla Sen took it off him at the top of the steps and did not open it, and said so out loud, and he said that was correct and he had been told to say that.

"Two things in it," he said. "**Two, and they are not from the same person, and the woman at the counter was very particular about that.** The first is on the back of a store's issue slip and it is in a courier's hand and it has her name at the bottom under it, which she said she wanted. The second is on a bit of painted board about the length of my hand and it is in a different hand and there is no name on it, and she said there would not be one, and I asked her twice."

"Read the first one," said Dalla Sen, and gave it to her and not to the room, which is not a thing she has ever done, and Tams Quill noticed and said nothing about and entered it in the column with no heading afterwards.

The room heard it at the fourth bell, and it is in the book in Talla Roke's words, and she wrote it on the back of a store's issue slip on a Saturday at a counter, and she had folded the paper five times by the time she gave it to the courier, and it is the reason a boundary is now a thing that issues notices:

---

**To the Hearthhold boundary. From Talla Roke, courier, of the middle gate's second gate, and this is my own hand and my own question and not the office's.**

"Your notice reached us. It is four days up the road and it took the clerk a while to open it and then she read it twice to a man at the counter, and I was in the room for the second one.

"It says what it says. It says the middle gate should stop holding the timber and put the water-lock in front of the people behind it.

"**The middle gate has not done that, cannot do it this season, and is not going to pretend to anybody at that counter that it can.** A water-lock is a sill, a bed, a wedge of hardwood and two hands a day for four days. The sill is your four hundred paces. Your notice has come the hold off that timber and the timber is the middle's now and it is the middle's problem, and the middle gate has no carpenter with a bond, and the trade's own rule will not let one of ours take a chisel to it, and there are four households behind that lock who have been carrying water up four sets of steps since the cold closed, and two of them are my brothers.

"So, and this is the question, and I have had it since Friday morning and this is the first time I have written it down: **what is this boundary's answer, in a form that a person at a counter can be given?**

"And if the answer is that nothing comes, and I am not saying it is, then somebody has to say it out loud, in a room, in front of those four households, because they are owed that and they are not going to be told it by a page that a stranger read twice at a counter.

"**Is a page like that any use to four households?** I am asking because I have read your notice three times and it is the first document I have ever handled in nine years that could not be used against anybody, and that is not a compliment, that is a fault, and here is the fault: it is a refusal, and a refusal is a thing a person can put down and walk away from, and a lock does not stay dry because a courier read a piece of paper twice.

**And one more, because I am a courier and this is the only part of it that is mine.** Four households. Two brothers, their sister, and one house that is not family and only carries. The three of the first sort live at the top of four sets of steps and the fourth is at the bottom, and the three of the first sort are the ones who will be asked and the fourth is the one that will not be told anything. Write the answer so that the bottom of those steps is in it, and not only the top."

---

Nobody in the anteroom said anything for a while.

Then Tams Quill said: "How long has it been in the air."

Asked how long it had been in the air, the courier said, "I was not told, and I asked at the top of the road, and the man who gave me the satchel said he did not know either and that the woman at the counter had not written it down."

"Right," said Tams Quill. "Then that is the first finding of the sixteenth week and it is mine and I am going to say it before the second one and nobody is to talk over me.

"**There is a page on a road with no date on it and no hour on it, and it was put there on purpose, and the reason it was put there on purpose is the best sentence in this boundary's book, and the sentence is: no date is attached to this, because a date would be a promise.**

"And here is what that sentence has done. A boundary four days up a road has received a notice, and has written back to it, and the reply is on the top of nine steps, and **nobody in this building knows how old it is.** Not the clerk, who has the day it went and not the hour the man read it. Not the keeper. Not me. And a question you cannot date is a question you cannot answer in time, and the woman who wrote it had to sit with it from the morning it was read until she could work out what to ask her, and a day and a night of somebody's winter is not a thing a boundary gets to spend by accident, out of a sentence about a date being a promise."

"I did that," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel.

"I know you did that," said Tams Quill, "and I am not saying it was wrong, and I am saying it cost something and nobody knew what until this morning, and I would like it entered as the cost of the best sentence in the book, because in four years somebody is going to read the release and admire it, and it should be in the same file as the day and a night."

"Entered," said Dalla Sen, "in the count's words, and read out."

---

"Then read the second one," said Leena Orr, from the litter, "and read it before the room starts answering the first one, because the room is going to want to answer the first one and the second one is the one we are not going to be able to answer."

It was a piece of painted board, soft at the grain, about the length of a hand, and it had been sawn off something, and there was a line of black paint along one edge of it that had not been there when it was part of a bigger board, and on the flat of it, in a slow careful hand that was not used to writing, five words and a question mark:

**"Who will tell the four houses?"**

"There is no name on it," said Dalla Sen. "The courier says the woman at the counter was told there would not be one."

"Then it is from Corin Bexley," said Wen Pask, "because I asked about the hold at the counter on my second day and it was the water-lock keeper who had put the hold on, and the woman at the counter only reads out what the office gives her and the office has not put out a sentence like that in nine years, and a man who has been on a dry lock since before I was born has."

"And nobody in this building has met him," said Marek Venn.

"No," said Wen Pask. "Nobody has."

---

Five words sat in the room for about four minutes and the room did what this room does, which is to try every cheap answer and watch all of them fail.

Dalla Sen said that she could write it, and then said, before anybody had answered her, that she could write it and that it would be a fourth sheet in a satchel going up a road to a lock-house, and that a lock-house is eleven feet deep with a bed on one side, and that a sheet of paper does not stand in a doorway.

Renn Vale said, "The crew's hours cannot go down that road, and they did not ask, and I am putting the no in before anybody makes an argument about it. The rule has been in this building since the tenth week and it is: hours given by five people who are going to leave are not to be spent on a work in another Hearthhold. **The middle gate is another Hearthhold.** And the second reason is that I have watched this boundary spend a morning on a woman's own door and it took eleven minutes and it was the best thing anybody has done here, and if we send four strong backs up a road to stand in a room at a lock, then in four years that is a crew of ours, and a crew is a crew, and a route that keeps arriving is a route somebody owns."

Renn Vale said *not yet* from the landing, quietly, to nobody in particular, and the room laughed for half a second and stopped, because it was the shape of a sentence that had come out of that room a month before and had been entered, and a shape everybody recognises is worse than a joke.

Then Sefu Aran came off the wheel, which he does perhaps four times in fifteen weeks, and stood in the middle of the floor.

"**It is him.** The keeper of the middle gate's water-lock is the man who has to stand in front of those four households and say the lock is not coming, and this boundary cannot do it for him at four days' remove, and the notice cannot do it, and the courier who brought it cannot do it, and a page cannot do it, and **I have been the keeper of this gate for the whole of the time and I am telling the room that a page is the cheap half of saying a thing and this boundary has just discovered what the other half costs and it is not four hundred paces of hardwood.**

"That is what the second one is. He is not asking us for timber. He is asking us whether a document is a thing a person can stand on."

"Then the answer is no," said Bel Ordry, "and it is the first straight no anybody in this room has had for six weeks, and I would like somebody to say the rest of it, because a no with a reason in it is worth four of them without."

"The answer is no," Sefu agreed, "**and the answer to the first one is that a page is worth something and not enough, and that is a different answer and it is the harder one, and it is the one that has to go up the road.**

"Which means this boundary now owes a second document. And I want the room to understand what has just happened in the last three minutes, because nobody else in this building is going to say it plainly and I am the man who sent the first one.

"**The first one said what we will not do. The second one has to say what we will do. And a thing that says what a boundary will do is half of a charter.** And there is no charter. There is a bench and a cleared table in a kitchen with three columns on it and one of them empty, and it has been two days, and if I write the second half of a charter this week then a keeper of a gate has built a charter in a cold month on the back of a butcher's sheet, and in a year that is the sentence in the file, and the sentence will not be *the boundary was built by nine people doing nine kinds of work correctly over fifteen weeks.* It will be *the keeper issued it in the sixteenth week.*"

"Then do not issue it," said Nera Oduya, from three steps, and everybody turned, because it was the air engineer and she had not been asked.

"I am not saying do not answer," she said. "**I am saying do not answer the second one with a list.** Answer it with the truth, which is that a boundary of eleven people with twelve days of water and a closed corridor has not got a list of what it will do, and it has got one work, and the work is finished and in a book, and everything else this boundary is doing is eleven people in a cold month doing what comes.

"And the difference between that and a list is the difference between *what we will do* and *what we are doing this week*, and **a list of what a boundary will do is a list of obligations, and an obligation on paper is an obligation an office can call in**, and in three months there will be a bad week and somebody forty days away will read that page and say *you undertook.*"

"Then what goes up the road," said Iven Tallo.

"What goes up the road is the truth and nothing else, and it is short, and it is not a list, and I will say what it is and the keeper can decide whether to send it." Nera came down one step, which she has done four times in fifteen weeks. "**This boundary has one named work and it is finished. It has no timber and no carpenter and no money. It has said what it will not do and it stands by it. It is not coming. And it is not asking anything of you, and it would rather have the question asked out loud in a room than have it answered on paper by strangers.**"

"That is six sentences," said Sefu Aran, "and it is the most careful thing anybody in this building has said since I came up a shaft, and it is not a list, and it is not a charter, and it does not say what we will do."

"It says what we are doing," said Tams Quill, "**and the difference between those is a *will*, and I do not know a single thing in this Hearthhold's fifteen weeks that has ever been written with a will in it, and I would like the clerk to write down that a man with a board has noticed.**

"And I would like it written as well that the second page will be as dateless as the first one, and that is going to cost a day and a night of somebody's winter, and that I have said so already today and I am not going to say it twice, and that I am the count and the count does not repeat."

---

Pell Roke had been on the bench at the top of the nine steps with his crutch across his knees and his ear against the stone of the step above the stair head, the way he has been every morning since the eighth week, and he had read the whole of a courier's question that named his sister in three hands and had not moved, and nobody in the room had looked at him, and that was correct, and at the fifth bell he said three words and then stopped.

"Second gate's families," he said. And then he put his ear back against the stone and stopped, and Dalla Sen entered what he had said in her own hand without asking him for any more, because he had not been asked for any more, and nobody in the room asked him for any more, and that was entered too, and the four households stayed in the courier's hand where she had put them, and nobody in that room asked a boy of fourteen to describe his own family to a stranger's letter.

Marek Venn stood at the top of the nine steps with the courier's question in the clerk's ledger open on the bench and worked out the thing that had been arriving for six days and had now arrived.

The release was right. He had known it was right on the Friday and had argued against it for a day, and the reason he had argued against it was that a promise costs more than a truth, and a release that says *we will not come* is a truth, and a truth costs a plank, and he had been perfectly willing to pay a plank.

What he had not cost was the second thing, and the second thing was a room with four households in it, and a man in it who has been on that dry lock since before the collapse and who wrote five words on a piece of painted board and sent them four days down a road to strangers, and the five words were not about timber, and they had arrived in a boundary that could not answer them.

And the worst of it — and this is the part he did not say to anybody, and did not put on a slate, and put nowhere at all — was that **he would have got this wrong if he had been allowed to write the notice himself.** He would have written an answer into it. He would have written the crew's hours and the middle passage and a spring he was not entitled to offer, because a man who has to leave a place is a man who wants to be forgiven by the people in it, and there is no sentence a man can put on a page that comes out of that. Which is why the keeper of a gate is the man who writes a release and not an engineer, and why Sefu Aran had said *a date would be a promise* and meant it, and why a clerk's page was the dangerous thing about her and not the release.

And the other half of it, which he was not going to think about on a stair head, was that he could not have gone. Four days of wet road, a satchel he would have had to change hands every two hundred yards, a bend a foot wide over a shaft nobody has sounded, a second pair of hands and a man of this boundary turning a courier into a party. He could not have stood in the lock-house. He could not have said the five words back. And the man who could not go was the man in the room who most wanted to, and the reason he could not go was a hand that would not close and a corridor two foot eight wide over eleven feet of shaft, and there is no version of this Hearthhold in which that is anything other than what it is.

At the sixth bell Sefu Aran sent the courier up the middle passage with six sentences, and the six sentences were read out at the threshold by the keeper, and by the clerk, and by the courier, and the courier repeated them back from memory before he left, and Dalla Sen entered that he had repeated them back correctly and that this was the first time in a month that anybody in this building had checked a thing on the road by making a stranger say it again.

Then Mikka Hale came in from the kitchen with her sleeves up and said that a cleared table was down to two days of paper and that a butcher's sheet had been finished and started again and was now in four parts, two in the kitchen and two in a school store under a shelf, and that they were all the same document and the room was going to have to say so out loud, and that she was not asking anybody's permission, and that the schedule was starting again in her kitchen at the first bell in the morning in her own hand, and that the third column was still empty and was going to stay empty until somebody in this boundary could say what the work was.

Nobody in the anteroom argued with her about it, and the pot was twelve days, and the corridor was shut, and had been since the Saturday night, and the nine children were on the anteroom's side of it where they had been since the Wednesday of the last week, and the stair was corded and shut and stayed shut, and a person was still under that lip.
