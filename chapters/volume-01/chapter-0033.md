# Chapter 33 — The Lower Side of the Stair

The work period was named on the board Jo held, which meant that Jo had written it, which meant it had a start, a stop, a reason, and the number of people who could be in it, and that two of those four things had been argued about before the ink was dry.

*Attended look at the fixed first landing of the returning stair, near side only, from the service bend. No crossing. No litter past the bend. Lamp stand wedge-driven. The stop is the stair cord, the cloth, or a watcher's word.*

"The line is mine," Nera said, before anybody asked for one. "The stair-side grille is opened and shut by a draw cord, and the draw cord is eight feet, and it is on my board, and I am releasing it for the period. Do not use the hand line. The hand line is on a school-door head, and that head is cracked, and I am not having a sounding take the only thing holding it."

"I had the hand line in my hand," Tams said.

"Put it back on the belay," Nera said, and he did.

Leena had put the second line in and had explained it to four people who had not asked.

"The litter goes to the service bend and no further," she said, "because the last eight feet are three steps and a lip and a litter does not go down steps, and because if I am on that lip I am the reason a stair is corded. So I am at the bend, and I have the slate, and I have the four minutes." She shifted on the frame. "Four minutes is how long it takes four people to get a person off this side on a wet floor. There is no person on this side. The four minutes are still the clock. Whatever else we do down there, we do it so that it can be undone in four minutes, because the day this works is the day somebody in this building is on a litter on this side, and I would like them to be able to leave."

Nobody argued, because there was nothing to argue with. That was what a condition written by the person who would have to live in it looked like.

Pell was not going, and made no attempt to make a case, and was given the most important job in the period.

"You are the dead window," Jo said, when he had asked, twice, whether there was anything he could do from the floor.

"I have a bench and a slate and a way of counting that does not need two hands," he said. "Nobody in this building can say when the stair will turn. Nera cannot. Renn cannot. I have been lying on this bench for six weeks with my ear against the stone of the step above it, and the wheel comes through the plinth, not the floor, and I can hear it coming about four seconds before the floor does anything." He put his crutch across his knees. "You are going to want to know when to start and when to stop, and if you ask Nera she will tell you she does not know, which is true, and if you ask Renn she will tell you the same, which is also true. So ask the stone. I am the stone's man. Call the interval and I will call the next one."

Nera set the air side before anybody went down, and her conditions were not conditions of caution. They were the shape of the work.

"The grille on the stair side stays shut," she said. "Not opened to give you light, not opened to give you air, not opened because it is more comfortable eleven feet down. It is the only thing between the stair's pressure and the clinic return, and I am not spending it on a look."

"Then we work by lamp," Marek said.

"You work by lamp and by the cloth, and the cloth is on a string and it is on my end and not yours, and when it moves you are all out, and I do not care what you have seen or half seen. I have watched this room for six weeks and the one thing I can tell you is that a turn does not announce itself to be polite about where your feet are." She lifted the dyed cloth off its hook and let it hang. "The dead window is not a number. I have no interval to give you. Take what you are given and come back."

Sefu walked the party to the service bend himself, which he did not usually do, and said his piece at the top of the three steps down.

"The cord is on the near approach and it stays on. If any of you put a hand past that line for any reason, including to point, the period is over and I have not been told and I do not want to know. The bypass hatch stays latched, and if the hatch is latched when the turn comes, that is correct, and Iven, take your hand off it."

"I have taken my hand off it," Iven said, without moving his hand, and then took it off.

They went down: Oren first, because Oren went first everywhere, then Jo with the lamp and the wedge, then Tams with the draw cord, then Marek, and Iven last because Iven read the mechanism before he stood in front of it. The litter came down separately with Mikka and Oren, who had come back up for it, and set down on the level stone at the bend with Leena propped against the wall and the slate on her knees.

From the bend you could see the cord, and past the cord the fixed first landing, and on the far side of the throat the lower run of the slab with its stone lip and its rail, and under the near wall's base, in the angle where the near wall met the throat, a block of dressed stone about the size of a bread loaf with a hollow cut into the top of it.

They were ten feet from it and they had a lamp and they had about as long as the bench allowed.

"Left," Jo said. "The lamp goes left of the throat, on the step, not in the throat. In the throat it is a stone with a light in it and I cannot see the bottom of anything."

Marek put his left hand on the fixed rail at the head of the three steps, because it was the thing he did, and the rail gave him nothing at all. Not a direction, not a warning, not a sense of where the force was going. He had written *null* four times since the twenty-first day and he wrote it a fifth time on the corner of the slate Jo handed him without being asked, and Jo took the slate back and read it and put it in her pocket, which was the most useful thing that had happened to any of his readings in two months.

"Cloth has not moved," Nera called down the string. "Go."

They went in. Jo set the lamp stand on the step with a wedge under the near leg and a wedge under the far and packed the far one home with the heel of her left hand, and the light came down into the hollow.

It was a bearing hollow, not a seat. Marek had expected a seat, because the guild's word was *receiver* and a receiver that receives a landing is a place a landing comes to rest on, and what was in front of him was a shape that had been made to take a load sideways rather than a load down.

And the inside of the hollow was not one wear. It was two.

Down in the bottom of the hollow, rounded and deep and polished to a shine you could see the lamp in, was a groove the width of two fingers, running side to side, the shape of something that had been pulled through that hollow ten thousand times in one direction. That was old. That was old enough that the stone around it had gone the colour of a wet pipe.

Above it, in the front wall of the hollow, was a bright band about four inches high, sharp-edged, where something had been sliding up and down hard enough to take the skin off the stone. The band was the same colour as the block around it, which is the colour of a thing that has never once been wet. The groove was a hundred years of use. The band was not.

Iven got down on the near side of the cord, which is where the cord put him, and read the band off the lamp's light for a long time without touching anything and without putting a hand past a line that Sefu had drawn at the top of three steps.

"The guild's drawing calls this a receiver," he said. "A receiver takes the landing. But a landing does not rub a landing socket four inches up the front wall. A landing comes down onto a top surface and it stops. This is a rope."

"A rope," Marek said.

"A line. Look at the groove underneath it — that is a running block, worn by a line, and it is the true part of the thing, and it is the part that is a hundred years old." Iven sat back on his heel. "The lower side of this stair was never a landing that came back. It was a line on the other side that the counterweight pulled, so that the upper side and the lower side did not have to take the same return at the same moment. The drawing says *receiver* because whoever wrote the drawing was reading a guild word into a shape. The shape is a line-block."

"Then the guild entry," Marek said slowly. "*Lower receiver — out, sections, to works, per clr 41.*"

"Three sections, to works," Iven agreed. "And the works the guild meant was a mason's yard, because that is what a guild removal means, and Renn's account is that the works was a school. Both can be true and neither of them tells us a thing, except this." He nodded at the hollow. "This tells us the counter-line was taken out in three sections and there has been no line in it since. The stair has been asking for a counter-line for however long the counter-line has been gone, and the only thing standing in the gap is a wedge-shaped piece of a third section, put in by a woman with stone chippings, and it is not a bearing. It is a *stop*."

He looked up at the bright band.

"And it has been doing the work of a stop for forty years, and the band is four inches high, which means the line it has been stopping has moved a great deal more in the last while than it did in the first ten years." He sat there a second longer than a man who had finished talking needs to. "That bright band is not old. Somebody has been using it again, recently, and not gently."

The turn came.

It came the way it always came, which was not from the direction of anything. Nera's voice went up the string before the floor did anything, and it was two words, and it was the whole of the drill.

"Out. Now."

Oren was the nearest and he was the largest and he went up the three steps at once with his hands out and his body across the narrowest part, and Tams came behind him with the lamp. Jo pulled the stand by its leg and the stand skidded a foot on the step and the near wedge came out of its packing and went over the lip of the step and into the throat, and everybody heard it go, and nobody went after it, and the party was up the three steps and out past the cord in fifty-one seconds, which was faster than the last time and slower than the plan.

Dalla rang once for the corridor. Mikka rang the clinic once. Sefu had the wheel. Leena's bearers had the litter moving before the ringing stopped, and Leena said the number out loud from the frame — *forty seconds to the bend, and the litter is on level stone, and the four minutes are for a floor, not for steps* — and it was four minutes and six seconds from the bell to the litter being back through the service bend into the anteroom, and Dalla wrote it down.

The wheel turned twice under the floor. The cord held. The bypass hatch stayed latched and Iven's hand stayed off it. Nothing crossed the slab, and nothing crossed the cord, and the anteroom cleared to the outer step and the litter went first and came back first, and the whole thing took four minutes and six seconds, and then the cloth hung still again, and the anteroom got its ten back, and Nera came down the three steps after the party with the cloth in her hand, and one of the watches had been empty the whole time, because Renn was in the anteroom holding the landing and could not be at the waist as well.

"The dead window is not a window," Nera said. "It is a gap, and the gap is not the same size twice, and you had nine minutes, and I will not promise you nine again."

"Nine was plenty," Tams said, and then stopped, because the sentence had come out of him automatically, the way a sentence comes out when you have spent four hours being the man who is given things.

"Write it down," Pell called from the bench upstairs, without raising his voice. "Nine was plenty *this time*. Do not write nine is plenty. Write what you actually had and that you do not know what you get."

Marek wrote it down, because Pell was right, and because it was the fourth thing in two months that the fourteen-year-old had said that the record needed.

When they went back down at the next gap, which was six minutes long, Jo's lamp stand would not take a wedge at the near leg. She tried the far leg twice. The hole the wedge had been in had opened into a crack in the step, and the leg was now standing in the crack on its own, and it stood, and it stood by a thing Jo did not trust and would not say so in front of anybody.

"Two wedges in the stand and I have one," she said. "And the one I have is the one I would spend."

Iven looked at the far leg. "You are not driving a second wedge into a crack that is already open. Take it up. Set the stand on the tread instead of the step, where the stone is flat, and take the weight off the far leg with your knee. If it tips, it tips onto your knee and not into the throat."

"That is not what the plan says."

"The plan says the lamp stand is wedge-driven, and I wrote that, and I am telling you the plan is wrong at the near leg." He did not soften it. "Write the change on the board, Jo, in your hand, because it is a change to a thing people stand next to."

Jo wrote the change. It took her four minutes to write four words and she wrote them larger than the rest of the line, which is what she did when she wanted a thing to be hard to misread.

At the end of the period the party came up, and the anteroom got its ten back, and the corridor had been shut for thirty-five minutes out of a work period of fifty, and Tams counted the wedges on the tally board out loud, because he always counted them out loud, and Jo made him count out loud even after the number had stopped changing.

"Eight," he said.

"Eight," Dalla said, and wrote it, and wrote next to it, in her own hand, *one lost down the stair throat on the day of the attended look. Cause: the turn, and the stand moving on a packed wedge. Not anybody's fault. Not anybody's claim.*

"Write the last part twice," Iven said. "Once is for the person who is not to blame and once is for the guild, who will want to know whether a stranger dropped it."

So it was written twice, and the eight went on the board, and the party came in from the service bend, and behind them under the near wall a bright four-inch band of unweathered stone held a piece of a wash door against nothing, and the groove underneath it was a hundred years old and had nothing in it, and nobody had any idea who had been pulling a line through that hollow lately, or why, or for what.
