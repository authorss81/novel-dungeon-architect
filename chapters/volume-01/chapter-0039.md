# Chapter 39 — The Plan, Named and Undone

Marek wrote it on his own slate, at night, in his left hand, and brought it to the anteroom in the morning and read it out loud standing up, and by the time he had finished, four words of it were his and about two hundred were not.

That was the practice now, and the practice had a reason: Jo held the board, so Jo's hand was the hand any change to a room people use went into, and a plan for a room nobody lived in was still a plan about rooms, and the only honest way to have it read was to bring it in unpolished and let six people take it apart in front of the people who would live in it.

"Sit down," Jo said, before he had read a line. "All of you. He is going to take twenty minutes and I am going to stop him eleven times, and I want the interruptions in the order they happen, not afterwards."

He read the first line and she let it stand.

*The stair's lower side asked for a line a hundred years ago and has had a stone driven into the line-block to stop it ever since. The aim of this work is to give the lower side a line that is not stopped, and to give that line something on this side of the throat to be made fast to, so that when the slab repeats its return it meets a condition that was built rather than remembered.*

"That is a sentence I cannot improve," Leena said, "and I would like it read again at the end, because it is the only line in here that anybody could repeat to a stranger."

The second line: the work is in three steps, and no step is begun until the step before it is finished and written down, and a step that is not finished is recorded as not finished and not as nearly done.

"Nearly done is how the gallery died," Ilsa said.

"Nearly done is how the gallery died," Jo agreed, and wrote it under the line in her own hand, and it was Jo's writing and not Marek's, and Marek read over her shoulder and did not object, because it was hers.

The third line was the school corner floor.

*Step one is the mouth, said Iven, and Jo wrote it down. It is opened and traced, It is opened and traced, and the void under the far end is cleaned to a stated depth, and it is given either a fall to the channel or a shut, and the fall is not cut through the wash room floor without the wash room asked first. This is not optional and it is not a preliminary. I will not have a single piece of work done at that stair until the school corner stops being a mouth, because nine children sit over it and I will not spend your children's air to find out about a rope.*

The objection came from Nera, and it was about a single word, and it took the better part of an hour.

"Stops being a mouth," she said. "That is a promise about a floor I have not seen traced. You are describing an outcome in the step that is supposed to produce it. Say what you will do, and say what you will know when it is done, and do not say what the room will become."

Marek tried three ways of putting it. The third worked.

*Step one is the mouth, said Marek. Nera is right and I was writing the answer into the question. It is opened. The void under the far end is traced to whatever it is at, and cleaned to a depth the room agrees, and it is then given either a fall to the channel or a shut. I do not promise that the school corner stops being a mouth. I promise that it stops being a mouth nobody has looked at, and that the person who keeps the floor is told what it turned out to be in her own language before anybody is allowed to work at the stair.*

"She is going to ask for the words *in her own language* to stay in," Ilsa said, and Jo wrote it in, and Ilsa read it back, and that was the end of the third line.

The fourth line was the sight, and Iven took it apart.

"You are asking the wash room floor," he said.

"I am asking to know what is under the school corner's far end, and the school corner's far end is over the wash room's floor, and the only way to know what is under the wash room's floor is to open the wash room's floor."

"Then write that sentence and read it to the room and see how it sounds in the room."

Marek looked at the room.

"I am asking to open the floor of the wash room where a woman and her mother sleep," he said, "and I am asking because the wash room's floor is the only thing between us and the far side of that stair, and I am asking on a day when the stair project has cost two fingers of Tams Quill and one day of the school and one of your own floor, and I am asking in a room where the woman who sleeps over that floor lifts a drain cover for us every morning and has never once been asked a question by a stranger."

Renn was standing at the back with her coat on and she did not have to be asked.

"That is step two," she said, "and you have just read it out, and the answer is the answer I gave you on Thursday and it has not changed because you said it nicely. No." She said it again, once, for the record, in the flat voice. "No. You will not open my mother's wash room floor. Not today, not next month, and not with a good reason. And if you want to know what is under it, then you will find out the way people in this building find out what is under a floor, which is by asking a person at her own door, and I have told you that you may not, and I meant the woman as well as the room."

"Then step two is not available," Marek said.

"Then step two is not available," Renn agreed, "and it is not struck, and I want the difference marked, because struck means we tried it and it failed, and it has not been tried. It has been refused by the person whose it is. Those are two different things and I am the one who tells the difference."

Jo wrote it: *step two is not available. Refused. Not attempted.* And she read it back to Renn, and Renn said it was right, and Dalla copied it.

The fifth line was the counter, and Marek read it and Nera stopped him in the middle of the first sentence.

"No," she said. "Not because it is wrong. Because it is third on a page whose first two steps cannot both be had, and because when I read a third step after a second step that has been refused, I read it as a reason for somebody to reconsider the second. I know that is not what you mean. I would still ask you to take it off the page, and to write underneath that it exists, and that it has no material, and that its material cannot be named from this room."

"You said the same thing in the thirty-sixth day."

"I said it in the thirty-sixth day about a bench on a lip. I am saying it now about a plan, which is a different thing and worse." She took the cloth off its hook and held it, and did not put it down. "You have written a page with one step that can be attempted and one that cannot and one that has nothing to put in it, and every person in this room is going to read the third step as the reason to argue about the second, because that is what pages do. Take it off. Write *the third step is unwritten and there is no material for it in this building*, and let it be the last line in the plainest possible words, and then somebody else can be angry at me for that instead of at you."

He took it off. Jo wrote the last line, and it was the shortest thing on the page, and it did not have a name on it.

The sixth line was the one he was attached to, and he read it as a favour to the room and it was a favour to himself, and Leena Orr stopped him eight words into it.

"Leave it," she said, not unkindly. "Say the rest of it. I want to hear the whole of it before I take it, because I have a feeling about it and I would rather be exact than right."

He said the rest of it, and the rest of it was this: that when the lower side has a line, and the line has somewhere on this side to be made fast to, then the stair will be tried with a walking load, because a walking person is the only thing that repeats the return, and the only honest test of a return is a person, and that the test would be one person and four bearers on the near side, at a named hour, on a day the room has agreed, with the school not running and the kitchen line clear.

"And when it works," he said, "then the stair is two exits instead of one, and it is a route, and I have said in writing that we would not call it a route before it was one, and this is the line where I would like to be allowed to write it."

"No," Leena said.

Marek stopped.

"No," she said, "and I am going to be exact, because you will think I am frightened and I am not. I am the person on the litter. I have been on the litter since the first week and I have been the four minutes in the record since the thirtieth, and you have just written a page whose last sentence puts me on it, and you have written it in the correct voice, which is the voice of a man who has thought about it and decided the cost is fair."

"That is not—"

"It is exactly that. And here is why I am striking it, and it is not courage and it is not principle. It is arithmetic. The moment that sentence is on a page, then the test has a person in it, and the person in it is whoever cannot refuse, and in this building that is me, because I am on a litter, and everybody in this room knows it, including you, and you wrote it anyway because the sentence is better with me in it. Every plan I have ever read with a name on the end of it has my name on the end of it. So I am striking it, and I would like it struck in my words and not yours." She looked at Jo. "And write underneath what the reason is, so that the next person who wants it has to read it."

Jo wrote: *the clause that closes this plan with a person on the slab is struck at the request of Leena Orr, and the reason is that a plan with a person at the end of it has already chosen the person, and the person chosen is the one who cannot say no, and that is not a test and it is not fairness.*

"That is the best sentence anybody has written on this project," Jo said, "and I wrote it, and I am going to be insufferable about it for a week."

Sefu had one more, and it was a single word, and he had been holding it since the thirty-first day.

"You keep calling it the way out," he said. "Marek, you have used that phrase in three meetings and twice to me, and it is a promise made by the person who says it, to people who will hold you to it, and you do not have it to give. You do not get to name it before it is one. Call it the stair. Call it the mechanism. Call it the thing in my mother's wall if you like. Do not call it a way out in a building that has one exit and a pump."

"Struck," Jo said, and put a line through it in her own hand, and Marek watched her do it and did not say stop, and the reason he did not say stop was that Sefu was right, and he had known it since the thirty-first day, and had said the words anyway because the words were more useful to him than the truth was to everyone.

And then there was the tally, and Tams did it, and it was the end of the morning, and it was the worst ten minutes of it.

"Step one," he said. "The mouth. Two days' opening, three days' tracing, a day to decide, and a day to fit whatever it turns out to need, and a day for the wash room to be told. Call it seven days, and name that three of the seven are days the school is shut, which are now days nobody runs the school, which are not days, which are Ilsa's days."

"Say it as days and not as free," Ilsa said.

"Seven days, and on three of them Ilsa Norr is losing school." He wrote it, and then stopped with the chalk up, because the next column was the one he had been carrying in his head all week.

"Crew hours," he said. "The six of us. Not Jo. Jo is the frame and the wedges and the record, and Sefu said in front of nine people that the six of us are the six of us, so Jo goes in the other column and Jo's own people can decide what Jo's hours are."

"Write it that way," Jo said.

"Marek on the survey, twelve. Oren on the bearing, eighteen, and the bearing is the whole of it. Iven on the only carpentry in this building, twelve. Tams on the count and not on the floor, six, and his hand is not part of a labor column. Leena and Pell give calls and timings and are written in the record as calls and timings, which is what they are and not hours. That is forty-eight, and nobody has any right to one of them, and all of them are given, and none of them is owed to anybody."

"And ours," Dalla said.

"Theirs. Ilsa, seven days at a day each, and three of them school. The wash rooms, eleven cover checks at a quarter of an hour, call it three, and that is the smallest number in this column and the most certain. Mikka, eleven pot interruptions at half an hour, call it five and a half, and she will not have it rounded and I have not rounded it. Renn, four door hours on the days her door is watched, offered and not charged for, because she is not paid. Dalla, nine hours of record, which is a cost and not a duty and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Jo Verrick, three and a half half-days out of a shutter route that is two weeks late and is a route that pays her, which is twenty-one hours and is the largest number on this board and it is hers and she is not in the crew." He looked up. "Forty-nine and a half. There it is."

"There is what," Marek said.

"There is the finding." Tams had the board turned round, because he always turned the board round. "Forty-eight hours on our side of it and just under fifty on the other, and I have been doing this arithmetic since the first week, and I have never once had a page where the two numbers were within an hour of each other, and I have spent seven weeks telling myself the crew's labour was the free thing. It is not the free thing. It is the half of it that everybody in the room can see. The other half is a woman lifting a cover at the second bell eleven times, and a school that does not run, and a shutter two weeks late, and a cook whose pot keeps being interrupted, and none of that is ours to spend, and all of it is being asked for by a plan with no material in it and a second step a woman has refused."

"It is not a fund," Nera said.

"It is worse than a fund," Tams said, "because a fund has a holder and this has a page, and the page has a step three on it that has nothing to put in it. So here is the tally, and the tally is that the crew can carry forty-eight hours, and the crew cannot carry the other fifty, and if the crew says yes to this then the crew has said yes to somebody else's month."

Marek looked at it for a while. He had wanted a plan he could attempt. He had got one he could attempt and one he could not, in that order, in his own hand, and the tally at the bottom of it said that attempting the first was a decision for eight people and not for six.

"Then it is eight," he said.

"No," Jo said, to the slate, not to him. "It is eight people who have been asked, and one of them has already said no, and the asking is not the deciding. Put it as *asked*, Marek. Do not put it as *agreed* and do not put it as *eight*. I have watched you do that three times this month."

He did not argue.

Jo read the whole page back, from the top, in her own hand, and read it the way Sefu read a boundary paragraph, in the order a person leaving would find it. She read the mouth. She read the refusal. She read the last line with nothing in it. She read the two struck clauses, in the words of the people who had struck them. She read the tally. And when she had finished she put the board down on the bench in front of him, and he read it, and every word of it was in a hand that was not his, and there was not one of the two hundred that he could have changed.
