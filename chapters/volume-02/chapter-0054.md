# Chapter 54 — A Shore Under the Lip

Five foot nine is not a post and Iven Tallo said so for about nine minutes before he let anybody cut it.

"You have all been calling it a post," he said, standing on the common landing with the length on the floor and his thumb moving along it the way a man's thumb moves along a thing he is reading. "It is not a post. A post stands up. This thing cannot stand up, because there is nowhere in this building for it to stand up *to*. There is no floor under the far end of that stone. There is eleven feet of shaft and then there is a bottom that nobody in this boundary has ever seen, and a man went down it, and we are not going down it this month or any month that ends before the weather turns.

"So it is not a post. It is a shore. And a shore is a different animal from a post, and the difference is that a shore is only as good as the packing at its head, and a post is only as good as what is under it, and we have neither of those and we have got one of the other thing, and I would rather have the shore, because a shore I can cut and pack in a fortnight and a post I cannot have at all before the frost."

"Then say what it is going to be," said Jo Verrick, "and I will measure it after, and I will measure it now as well, and the difference is the number this work produced."

"Glory be," said Iven. "A woman with a before and an after."

---

The day's board was written up in the anteroom at the first bell and Dalla Sen read it back, and it had the six watches on it, and it had the day's terms on it, and it had four things on it that had not been on a board in that room before.

A day the school does not run. The corridor shut. The waist not a post, said out loud by Renn Vale, and Jo Verrick said it after her, because Renn had made a rule out of it in the ninth week and rules that are said once become rules that are said twice.

The bearer at the near lip, Isel Ferrow, named, and the reason he was not on the plinth that day, which was that a man who is doing two things in a stair is doing neither, and that had been Sefu Aran's sentence since the fifth time that bell had been given to somebody.

The plinth read by Ruk Sella, and the string by the woman from the wash rooms, and the threshold bell by a fourth person, and the finding of that fourth person took six minutes and two refusals, which is how it works and which nobody in that room apologises for any more.

"There is nobody else," Dalla said, after the second refusal. "Isel is at the lip. Ruk is on the plinth. The woman is on the string. The air engineer is at the valve and the keeper is at the wheel and I have the bell relay and I cannot be two places, and the six of you are not on this rota and cannot be on it, and I have said both of those things out loud before and I am saying them again so that the person I am about to ask does not spend a day wondering."

"Mikka," said Sefu Aran. "No. You are at the clinic return and I will not have you, because the clinic return is the one watch in this building that has to be answered by somebody who is not doing anything else, and you have a flue wall."

"I have been asked," said Bel Ordry, from the bench at the side, before anybody had looked at him.

He was a man of about thirty-five out of the two rooms above the clinic return, and he had held nothing in the eleven weeks anybody had been counting, and he had been at the water mark once, on a day the water mark was not the water mark, and everybody in that anteroom had a reason to be careful about him and nobody had ever said it out loud.

"You have not been asked," said Sefu Aran, "and I am asking you now, in front of four people, and I am going to tell you what it is before you answer, because I have watched this room get one person out of a bell by telling him afterwards.

"It is not a watch. It is a bell. You pull it if the cloth moves or if I say the word or if anybody shouts, and then you go up the nine steps and you stand at the top of them, and you do not come down them. I will be on the common landing with a rope and I will not be able to see the cloth. The moment you have pulled it you are not the man on the mark, you are the man on the bell, and the mark waits for whoever holds the mark to come back, and I will not have a person doing both. Do you want to know what a bell is for?"

"Yes," said Bel Ordry, and then, before anyone could answer for him: "Yes. Because I have lived in this boundary for eleven weeks behind a shut stair and I have never once been asked what the noise was, and I would rather be asked late and be angry about it than go on not knowing."

Sefu Aran told him, and Bel Ordry repeated the whole of it back, which is the fourth time in eleven weeks that anybody has repeated the whole of it back, and the room had stopped remarking on that and had started expecting it.

And the string was the woman from the wash rooms, and Renn Vale asked her for it in person on the morning, at the top of the three steps, and the woman said yes, and Renn wrote the asking and the answer down in her own hand with the time on it and read it back, and the room did not remark on that either, because it was the whole of what the ninth week had been about, and it is the single most important line Dalla Sen writes that week.

---

Iven cut the head in the anteroom on the day before, standing on the bench with the old flat file's tang as a chisel and a hammer Jo's neighbour had lent and would take back the same afternoon, and he talked the whole time in the voice he used for teaching, which nobody in that building had ever heard him use on purpose.

"You are not cutting a length," he said. "You are cutting a bearing, and a bearing is a face the wood can push against without the wood splitting and without the stone splitting. So the head is not flat. It is *set* — a little proud at the top and a little hollow at the bottom, so that the head sits in the hollow and the proud bit at the top is what stops it climbing. Cut it flat and the first time that head comes tight the timber rides up the stone, and a shore that climbs is not a shore, it is firewood leaning on a wall for a while.

"And the foot is not cut either. The foot wants a long reach, because the reach is what makes the rake, and the rake is what makes it a shore and not a prop. I have measured the reach twice and it is four foot two from where that stone beds at its inner end, and I want that in the record with the two on it, because the other shore on this landing is four foot seven back from the wall and I checked, and it is a different number and I am not going to have anybody round here thinking I reused one. Four foot two is measured off the stone. Four foot seven is measured off the wall. They are not the same datum and a person who treats them as the same datum will put this shore a hand's width out and we will find out at the wrong time."

"And here is the part I have been sitting on since the length came up, and I am going to say it now so that the room hears it before the work starts rather than at the end of it when everybody is pleased." He put the chisel down. "That head wants a wedge. Every shore I have ever built wants a wedge at the head, because a wedge is what tells you at a glance whether the head is moving. I have got nothing. I have got five public-works hardwood wedges in the other shore on this landing, and I have not got one spare, and I have asked, and the answer I have got is no, in Renn Vale's own words, about that head and about the queue behind it, and the refusal is not withdrawn. And I am treating it as the answer about wedges in this building as well, because I am the one who wants one, and I have said that out loud so that somebody in this room can tell me I am wrong."

"Then pack it," said Jo Verrick.

"Pack it," Iven said. "Which means a man on the head of that timber with his own weight and his own knee, and the wood goes in under him or it does not go in at all, and if it does not go in he takes it out and cuts a different piece. And there is no line on that, because there is no line in this building free, and I am not asking for one, and if I did ask Renn would say no and she would be right."

The room was quiet for a moment.

"Read the *carried* column," Tams said, from the bench.

Tams read it out loud, because that is what the column is for, and he read it in his left hand, slowly, longhand, and nobody took the slate off him, which had not been true in the seventh week and was true now.

"Spent," he said. "One frayed nine-metre line, on a ladder foot, in six weeks of standing water. One cracked lamp, standing on the third step of a stair. One pry bar, in the haunch of the third step. One screw jack, under a shore's foot, and it is the best thing any of us has ever owned and that none of us ever owned, and if I put it in that column twice then I have made it ours twice. Two refusals of salvage.

"Carried," he said. "One item, and it is in Oren Sill's words and I wrote it out longhand because I was frightened of losing it. *You do not pack a shore with wedges. Wedges take up slack. A shore is packed with a face, and a face is made by putting the right piece of wood in the right place and then leaning on it until it goes in. So: a piece of the packing offcut, sized, set at the toe, and then a man on the head of the timber with his whole weight and his own knee, and the wood goes in under him or it does not go in at all, and if it does not go in you take it out and cut a different one, because a shore packed with wood that was not sized is a shore that has a spring in it, and a shore with a spring in it puts its head out four months later and takes a person's hand off at the knuckle.*"

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"That has been on that board since the ninth week and it has been read out four times and it is going to be read out a fifth time in about four hours," Tams said, "and that is the whole of what this work has to pay with, and I have got it in one hand's writing and I am not going to lose it."

---

They took it down on the second morning of the work at the first bell and they were on the near landing inside four minutes, because the school door is two foot eight at the head under a cracked lintel held by two shoring lengths and three wedges and a hand line, one wedge short of what it wants, and everybody who goes through it goes through it the way you go through a door you have decided about.

Marek Venn went last and stood on the common landing with his left hand flat on the wall above the stone, and waited.

That is the whole of it. He put his palm on the dressed stone over the lip and he stood there with his eyes shut and he did the thing he has done six times in ten weeks and has never once been able to describe to another person, and nothing came. Not a number, not a direction, not a sense of where a thing was heavy. Stone, cold, slightly damp at the left edge, and the ordinary enormous patience of eleven feet of shaft under his hand.

He wrote *null* on the corner of a slate in his left hand and did not hand it over and did not keep it, which was what he had started doing, and Jo Verrick took it off the bench at the end of the period without being asked and pocketed it, and it was the seventh.

---

The work went on for a day and a half and it is worth saying how, because it is the whole of what this building got out of a flooded room.

Jo Verrick packed the head. Not Oren — Oren, who had the hands for it, who had demonstrated it, who had done it four times in nine weeks — and she said so out loud on the first morning, to the room, in the anteroom, before anybody had got as far as the landing.

"I am going to be the packer on this," she said, "and I am going to say out loud that I learned it off a man who is under a stone eleven feet down, and that I am doing it with two hands and my own weight and a knee, and that if I get it wrong then the thing that goes wrong is a lip that a person is standing under at some point in the next season, and I will not be able to say afterwards that I did not know."

Renn Vale had said on the second morning, in the anteroom, the other thing, and it was in the ledger in her own hand with a time on it, and it was the line the whole week turned on.

"I am not going to be down there," she said. "I said in the thirty-eighth day that I would not stand a watch on a stair day, and I said it was wider than the wet seam, and I have not withdrawn a syllable of it and I am not going to now because a woman is about to lean on a piece of timber on my floor. So I will not be on that landing while it is being packed. I will be at the upper landing and I will be at the bell and I will be here, and if the room needs me I will be told, and if the room thinks it needs me it can ask me, and I will say no, and it will be in the book with the time on it."

"Isel asked," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel.

"Renn is not to be asked and Renn is not to be asked," Renn said, "and I have said it faster than you could ask it, and put that in too."

---

The plinth was read by Ruk Sella and the string by the woman from the wash rooms, and Pell Roke sat on the bench at the top of the nine steps with his ear against the stone of the step above the stair head and his crutch across his knees, and he was not on the rota and was not a reader and was not a post.

"I want that said in the room before anybody starts," he said, to Dalla, who wrote it in her own hand. "I am not on the rota. I am a boy with an ear. I cannot come off this bench and if I could I could not cross the waist, and anybody who builds a plan on me is building a plan on a person who cannot leave. So I am going to say what I hear, and it is going to be a warning, and it is not a stop, and if anybody in this building treats what I say as a stop then I have taught this room to be afraid of me, and I will not have that on a bench in a stair."

"Written," Dalla said, "in his words, and read back."

"It is a warning and not a stop," said the woman from the wash rooms, from the string, "and I will say it as well, because if he is going to be the warning then somebody has to be the stop, and the stop is me and the cloth and any of the four, and the four are me and Ilsa and Mikka and Renn, and I have been on that slab once without being asked and I am not going to be on it again without being asked, and Renn asked me this morning at the top of the three steps and I said yes, and it is in her hand and not in mine, because she is the one who asked and I would not want it in my hand where it looked like a favour."

---

What the plinth reader's warning was worth that day.

Pell called it about eight seconds before the wheel, the way he had called it in the ninth week, and he called it out loud in a voice that did not carry and did not need to, and Ruk Sella came off the plinth with the party's slack, and the woman on the string had the word out, and Jo Verrick was up off the near landing with the timber's head still under her own arm before the slab had finished moving, and Isel Ferrow did not leave the lip, because a bearer at the near lip is there for the whole of a crossing, and Isel Ferrow knew it, and stayed where he was.

They were fifty-one seconds off the near landing and up the three steps, which is the best figure in the record for a party with a head of timber in its hands, and the best on the stair is still the thirty-eight and nobody should be proud of either number, and Jo Verrick came up the three steps on her knees and said one word, which was "packed," and then lay on the common landing's floor for a while, and Renn Vale, who was at the upper landing and who had said in the anteroom that she would not be a step further down, watched all of it from where she was standing and did not come down, and nobody asked her to.

And the lip went about a finger and a half.

"I want that written down and I want it read back and I want it in the same breath as the number from the fortnight," Jo said, from the floor. "A finger and three-quarters to two inches before. A finger and a half after. So the shore took about a quarter of an inch off the worst day of my life and a quarter of an inch off the smallest reading I have ever had on that stone, and I am telling you now, out loud, in the anteroom, with the room full, that **that is not a fix, and the man is still under the lip, and he will be under the lip next time and the time after, and the only thing this shore has done is make the number smaller.**"

---

The tally that night was read out loud at the fifth bell, and it was read in the order Tams had invented, and he read the new one first.

"Received," he said. "One length, five foot nine, public-works, number chalked on the end, condition as found, and it is now in a shore on the common landing, and it is not ours, and the crew moved it, and the number is still on the end of it under a face, which Nera wanted and which I have written down and which I think about more than is reasonable.

"Spent — one more, and it is not a tool. It is the packing, off the anteroom's four foot eleven and a bit, cut into sizes, and the four foot eleven is now a two foot ten and a handful, and the handful is in Jo's basket with the file and the mended-handled basket, and the basket is Jo's and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

"Material. No sound timber over six feet. No lath. No bronze. Seven sound wedges and two dead sticks, and every sound one of the seven is spoken for, three at the school-door head that wants four and has been refused its fourth by Renn Vale in her own words, three at the waist in washed-out fill, one in the basket, and none of the seven is spare and none of the seven can be got. Five public-works hardwood wedges in the other shore. Four foot eleven is a two foot ten and a handful and it is a shore's packing and not a length.

"Timber in this building, total, of every kind anybody can point at: a scarfed head of three pieces on the common landing, and one length of five foot nine under it, and a two foot ten and a handful in a basket.

"**And a post under the near landing's lip, which wants nine foot and a bundle, and which is not built, and which is not going to be built this year, and which is on the list as the tenth item, and I am reading the list, and the list has got one more item on it than it had on Thursday.**"

"And the seat," said Jo Verrick, from the frame, before anybody could get to the end of it, because that is how the tally has ended for nine weeks and she had asked for it on the first day of this week and it was asked for.

"Twenty-six strokes, six minutes off, nineteen," she said, "and the housing shut and untouched, and two cleanings or fewer, and I have not moved the estimate and I am not going to. The check is gone. The check is a hiss and the delivery is by ear now, and if it changes its note the draw stops and the work stops and nobody is to be angry with me. And I want the seat read out as two or fewer, in my own hand, with a time on it, and I want it read out on the same morning as the check, because one day somebody in a hearing is going to put the check and the seat side by side and ask me which of the two I would have gone after first, and I want the answer to already be on the page."

The anteroom went on being lived in. The wheel turned twice under the floor. The bell rope hung at the threshold, tied at the newel, and the restriction cord across the fixed upper approach had been re-tied that morning by two people and nobody else, as it is re-tied every time, by Renn Vale and Sefu Aran, in that order, and neither of them has ever once been asked to explain it to anybody.

And at the top of nine steps, on the bench where a wrapped plate had lain for eight weeks, a boy with a splinted ankle and a crutch across his knees had his ear against the stone and his eyes on the corner of the stair head, and Talla Roke was in the anteroom below with a folded paper in her hand, and the two of them had not said one word to each other since the one word on the steps, and Dalla Sen had written that down with the time on it, because a thing that does not happen is a thing a record can hold.
