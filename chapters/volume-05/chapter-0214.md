# Chapter 214 — Nine People And What They Are Owed

The fourth day of the forty-first week is a Thursday, the corridor is shut, and the school did not run and lost nothing, and nine people who had been in standing water since the sixth day of the fortieth week were sitting on the floor of the room at the kitchen-line end at the first bell, and the reason they were on that floor and not somewhere else is that Mikka Hale gave it to them at about the seventh bell the night before, in her own hand, on a line in her own book, and gave the reason out loud to a room that had four people in it.

"**I have six people who are not this building's at my table every night and I have had them since the thirty-fifth week, and there is room at that end for nine, and I am not having them in my kitchen because I have got a pot at three days and I am not having them in the anteroom because the anteroom has nine children's school in it on a Wednesday,**" she had said. "**So they get the corner and the corner is mine and I am the one who gives it and not a keeper and not the clerk, and if a man at the top of those nine steps wants it moved he can come and ask me and not you.**"

Nobody had asked her.

Jo Verrick had the frame at the first bell, a finger and two-thirds, and the party wall was a quarter of an inch and the same as the one before it, and the string was where the string was. She put her thumb under it and the thumb came out of it right, and she gave the four refusals in her own words before anybody asked her. She did not say the number of the morning, and the clerk entered that she had not, and entered the reason, and the reason was that the woman who reads walls has said in a room that she is going to give a reason instead of four refusals on Sunday and that she is not spending a number between now and then.

Renn Vale put her ear to the wall at the first bell, four times over, and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, and the run of mornings out of mornings is nineteen.

The terms were said at the first bell, all six, and the third of them was said without anybody being asked whether it was right. Ten including one litter for no more than two hours, and it did not change on the morning nine people were owed a slate. The outer service door is the one a body went out of. The fifth term is in a room with both of the people it names in it, and he named it and read nothing. The sixth was the pump, and it ran from the second bell to the fifth, and a foreman asked five men for four and a half hours after it had been said, which is not something a room is supposed to be able to do and which it did.

Then the pump was started, two people turning it, and at about the third bell the man of about fifty with grey at the side of his head stood up off the floor at the kitchen-line end, and he was steady on his feet, and he had been in water to his chest for three days and had come up a rope, and he came to the end of the trestle and stopped there and did not put his hands on anything.

"**I have got one thing to say and I have had it since the sixth day and I have not said it to anybody in a room because there was nobody in a room,**" said Seth Wray. "**We went in on somebody's word. Not mine. I am the man who brought the claim up four days on a road and left it on a table and said I would come back when this room said. I did not know about the water and I have not been up here since I left.**"

"**Then say whose word,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**A man's I have never met,**" said Seth Wray. "**He wrote it. It came down the middle passage with the claim and it is not a second line on the claim, it is a separate paper, and it is four lines long, and it is about pumps. It says the pumps on the line have not been turned over in nine years and that a turn-over crew is wanted and that there are nine of you. It does not say where. It does not say when. It does not say anything about water and if you want my opinion, which you have not asked for, then a man who writes four lines about pumps and does not write the word water has either never seen any or has decided it is somebody else's business.**"

"**And that is your half against,**" said Renn Vale.

"**That is my half against and I have been carrying it up a rope with me,**" said Seth Wray. "**I took nine people down a set of steps on a piece of paper from a man I have never met, and I did not put my own name on it because the name on it was not to be me, it was to be the nine. That is a form. I have worked out what that is and I have worked it out in three days in a room with a book on the wall and I would like it in that book in my own words, and I would like it entered that I worked it out in three days and that a man with twenty-three years would have worked it out in a day.**"

"**Entered, in a foreman's words, and entered that he was not asked,**" said Dalla Sen, and then she put the chalk down and looked at him for a moment longer than she usually does, and what she did next was the thing this room was owed.

"**Emrys Callow,**" she said. "**You have been owed a morning and you have had a fortnight to decide whether you were going to use it. I would like to know whether you are going to ask your crew for something, and I would like to know it before the sixth bell, because the sixth term says a pump is attended and a pump that runs on a morning nobody asked for anything is a pump that ran on nothing, and I have had that argument three times this month.**"

Emrys Callow put his cup down.

"**Then I am going to ask,**" he said, "**and I am going to do it the way I do it, and the way I do it is that I say the terms before anybody names them and I do not carry for a man whose name is on two lines of a form, and Renn Vale is the person who can be told no, and I am telling her now in front of everybody.**"

"**I have not said yes,**" said Renn Vale.

"**I have not asked you,**" Emrys Callow said, and she agreed with him, and the clerk entered that he had not asked and that she had not said yes and that both of those were in the room at the same time.

"**Four and a half hours,**" Emrys Callow said. "**There is a chamber at the bottom of a passage with nine hundred years of water in it and a course of stone round it at the height of a woman's knee, and nobody knows what is under the water, and there is a floor in it that does not dip, and the only way to find out what is under the water is to stand in the water with a lamp while somebody else holds the line. That is not shoring and it is not bracing and it is reading. It is reading, in a wet room, with a line.**"

"**The split,**" said Dalla Sen, "**before anybody names it.**"

"**I have named it,**" Emrys Callow said. "**It is the same split as the fourth asking and the sixth and there is no new split: two of mine, two of hers, and half of the fifth man who reads numbers and has still not been asked for an hour of work in nine weeks. I am naming the half because if I do not name it then somebody will offer it and it will be a favour, and he has already said once in this room that he is being paid for sitting in it, and I am not going to be a second thing that happens to that man.**"

Tams Quill looked up from the end of the bench.

"**I will take the half,**" he said, "**and I have nothing to say about it, which is a thing I have never once been able to say in this room, and I am not going to make a moment of it.**"

"**Then the three who cannot be asked,**" Emrys Callow said, "**and I am naming them before the asking, and the reasons are new, and none of them is carried from the last one.**"

"**Jo Verrick,**" he said. "**The reason is not a body and I want that said first. The work is four and a half hours of a person standing in water with a lamp, and the one thing she has said out loud in this room every morning for thirty-four mornings is that she will not put a rule on a wall. I am not going to be the man who makes her take that rule out of her belt in front of a crew on a Thursday afternoon. The day that rule comes out of that belt it is an instrument, and an instrument belongs to somebody, and she has not agreed to that and I am not entitled to make it happen to her.**"

"**Entered, in the foreman's words, and it is a new reason,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**Mikka Hale,**" Emrys Callow said. "**The reason is the number and not the water. If a man is going to stand in this boundary's water for four and a half hours then the only person in this building who can say how much water that is, at the moment he is standing in it, is the cook. And asking her for it in a room is asking her to say a number of hers out loud in front of five men and a clerk and nine strangers who were in it on Saturday. She has refused this book twice. She has told the clerk it was easier the second time and that she does not know why. I am not going to be the reason it stops being easy.**"

"**Entered, and entered that the clerk noticed the second half of that and has not written down what she noticed,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**And the third,**" Emrys Callow said, "**and this is not the third from last time and I am going to say so before I say it, because the third from last time was a different name with a different reason and I remember it.**"

He put both hands on the table.

"**Iven Tallo,**" he said. "**The work is holding. That is the reason and it has been the reason twice. A carpenter is the only man in this room who would want four and a half hours of a wet wall, and he has had a post refused for three volumes running and he has never been given anything, and every hour I give him is an hour he is useful. And that is precisely why I am not going to give him it. A foreman who hands a man the first thing in three volumes that is worth having has handed him a post. A post with a different name is still a post. This boundary has said no to six of them on the record and I am not going to be the seventh thing that happens to him by accident.**"

Nobody in that anteroom said anything for a moment, and at the anvil end a man with dye on his hands did not move and did not look up, and the clerk entered it in his own silence, because that is what the practice is.

Marek Venn, who had been at the end of the trestle since the first bell with his shut book under his forearm, did the thing he has done about four mornings in ten and did it badly, because he already knew the answer. He listened to a foreman refuse to give a man a work, and to the reason, and the reason was about a man, and that man could not hear a word of it, because he had not been asked and had not been told and was sitting at an anvil eight feet away with his hand flat on a piece of iron.

Three volumes. A post refused in the first of them and never offered again. A bond suspended and a second line of a stiff cover that has been empty since before Marek Venn came into this building. A man who told a room that a line is a line, into a room that was not listening, and was right, and was thanked by nobody, and asked by nobody, and put nothing anywhere.

And this morning a foreman with nine years of his own stood up in front of eleven people and gave a reason for not handing it to him. That was the best sentence in this room in five volumes and it belonged to a man who was not in the conversation, and Marek Venn understood, sitting at the end of a trestle with nothing in his hands, that it had been built to protect him from him, and that nobody in that anteroom had put it that way out loud, and that a clerk had taken it down in a man's own silence, and that the whole of the standing arrangement in this building — no column, no heading, no name, no post — ran on men refusing to be given things and not on anybody refusing to give them.

"**Then the asking,**" said Dalla Sen, and she did not put her hand near the book.

"**Emrys Callow, nine years, shoring, gave up a cleared-risk post-holder's bond in this district, and five men. Four and a half hours, on the open days, with the terms as I have given them. I will not carry for a man whose name is on two lines of a form. My five are not going on any column and their hours go on the line they are already on and nobody adds them up. And Renn Vale is the person who can be told no, and I am telling her now, in front of everybody, and she has not said yes.**"

"**I have said yes,**" said Renn Vale.

The room turned round.

"**I said yes,**" Renn Vale said, "**and I said it at the same moment he finished and I would like that entered, because if I had waited a second it would have been a yes after a man had finished asking, and I have watched this room make that shape four times and I am not doing it. Two of his and two of mine, and I am one of the two of mine, and I am not standing on a landing watching a man in water with a wall behind him. You can be told no and you have been told yes and that is the whole of the difference between the two and it is not a small one.**"

"**Entered, in the keeper's words, and entered that she said it at the same moment as the end of the asking and not after it,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**Then it is made,**" said Bel Ordry, from the step.

"**It is made,**" said Emrys Callow, "**and I have got no work for it until Monday morning and I will not pretend otherwise and I will not ask for it before Monday.**"

Then the room turned round, because that was how the morning went, and the nine people at the kitchen-line end had been sitting on a floor listening to a crew being asked for four and a half hours, and one of them was on his feet before the foreman had finished sitting down.

"**Now say what they are owed,**" said Bel Ordry. "**In a room, and not at a gate, and by a person, and it is going to be written down.**"

"**Then let me say the first thing,**" Seth Wray said. "**Whatever it is, it is not a rescue, because a rescue is a thing you do to people and we are nine adults with a line and a foreman and a letter. If anybody in this room says the word rescue I will go back down that passage tonight and I do not mean that as a threat, I mean that I would rather be in the water than be rescued, and I would like that written down first because it is the only thing I am certain of.**"

"**Entered, in his words, and entered that he was not asked,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**Then it is written,**" said Nella Bray, at the anvil end, and she had a slate under her arm and she had had it under her arm since the first bell. "**I have been at a counter for nineteen years and I have written about four thousand things that were owed to people and the thing I have learned is that an owing is never a feeling. It is a list and it is written in somebody's hand and it goes to the person it is owed to and it does not stay here.**"

"**Then write it,**" said Bel Ordry.

Nella Bray wrote for about as long as it takes to say a sentence and read it out.

"**One. Dry, and a place to be dry that is not a floor of an anteroom, and the cook's corner stays the cook's corner and that is hers and not ours.**"

Mikka Hale did not look up from the cloth over her arm.

"**Two. Warm, and there is a stove in this building that has not been lit in twenty weeks and that stove is not mine to promise, and what I can promise you is that this room will go and find out whether there is anything in it that can be lit without setting a school on fire, and that finding out is a work and not a gift.**"

"**Three. Fed, and that is the same pot,**" Nella Bray said, "**and the pot is at three days and the seam gave nothing yesterday and I have written *fed* and I have not written how much, because the woman who holds that number will not have it written and a slate is not where that number goes.**"

"**Four. An hour of air that somebody else is responsible for,**" Nella Bray said, and looked up, "**and I am putting *else* in deliberately, because a physician in this room has not certified the air in two rooms this side of that corridor and an air engineer has been asked nothing at all in forty weeks, and if those nine people are going to sleep in this building then this building is going to have to have somebody who is answerable for that, and that somebody is not a rota and it is not a column and it is going to be a person, and I do not yet know which person and I am not going to write a name in a slate I do not understand.**"

"**Five. And this is the one I was asked to write and I am going to write it in the words of the man who wants it,**" Nella Bray said. "**This boundary did not send you, this boundary did not know you were in there, and this boundary will say so in its own hand to anybody who asks, including the office four days away that wrote the paper.**"

"**Say the last part out loud to the room,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**This boundary did not send you and did not know, and will say so to the office that wrote the paper, in its own hand, to anybody who asks,**" Nella Bray said, and Seth Wray looked at the floor, and the clerk wrote for about six seconds and then stopped and read it back to herself and did not read it out.

"**Then the names,**" said Renn Vale.

"**The names go on the sheet,**" said Nella Bray, "**and the sheet goes to you, and the sheet does not come into this building's book, and I want the reason for that entered by a person who is not me.**"

"**The reason is nine,**" said Dalla Sen. "**Nine names in a clerk's book in a boundary that has entered one chamber and one room of shelves in a fortnight is nine names a person in a year can divide by the number of men on that bench. And a division is how a crew becomes a column. I have watched this building spend three volumes refusing to make that division and the four numbers that would let somebody else make that division are not going into a clerk's book out of me. The names are on a slate. The slate is theirs. This book has got *nine* and it has got what is owed and it has got the half of a sentence about an office four days away, and that is the whole of what this boundary is prepared to have written down about nine people who are not its.**"

"**Then the hours,**" said Bel Ordry, "**because somebody in this room is going to try to make this a crew this afternoon and I would like it stopped before they try.**"

"**The hours go on their own line, under nothing, and nobody adds them up,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and they do not join the five on the bench, and they do not join Nera's crew, and the two lines have not touched in forty weeks and they are not going to start on a Thursday. The check crosses. The hours do not. I have entered that and I would like a person in a year to be able to find this line and this sentence and to know that the hours of nine people who went into a gallery on somebody else's word have never once been added to the hours of five men on a bench in this building.**"

"**Nella Bray,**" said Tams Quill, from the end of the bench, and she turned round, and he had the piece of chalk he has not used in his hand and he did not look at it. "**You are going to want the nineteenth.**"

"**I am not putting anything on that board,**" he said. "**And I am going to say why, and it is not about me, and it is the second of mine that is not about me, and I would like the clerk to have it in these words. A debt is not a thing this building found. A debt is a thing this building has. That board is a list of what we found, and eleven of the eighteen on it are nothings and seven of them are things, and every one of the seven was something a person in this room went and got. You have just asked me to put an owing on it. The day that board has got an owing on it, it stops being a list of what we found and it starts being a list of what we are prepared to say out loud in a room, and that is a plan, and I have said the word *corner* in this room once already and I am not going to say it twice.**"

"**And the number,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**That is the ninth,**" said Tams Quill, "**and I have said it out loud before she writes it, and I have said it six days after the eighth and not on the morning after it, and I would like it entered that the ninth and the eighth are six days apart and that I have not been in any particular hurry.**"

Nobody told him thank you, and nobody told him he was wrong, and Dalla Sen entered the ninth and entered that he had said the number before she wrote it and that the reason was in his words and was not about him.

At the sixth bell the clerk read the day's figure and the total out loud, and gave the reason, and the reason was that a morning on which a crew is asked for four and a half hours and a debt is written down on a slate that is going out of the building is a morning on which this room has done two things it has refused to do for three volumes, and a clerk who reads a total over that morning is a clerk telling the room it is finished.

"**Two today and three hundred and forty-six since the keeper signed. The boundary is at four hundred and sixty-three and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.**"

"**And the crew's column has moved,**" she said, "**from seventy-five and a half to eighty, and it moved by four and a half hours because a foreman asked in a room with the terms out loud and a person named who could be told no and three people named who could not, and it is the first time in nineteen weeks that anything has moved on that line, and that is the whole of what I have to say about it and I have said it.**"

The count went in underneath that on its own line, in the ordinary hand, and was not read, and the reason was given and was that the clerk read that number out yesterday to a room in which a man had come up a rope and a woman had come up with a leaf in her coat, and she is not going to say it again the day after, and that is a reason about the clerk and not about the count. Wen Pask is one hundred and fifty-nine days and one hundred and fifty-nine nights, forty-eight weeks of a clerk saying out loud that she is going to keep writing it down. It is not going to be one hundred and sixty.

The board was not walked and did not need to be, because it had been argued at, and the clerk entered that the board stands at eighteen entries, eleven of them nulls and seven that are not, and that the unheaded column has nothing over the top of it this morning and had nothing over the top of it before anybody spoke.

The pot is three days and the seam gave nothing this morning, and the fire in the room at the kitchen-line end has now not been lit for twenty weeks, and the four households at a second gate have nothing and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody, and the nine who are owed five things are in a corner that belongs to a cook and are not anybody's.

And at the seventh bell the anteroom was nine steps and a trestle and a bench of five men and a man at the end of it with his hand open and flat, and a slate with nine names on it lying face up at the kitchen-line end where anybody could read them, and a pump that had run, and a room that had written down what it owed in a stranger's hand and was going to send it out of the building in the morning.
