# Chapter 212 — A Chamber And Not A Cell

The second day of the forty-first week is a Tuesday, the corridor is shut, and the school did not run and lost nothing. The pump ran. At about the second bell two people went down a passage at the bottom of which there is a gallery that nobody in this building has ever been in, and neither of them had said so in the morning, and both of them had been named in a room before the pump was started, and the reason they were named in a room before the pump was started is that a thing you are going to do in the afternoon is a thing the room is entitled to know about in the morning.

Jo Verrick had the frame at the first bell, a finger and two-thirds, and the party wall was a quarter of an inch, the thirty-second reading, and the same as thirty-one, and the string was where the string was. She put her thumb under it and the thumb came out of it right, and she gave the four refusals in her own words before anybody asked her, and the count of mornings is thirty-two and is the same count as the wall's, and the folding rule is in a belt and has been on a belt for thirty-two mornings.

Renn Vale put her ear to the wall at the first bell, four times over, and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, and the run of mornings out of mornings is seventeen.

The terms were said, all six, by a person and repeated by the clerk, and the third of them was new and was said without anybody being asked whether it was right, and the clerk entered that the cook was in the room and entered not one word about whether it was true. Ten including one litter for no more than two hours, and it did not change. The outer service door is the one a body went out of. The fifth of the six is in a room with both of the people it names in it, and he named it and read nothing, and the clerk entered that she did not ask him to read it on a morning when two people were about to go into water. And the sixth was the pump, and the pump runs, and nobody said out loud that the pump is the reason two people were going down a wet stair at the second bell, and the clerk entered that nobody had said it.

Then the man at the end of the trestle said the thing he had been carrying for a week, and he did not say it the way anybody expected, and afterwards two people in that anteroom said so.

"**I am not going to say what I know,**" Marek Venn said, "**and I am going to give you the reason for that in my own words first, because I have given four reasons in a fortnight and every one of them was about other people and the clerk has entered that four times, and this one is also about other people and I am going to say so out loud before she does.**"

"**Go on,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**There is a shape in my head and it has a place at each end of it, and I have had it since the second day of the fortieth week, and I have let a child of nine say it better than I would have said it. So here is the fifth reason and it is the same shape as the other four: if I say the shape in this room then two things happen and neither of them is that the room knows something. The room builds a plan on it. And then I am a man who handed a room a plan, and in four months this building has got a corner with a day in it, and it will have got it out of my mouth.**"

"**Then what are you giving us,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**Two names and a task,**" Marek Venn said. "**Jo Verrick, because there is a wall at the bottom of that passage and she is the only person in this building whose trade is a wall that is already built, and she has told this room every morning for thirty-two mornings that she is not going to put a rule on the join in the party wall and she has never once said the same thing twice, which means she is thinking about it. And Nera Oduya, because that room is going to be full of water and a full room is her trade, and because she has been in this building for forty weeks on a stair with a piece of chalk in her hand and I would like one thing in this building to be built out of what she is for.**"

He put his hand flat on the shut book.

"**I am not going down,**" he said. "**My knee does not bend past about half and a man who cannot get down a stair is a man who cannot get out of one, and if this room sends me and I come up slower than I went down then the room will have sent the wrong man and it will have sent him because he is the man who has the shape. So I am staying at the end of this table and if it is wrong then the two people who found out will have found out wrong and not a man who read it in his head.**"

"**Say the half against that,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**The half against is that a shape in a man's head is not evidence and a room that is sent to look for a shape comes back with the shape whether it is there or not, and there is nobody in this room to cross it,**" Marek Venn said. "**Enter that I said it and that I said it about myself.**"

Then the physician, from the frame of the far door, with his bag against the wall where it goes.

"**Then I am going to name a person before two people go down a passage into water,**" said Mero Senn, "**because a plan with a named injured person in it is not a plan, and the person I am going to name is the person you should have sent and did not send.**"

"**Say the name,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**Leena Orr,**" said Mero Senn. "**Right shoulder out since the twenty-first week, on the lower level, on this side of the waist, and she is the only person in this building who could read a nine-hundred-year-old wall the way it ought to be read, and she put a line on a pump frame in the eleventh week that a pump has been running against for nineteen weeks, and this room has kept her off every wet corner it has had for five weeks and has been very pleased with itself about it. So the woman you are not sending is the woman who would do this properly, and the woman you are sending is the woman who has been telling this room every morning that she will not put a rule on a wall. You are asking her to go and look at a wall without a rule. That is not caution. That is sending the second best because the best one is easier to live with.**"

Nobody improved on that, and Dalla Sen entered it, and entered that nobody had asked the physician for it.

"**Then say why you are going,**" said Jo Verrick, from the wall, without turning round.

"**Because he is right and I am not the woman he means,**" Nera Oduya said, from the second of her three steps, and had the dyed cloth folded in her hand and not out, "**and because I would rather go and be wrong in a wet room than stay here being right about a stair.**"

They went at the second bell and they were gone about two hours, and nobody in that anteroom did anything for those two hours except wait, badly, and the waiting is in the book because the clerk entered that she wrote nothing at all for two hours and would like that entered as a fact about her own hand.

They came back at about the fourth bell, and both of them were wet to the thigh, and both of them were cold in the way that people are cold when they have been standing still in water longer than they meant to, and Jo Verrick sat down on the floor of the anteroom without being asked and without any particular dignity, and the man at the anvil end put his coat on the floor beside her without being asked either, and neither of those was entered and both of them were noticed.

"**Say it,**" said Dalla Sen, with her hand near the book.

"**Four walls dressed,**" said Jo Verrick, from the floor. "**Not one of them rough. Not one of them. Dressed stone, laid in courses, and the courses are level, and I have been reading dressed stone for nineteen years and I have never once seen a course that is level going round a room, because a course goes where the wall goes and a wall goes where the stone lets it. This one does not. It is the same depth all the way round, and the joints are tight enough on the inside that I could not get a thumbnail into one, and it is nine hundred years old and somebody laid it. And the floor.**"

"**The floor,**" said Nera Oduya.

"**The floor does not dip anywhere,**" said Jo Verrick. "**I was in water to my thigh and I put my hand down in four places and in four places the floor was the same height. In a cave the water finds the low place. In a cave you put your hand down and you are putting it down in a puddle. I have been in eleven flooded rooms in nineteen years and every one of them had a low place and I have never once been in one that did not.**"

"**And the wall does something else,**" said Nera Oduya, "**and I have been arguing with myself about whether to say it since the fourth bell.**"

"**Say it,**" said Renn Vale.

"**The water stands at one height on all four sides,**" Nera Oduya said. "**Not level with itself — level. I put my hand flat on the wall at my shoulder on three sides and the water was at the same line on all three, and above the water there is a band of salt about a hand's breadth deep going right round the room, and it is grey and it is old and it is not a tide, because a tide goes in and out. That is salt that has been laid down by water standing and gone and standing and gone. So this room has been filled and emptied more than once, on purpose, and the reason it emptied is not in it any more, and the only reason a room gets filled and emptied on purpose is that somebody built it to hold something and then had to let the something out.**"

"**Say the last thing,**" said Dalla Sen, "**because you have both stopped and one of you has not stopped for a reason.**"

Jo Verrick put her hand flat on the floor of the anteroom and got herself up off it in two stages, which took a while, and nobody helped her and nobody offered.

"**There is a course of stone set in at about the height of my knee, going right round the room, and it is not a ledge,**" she said. "**A ledge is where a wall stops and you can put things on it. That is a course. It is a course set at one height all the way round a room, and a course set at one height is not a thing anybody does for the look of it, it is a thing you do when you have to get something to a level, and I do not know what the something was, and it does not matter, because the thing itself is a thing a person did and not a thing the water did.**"

"**Then say the sentence,**" said Bel Ordry, from the step.

"**A chamber built to be filled is not a cave,**" said Jo Verrick. "**That is what a cave is not. I have been in eleven of them and I could not tell you one thing about any of them except that they were not built. That room was built. Somebody cut four dressed walls and a floor that does not dip and set a course at one height so that they could work to it, and then they filled it, and then they let it out, and then it has stood for nine hundred years with salt on its shoulders.**"

Nobody in the anteroom improved on that, and Dalla Sen entered it in the words of the woman who read it, and entered that the woman who read it gave the reason she had not said it for about an hour before she said it, which was that she had been arguing with herself about the salt.

"**I do not agree,**" said Nera Oduya.

"**Say the half against,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**I agree that it is built and I do not agree that it is a chamber,**" Nera Oduya said. "**A chamber is a room that a person put inside a building. That is a room that a person cut out of rock nine hundred years ago on purpose, and a room cut out of rock on purpose is not a room, it is a hole with a floor in it, and I have spent forty weeks teaching people that the difference between a room and a hole is what goes round it, and a hole with a level floor and a course at one height is still a hole. It is a very good hole. It is the best hole I have ever been in. And I am not going to call it a chamber in a room in front of a book until somebody has shown me what goes round it.**"

"**Then that is the half against and it is hers,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and the clerk has it, and nobody in this room is going to improve on it, and the sentence about the chamber stands on one woman's word and one woman's not.**"

Then Iven Tallo put his hand flat on the iron at the anvil and said a thing that had been in him since the eighth bell.

"**There are nine people in there,**" he said.

"**Say more than that,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**There are nine people in the water past that chamber,**" said Iven Tallo. "**On the far side of it, in a passage that goes on, and they are up to their chests and they have a line tied round the third one from the front because somebody tied it, and I have been in this building forty weeks and I do not have a sentence for what I am looking at and I am going to say the pieces of it instead. They are nine. They are not dressed like this building's people and they are not dressed like anybody who lives in a house. They have a foreman with them because the one at the front is a man of about fifty with grey at the side of his head and he is not shouting and he is holding a line. And he looked at two people coming down a wet stair in a building he has never been in, and he did not shout at us, and he said nine words.**"

"**What were the nine words,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**We went in on somebody's word,**" said Iven Tallo. "**That is eight. The ninth was *yours* and I do not know yet whose it is, and neither does he.**"

The anteroom was very quiet for about four strokes of a pump that is running.

"**Entered, in a carpenter's words, and entered that he was not asked,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that nine is a number of people and that this clerk has written no name and has not asked for one, and entered that the two people who went down this morning went down to look at a wall and came up with a wall and nine people, and entered that neither of those was the task they were given.**"

"**Then say the cost of it out loud,**" said Marek Venn, from the end of the trestle, and he had not moved all afternoon. "**Say the cost of it, because I sent them and I did not know, and the room is going to find out in about a day and I would rather it found out now.**"

Dalla Sen put the book down and turned it round on the trestle, which she does.

"**The cost is this,**" she said. "**For nineteen weeks this boundary's whole position in a file has been that nobody in this building has been in that room. That is the sentence that goes with a claim, that goes with a form, that goes with a hearing this room is still owed a man for, and it is the one true thing this boundary has about the water. This morning two women stood in it. So the sentence is not true any more, and it will not be true again, and there is a guild's claim on the table in this room with a seal on it that was made out in the ninth week, and the claim describes a gallery and rooms off it, and as of this morning the claim has a fact in it that this boundary put there itself.**"

"**So we have made the claim better,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**We have made the claim better,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and I would like it entered that a clerk noticed it at the same moment as a man with a page in a coat and that neither of us said anything for about four seconds, and that this room's best piece of evidence went out of the door this afternoon with two women who were sent to look at a wall.**"

At the sixth bell the day's figure and the total went into the book and neither was read out, and the reason went in beside them: a room on the afternoon of which two of its people have been in water and come out of it, and a clerk who reads a total over that afternoon is telling the room the afternoon is over, and the rope is still going down a passage.

Three today and three hundred and thirty-eight since the keeper signed. The boundary is at four hundred and fifty-five and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.

The count went in underneath that on its own line, in the ordinary hand, and was not read, and the reason was given and was that the clerk read that number out yesterday in a room where a term had been changed by a person who will not have her own number checked, and she is not going to stand in front of nine people and four wet footprints and say it a second day running, and that is a reason about the clerk and not about the count.

Wen Pask is one hundred and fifty-seven days and one hundred and fifty-seven nights, forty-six weeks of a clerk saying out loud that she is going to keep writing it down. It is not going to be one hundred and fifty-eight.

The board was walked, and it is at eighteen entries, eleven of them nulls and seven that are not, and the two columns are nine and nine, and the second of them has nothing over the top of it. Tams Quill looked at the end of the bench and did not say anything about it, which is his practice and not a refusal, and the clerk entered that he had not been asked and had not offered.

The crew are at seventy-five and a half and did not move, and the reason for the day is in the book, and the reason is that a foreman has said out loud in a room that the water is going to take four and a half hours off him on Wednesday and has not yet said why, and that a crew's column does not move on a Tuesday on the strength of an afternoon.

The pot is three days, and the seam gave nothing this morning, and it is at four threads, and nobody measured it, and the fire in the room at the kitchen-line end has now not been lit for eighteen weeks, and the four households at a second gate have nothing and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody, and there are nine people in a chamber with a course set round it at the height of a woman's knee who went in on somebody's word.

And at the seventh bell the anteroom was nine steps and a trestle and a book with a cost entered in it, and two women who had been wet to the thigh and had come back with a sentence one of them agreed with and one of them did not, and a rope going down a passage in the dark at the bottom of nine steps that nobody in that room had ever seen the bottom of before this morning.
