# Chapter 49 — For People and Air

On the first morning after, the room did what it always did, which was to find out what had changed, and the answer was that a great deal had changed in a way that was not on anybody's page, and one thing had changed in a way that was.

Mikka Hale had the pot on at the fifth bell and there were fourteen people in the anteroom and the anteroom holds ten including one litter, and four of the fourteen were crew who do not live here, and the other ten were residents who had nowhere else to be, and nobody said anything about it for about a minute, and then Sefu Aran said, "Then we do that on a day, and we do it in words, and today we do it in the fact that the corridor was shut and the anteroom was emptied inside a minute and a half and I counted it and it is on the ledger, and I am not going to pretend a page is a thing that fixes a floor."

Leena Orr's litter was in the anteroom and it went to the clinic at the second bell, four minutes and six seconds, on a wet floor, through two feet eight, with four bearers, and the four minutes are in the record in three hands, and they were the four minutes in the record for the whole of that day and the day after.

---

Nera Oduya had the finding and she gave it in the anteroom, standing at the valve, with the cloth in her hand, and she gave it before she gave anything else because the air had moved and the moving of it was hers.

"Two things, and the first is a number and the second is not a number, and I am going to give them in that order because I have spent nine weeks watching this building put the comfortable one first."

She held up a strip of dyed cloth and did not put it near anything.

"Since the shore went in, the pressure in this boundary has changed twice in a way I can measure and once in a way I cannot, and the measurable one is this: the stair is a vent. It always was. The throat goes down eleven feet and it is open at the bottom to the shaft, and the shaft goes down to the pump gallery, and the pump gallery goes down to the chamber we all came out of, and when the pump runs that column is pulled and the air comes up through the stair, and when the pump stops it is let down and the air goes the other way. I have known that since the twenty-ninth day. What I have not had before is a second opening at the top of it."

"You have opened the stair's top," said Marek.

"I have *not* done anything," Nera said. "I have a line in a hollow that has been a hole with a stone in it for forty years, and I did not make the hole. I made a stone stop stop stopping, and the air has noticed, and the air has had a second road for a week, and it has used it. So: the boundary is now breathing through the stair, and the stair breathes with the pump, and the school corner's mouth is shut and the fall is cut, and there is no grit and there has not been for six days, and I am not going to tell anybody that the shut caused that, because I have one reading and a hundred years of somebody else's reading and a week's of mine."

"And the second thing."

"The second thing is that I have a new column on the valve board with two marks in it and I do not know what either of them means, and one of them is the same shape as the reading that came up under the school corner floor in the thirty-seventh day, and the other one is a shape I have never seen and it is on a Thursday." She hung the cloth up. "So I am not certifying the air of that stair. I am not certifying it now and I will not certify it on the day anybody asks me to, and what I am putting on the board instead is: *the stair is on the boundary's air and is to be read at the second bell and the fifth by a person, out loud, to a person, and not to a page, until the air engineer says otherwise in writing.* That is not a rating. It is a watch, and it is the cheapest possible thing, and it is what I have got."

"And the anteroom," said Sefu.

"The anteroom does not change," Nera said, and she said it in the way she said things that she had decided and would not be moved on, which is once. "Ten people including one litter, no more than two hours, with the outer service door open and the pump attended and one of the two of us able to stop it. That rating was made for a boundary with one exit and a pump. There is now a second thing that moves air, and the second thing does not make the anteroom safer and it does not make it worse, and I am not going to let anybody in this room use a new piece of air to argue a bigger number for a room that has been standing at ten for nine weeks. The route changes the escape. It does not change the occupancy. Say that on the page."

"Say it on the page," Jo said, and wrote it in her own hand, and Dalla copied it and read it back.

---

Somebody asked about a cart on the second morning, and the somebody was Mikka Hale and the asking was entirely reasonable, and Iven Tallo answered it in four sentences and did not enjoy any of them.

"The stores are up nine steps and half of what a kitchen wants comes up those nine steps on somebody's back, and I have been doing it for nine weeks and my shoulders have not been right since the twenty-fourth day, and I am not asking for a cart, I am asking whether one could come." She said it the way she said everything, with her hands on the shelf. "And no, and I will tell you the three reasons and then I will stop talking about it forever."

He held up a hand.

"One: a handcart is four hundredweight and it goes on the slab on four little wheels, and the slab is a bridge with a rail on the far side and a lip on this side, and I do not know what happens to four hundredweight on that slab because nobody has ever put a quarter of it on it and I am not going to find out with a kitchen's stores. Two: a cart cannot be stopped. Every crossing this building has done for nine weeks has been stopped four times by a word from a woman with a bell. A cart does not hear a bell. Three, and this is the one that matters, and it is Jo's and not mine."

He looked at her.

"Four at a time is a rule about people," Jo Verrick said. "It is four because four is what the lip will take and four is what four minutes will move and four is what a man under a lip can be told to bring over. Every number in that route is a number about bodies. Take the bodies out and you have not got a bigger route. You have got a different and much more dangerous thing, and I have spent nine weeks in this building learning that the way you find out a load is to put it on, and I would rather we found out with somebody's shoulders than with a kitchen's winter."

"That is the right answer," Mikka said, "and I am annoyed about it, and I am not going to ask again, and I want it written that I asked once and was told no for reasons and not because anybody thought I was being silly."

"It is written," Dalla said.

---

And then the thing that Iven Tallo had put on the page in the thirty-eighth day came back into the room as a fact instead of a warning, which is what a warning is for.

"A stair is a route," he said. "A route is the one thing in this building the Cartwrights have ever had a legal instrument for, and I said in the thirty-eighth day that putting the lower side back into service is how a route charter arrives, and I said the restriction protects you as well as it protects the rooms, and every one of the six of you thought I was being dramatic."

"It is not dramatic," Sefu said.

"No. It is here." Iven put his hand flat on the bench. "There is a route in this Hearthhold with no charter. It goes down four steps and across a slab and down four treads into a shaft nobody has seen the bottom of, and it is open, and no document anywhere in Marrowgate says a word about it, and there is a Cartwright clearance master forty days of walking away with an order that locks a surface door we cannot open and cannot reach. The first time anybody from that office asks a question about this building, the question will not be *is the route safe*. The question will be *who maintains the route*, and the answer they will want to write down is a holder's name, and the answer they will want behind it is a piece of paper."

"So what is ours," said Dalla Sen, with her pencil out. "That is the question and I want it in words and I want it now, because I am not writing a page that says *not* in eleven places. What is this route, on paper, that is not a charter and is not a claim and is not nothing?"

Jo wrote it, and it went in her hand, and Dalla read it back twice and then put it in the ledger in her own, because a thing that is going to be read in a hearing should not be in a stranger's hand.

*That the stair in the Hearthhold boundary is a route maintained by the people whose rooms it is under, and is not held by anybody. That the maintenance is in Renn Vale's name and is a road and not a wall, and that the line that holds the work is the boundary's own line from the boundary's own store, released by its keeper for a named work, and re-tied by the keeper and Renn Vale and nobody else. That the work was done by six people from above who do not live here, are not on the rota, are not a maintenance party, and whose hours were given and are owed to nobody. That the crew hold no document, no seal, no receipt, no claim, and no part of it. That there is a reading of the air at the second bell and the fifth by a person, out loud, to a person. That the route is shut by any of the four people who keep a room, and by the cord, and by the cloth. And that no person may add a name to it.*

"That last line is mine and I want it in capitals," Jo said, "and I want the clerk to read it back to the room as a line and not as a paragraph."

"It is read back as a line," Dalla said.

And the guild, and the claim, and the fear of it, were not settled by that page, and Iven said so, and it should have been said in the room and not in a hearing and Iven said it in the room.

"It is not a protection. It is a *position*. If the guild asks, the answer is a route maintained by a Hearthhold and a work done by strangers who left and a line that belongs to a boundary, and that is a harder thing for an office to answer than a charter is, because a charter has a hole in it and this has six names in it that are not parties to anything. It is not a shield. It is a wall with no gate in it, and the first man who wants through it will put his shoulder against it, and the wall will hold longer than people think and it will cost somebody something that is not written down anywhere, and I would like somebody in this room to remember that I said it on a day when it was cheap to say it."

Nobody wrote it down, and Jo Verrick did not put it on the page, and Dalla Sen looked at him for a moment and then did not ask, and the reason she did not ask is on no page at all.

---

Tams Quill read the tally out in the anteroom at the fifth bell, and he read the *spent* column first, because that is the column he invented it for and it is now the only column anybody looks at.

"Spent," he said. "One frayed nine-metre line, on a ladder foot, in six weeks of standing water. One cracked lamp, standing on the third step of a stair, because there is one lamp and it was not coming up nine steps in the dark with a man carrying something else. One pry bar, in the haunch of the third step, driven in on the morning of the day and not coming out this week. And the jack."

He stopped, and nobody made him go on, and he went on.

"The jack stays under the shore's foot and it is the best thing any of us have ever owned and none of us ever owned it, and I have written it in the *spent* column and not in a column of my own because if I write it in a column of my own then it is ours and it is not and it never was." He turned the board round further, so the room could see the rest of it. "And the salvage: two refusals. The piece of stone in the hollow, kept where it is at the boundary's keeper's word. And the fallen parapet slab, kept where it is, at the clerk's word, asked for by name on the day and refused, and the person who asked has asked his name to be written beside it."

"And refused to build on it for nine weeks," Jo said, from the frame, where she was, and where she was going to be for a while.

Then he read the rest, which is the part a room needs at the end of a day like this one, and it is all of it and none of it is new.

"Water: five liters from the collapse and one sealed liter of verified condensation, and the sealed liter did not open this month and has not opened since the sixth week, and that is a fact and not a victory. The kitchen's nineteen days are given and not waived and have not gone down by one and the kitchen is not fed by this crew and does not feed it, and the pot is not a wage and Mikka has said so in public twice and I am putting it in the tally so that it is in arithmetic as well as in words.

"Wedges: seven sound and two dead sticks. Three at the school-door head, which wants four, and does not have four, and did not get them this week, and Renn Vale refused that in her own words and the refusal is in the ledger and it is hers and it is not withdrawn. Three at the waist, in fill that is gone. One in the basket. And off the public-works register, in this room, on the common landing: five hardwood wedges, four in the shore's head and one in its foot, and the crew owns none of them.

"Timber: no sound timber over six feet. No lath. No bronze. The delivery seat is scored and has two cleanings on it or fewer and the estimate is Jo's and it is in her own hand and it was read out before the fifth cleaning and not after. A screw jack and a pry bar, one of which is under a shore and one of which is in a step. Three lengths of spare timber in the shore on the common landing, one of them three foot four and one of them two foot nine and a stub, and four foot eleven and a bit of offcut in this anteroom, and a fourth length of five foot nine on the bottom shelf of a flooded pump gallery with a number chalked on it, and the inventory line open, and nobody is going down there this month.

"And the crew's hours, which are given and are owed to nobody, and which Tams Quill is the only person in this building keeping a count of, and which are on the board, and which are not to be spent on anything without a named work and a person who has asked."

He put the chalk down and picked it up again, which is how Tams ended a figure.

"Thirty-eight and a half," he said. "And forty-six. And the room does not divide it, and I have stopped trying, and I would like it read that I said forty-six twice before I believed it."

---

Nobody had put a bell on the count yet, and that was a thing Pell Roke had said, and on the last morning of the week the boundary put one on it, and it was not a bell on a number.

It was a rota, and it was Mikka's, and it was people and not jobs, and it had two entries for the plinth of the stair and both of them were people who lived here, and Isel Ferrow was one of them, and the woman from the wash rooms was the other, and neither of them would hold the string, and the rota said so, and it said in Mikka's hand that a person who reads the plinth and cannot leave it is no use as a reader, and that the reader of the plinth and the reader of the string are two people for eight crossings.

Jo Verrick tried to argue and lost in one sentence.

"He is the boundary's," she said. "That is the whole of it and you have been the boundary's for nine weeks. I have watched six people learn that lesson in a room where nobody could teach it and one of us taught it by saying no, and it is the only thing I have ever been glad to lose."

And the terms for the route went up on a slate and not on the board, because the board was Jo's and the slate was Renn's, and Renn held it and wrote on it in her own hand and read it out loud in the anteroom and would not let anybody else read it for her.

And on the last line of the slate there was a line that Sefu Aran had not written and would not have written and had insisted upon after hearing the other one.

"Any of the four of us can shut it," the woman from the wash rooms said. "Me, and Ilsa, and Mikka, and Renn. That is right and I want it and I am not going to argue about it. And I want one thing added and I want to be asked to say it twice, because I have watched a thing become a closure in this building. When somebody shuts it, it is shut with a reason written down, and it is shut until somebody looks at the reason. It is not shut until somebody decides. It is not shut until it suits somebody. And if it shuts and stays shut, that is a decision, and a decision has a name on it and a date on it, and it is read out at the next bell like everything else in this room."

"You are refusing half your own power," Nera said, and said it carefully, because she was the one person in the room who knew exactly what a stop was worth.

"I am keeping half of it," the woman from the wash rooms said, "and I have been in this building for seven weeks and I have not said one word, and I am saying eleven now, and I am asking somebody to make me say them twice so that I know I meant them."

Dalla Sen read them back, and made her repeat them, and wrote them down in her hand, and then asked her, in front of the room, whether she wanted the line in her hand or in Renn's, and she said it had better be in a hand and not in Renn's, because Renn had a hand and a road and a line and a door and four people, and she had a mother and a room and a morning in which she lifted a cover, and she would not put this on the woman who already had everything.

So it went in her own hand, and it is the only line in that ledger that is in the hand of a person the crew has never once spoken to, and Dalla read it back twice, and nobody marked the slate, because a slate is not marked and is only agreed, and being agreed is a thing eleven people do out loud one at a time, and nobody in that anteroom was going to do it until the air had been read and the people who were going to live over it had been asked.

And on the anteroom bench there was no longer a plate, because the bench was needed, and the plate was on a shelf above it in a bundle of work cloth with the four corners tied off by Mikka's hand, and it had been on that bench for eight weeks and had had an empty seat in the middle of it and lines going out to supports that nobody in this building had identified, and it had not said one word about a stair, or a line, or a shore, or a man.

And on the ninth step, where the boundary stair began, the cord was where Renn Vale had put it, and the black cord came off the shore's head, up the open face of the wall, through the fixed rail, and back down to its own foot, and where it bore on the stone it had already laid down the first bright inch of a band that nobody in this building would be alive to see the whole of.
