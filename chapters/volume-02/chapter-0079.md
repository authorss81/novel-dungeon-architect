# Chapter 79 — Asking

On the Monday of the fifteenth week Dalla Sen read the third item on the review page at the fifth bell with a blank against it, the way she has read it every day for four weeks and the way she said in the twelfth week that she would go on reading it.

"*Somebody stands at the low end of the corridor and watches the water in the six minutes after the lever comes off, and there is nobody standing there.* The line is chalked on the frame and the frame is in this anteroom. **No name.**"

Nobody in the room said anything, because eleven people in this Hearthhold had spent thirteen weeks learning that a line read out without comment is the only way anybody has ever found to look at a thing like that, and a man who filled the space after it would be stealing it.

Then Marek Venn said, "Jo."

Jo Verrick was at the frame with her hand flat on the wood and she did not take it off.

"You heard it," she said.

"I heard it in a corridor on the last day of the twelfth week," said Marek, "and you said a name and then did not say it and then said it, and I have had it for a fortnight and I have not written it down and I have not said it in a room, and I have been walking about this building like a man carrying a piece of paper he is ashamed of, which is a thing I have done for thirty-two years and I am too old to be doing it in a cold month."

"And you want to ask her."

"I want to ask her." He had his right hand in his pocket and his left hand flat on the end of the bench, and he was not a man who took a hand out of a pocket in a room like this and everybody there knew it. "That is the sentence. Fifteen words, and I have never said it in a room in my life, and I got it off a man on a wheel who asked three of us for our afternoons by name on the second day of the twelfth week. **I am asking her.** And I want to do it properly, which in this building means I go to her door and I tell her what it is before I ask her, and if she says no then the line stays blank and Dalla reads it out tomorrow the same."

"Then go to the keeper first," said Renn Vale.

Marek turned round.

"You did not hear me," she said. "You were standing there having a good morning about a sentence. **I have refused the crew access to that woman and the refusal is not withdrawn, and I do not care that you are not going near her room, and I do not care that you are asking her about a corridor.** A post is a thing I hand out. That post has been on my page since the eleventh week with a blank against it and I have not filled it, and the air engineer put it there and would not fill it, and neither of them is wrong.

"So: you are going to ask a woman of this boundary for six hours a week on a day the school does not run, and if I do not go with you and set out what the thing is before you ask it, then in six weeks somebody in this room will say that you went round the keeper, and they will be right, and you will not be able to prove otherwise, and I have watched this boundary lose a woman once already over a rota and I am not having it twice."

---

So Renn Vale went with him as far as the clinic return, which is as far as she goes, and said the rest on the landing, in front of the clerk.

"Here is the post," she said. "It is on the air engineer's page and it has been since the eleventh week and it has never been filled. It is this: in the six minutes after the lever comes off the pump, the water in the dip comes up, and the low end of the corridor is two foot eight wide and stands on the edge of the shaft, and the shaft is eleven feet deep and has a faultform's cousin at the bottom of it that nobody has been near, and in those six minutes the water comes up under a floor that four people cross twice a week in a cold morning.

"And nobody is there. And it is not a post anybody can be told to take, because there is no instrument to read — the check is on one note and the vocabulary is dead — and so the only thing a person can do there is **watch water and shout**, and the shout goes to a corridor with a shut waist in it and a school on the far side, and a shout into a shut corridor is a shout into a wall.

"That is the whole of it. It is the worst post in this building and I have been keeping it blank for four weeks because the day I put a name on it is the day that person is a person who stands in cold water six times a week for the good of a floor."

"And the woman on the string is already on the stair six times a week," said Marek.

"Yes," said Renn Vale. "Which is why I have known since the fifth day of the twelfth week that that is the answer and have not written it down, and I would like you to understand what you are about to do."

---

He did it at the sixth bell on the Monday, at her door, alone, with no slate and nothing in either hand, and he said what the post was before he asked for it, in the order Renn Vale had given it, including the part about a shout going into a shut corridor, and he said it badly, and he knew he was saying it badly, and he said it anyway.

She listened to the whole of it with her hand on the door the way she had listened to the other half of her life in this Hearthhold, and when he had finished she said:

"No."

"May I say the rest of it before I go," said Marek Venn, "which is that I know what it is and I know what you are saying and I am not here to argue you out of it, I am here because a line on a page has been blank for three weeks and a woman said a name in a corridor and I am the only person who heard it, and I would rather it be a no that is on the page than a nothing that is on the page, and I would rather be the one who brought you the no than somebody else."

"You would," said the woman from the wash rooms, "and that is the second time you have said something decent in this building and I have not got a use for it, so we will both pretend you said something else."

Then she said the rest of it, and it took about ninety seconds, and she said it on her own step with her arms folded, and Dalla Sen was not there and there was no clerk and there was nothing in the room but the two of them, and none of the boundary was in it, and the whole of the boundary was told what she said afterwards in Renn Vale's words and the woman's, and it is in the book.

---

"No, and here is the reason, and it is not that I am frightened of it.

"That low end of the corridor is the loudest place in this Hearthhold. I know that. I have been in that corridor at the fifth bell every day for eleven weeks with a bucket and I have heard the water come up under that floor on the sixth day and the ninth and the twelfth and every single day since, and I cannot read a chalk line and I cannot hear a hiss on one note, and I have said that in a room and it is true, and nobody has been able to teach me anything in a month.

"**But I know what that corridor sounds like when it is filling faster than it usually fills, and I know it the way you know your own kitchen at night.** It is not a reading. It is a thing a person has been in a room for a long time.

"And that is the whole of what I can give and it is not a post and it is not a rota and I will not be a woman on a rota for this. So:

"**I will not stand at that low end six times a week. I will come, any day, at any hour, the moment that sound changes, and I will come once and I will look and I will shout if there is anything to shout about, and then it is over and I go back up to my covers.**

"And I want three things and I am writing them down before you say anything, because I have watched this room all week and I know what it does with a line that has a name on it.

"One. **Put no name against it.** Not on the review page and not on the board and not on a day sheet. If somebody in four years wants to know who stood at the low end on the nineteenth they can read that somebody came and looked, and that is all there is, and it will have to be enough, and I am not going to be a person in a book about a floor.

"Two. This is not a rota and it is not a rota and nobody is to make it one, and I know what *wet* means in a column this week, because I have heard all about the ninth line and I have heard the cook's book and I know exactly what those two things are and I am telling you now that a woman coming when a sound changes is not the same as a woman on a Wednesday, and if it becomes one then I will stop, and that is not a threat, it is a condition, and it is the same condition I said twice in my own hand in a colder month and I will say it a third time if I have to.

"Three. **Do not send anybody to make it true.** Do not send a person down that corridor to check on me and do not send a person down that corridor to check the water, and if somebody walks down there in the cold to see whether I came, then that is the day this stops and I will not tell you which day it was."

"Wrote it," said Renn Vale, from the landing, ten minutes later, in her own hand, in the words the woman gave, and made the clerk read it out, and read it out herself a second time.

---

"Two lines, and no name," said Nera Oduya, from the top of three steps, and she had been up there for the whole of it. "**The page stays blank and the page gets a sentence, and a sentence in a hand is a better thing than a name in a column, and I have been the one refusing to name a person on that line for four weeks and I have not been given anything to change my mind about.** Somebody who comes when a sound changes is not a holder. She is not on anybody's rota. She has refused a post, in her own words, at her own door, to a man who came and asked her properly, and it is the first refusal of a post anybody in this Hearthhold has ever been able to write down as a refusal instead of a gap."

"And the other one," said Bel Ordry. "The one on your own board. The air valve watch, and the name you said in a room on the second day of the twelfth week, and the door you went and stood at on the third day."

Nera did not move.

"It has not been asked," she said.

"Has it not."

"It has not been asked by anybody who has the right to ask it, which is me, and I have not asked it, and I will not have it asked by a room, and if somebody in this anteroom asks me in front of the clerk this morning I will say no and it will be entered that I said no.

"**The reason is that a person finds out about a post from the person who is handing it over, on a day when the handing is a choice, and not from a page in a week when it has already been decided.** And I have not had that day, and I am not going to have it in a room with eleven people in it, and I will say so out loud now and then I am going to go and stand somewhere else, and I would like it read at the fifth bell that I have refused to be asked in a room and that the reason is in my words."

"Entered and read out," said Dalla Sen, "and I am adding, in my own hand, that the clerk has now been asked to record a refusal to be asked, which is a first in thirteen weeks and is not in any column of this book including the one with no heading, and that is a hole in the record and I am not going to paper it over with a line."

---

Wen Pask had spent eleven days in this Hearthhold and eleven nights in a corner of a school store and he had not been asked for one hour of anything, and on the Tuesday morning he came to the end of the tally board and asked Tams Quill for a scrap of paper and a bit of pencil, which Tams Quill gave him, and then he stood at the end of the board and did not write on it, which is a thing he had watched a man do eleven times in thirteen weeks and had apparently learned from.

"This is not a column," he said. "I want to say that first, because I have watched him say it and I am twenty-two and I have had a trade and I know what a column is for. This is four days of a man at a store counting what comes up a road."

"Go on," said Tams Quill.

"**Forty pails.** Up the middle passage, into the top of the nine steps, on the four days I have got written down, and I have got who carried them, because it is four or five people and they are the same four or five people every day, and I have got that four of the five are the same people who stand on the stair, and one of them is fourteen and should not be carrying anything.

"And I have got the days, and I have got the rule, because a woman at a counter told it to me twice without being asked to. Twelve on an open day and eight on a shut one, and I have got two of the open ones and two of the shut ones in there, and it comes to forty, and I worked it out twice because I did not believe the first one.

"And the count is right and it is wrong, and it is wrong in a way I could not have found out on a road. I have been counting pails and not days. I have got a heading and the heading is *pails up*, and I know already that the heading is wrong, and I would like somebody to tell me what a pail is, because I have been a man with a page and I have got no idea what my page is for."

"A pail is water on a shoulder," said Mikka Hale, from the frame, "and it is not a day and it is not an hour, and there is a column on that board with nothing over the top of it and nineteen lines in it and a boy of fourteen in it, and you have just described a different column and you have described it better than a woman with a board did in a fortnight."

Wen Pask looked at the scrap of paper for a moment.

"Right," he said. "Then it is not mine to head, and I will take it off. But I have got one more thing and it is the reason I asked for the paper, and it is not about pails.

"There is a **hold on the middle gate's timber.** Not on the road, not on the men. On the timber itself, in the store, four hundred paces of seasoned hardwood for a sill, and there is a water-lock keeper in that gate with a name and he has put a hold on the store's own wood while his lock is dry, which is sensible and is not my business. Except that he has sent a message up the middle passage, three times, to two different people, and the message is not about wood.

"The message is: **has this Hearthhold said it will not come.**"

The anteroom went quiet in the way it has gone quiet about four times in thirteen weeks.

"Because he has been holding that timber for a dry water-lock since before the collapse, and it is not his timber, it is held against the work, and every month it sits there the whole of the middle knows somebody is coming for it and planning around it. And nobody in that gate has ever had a piece of paper saying *stop holding it.* I asked at the counter on my fourth day and the woman there said the same words three times, and the third time she said them slower, which is how I know it is not a thing anybody there is proud of.

"So," said Wen Pask, "**has this boundary said it will not come, or has nobody ever thought to say it, because it is not in a book and I have been in eleven rooms of this Hearthhold in eleven days and I have not found it in one of them.**"

Marek Venn stood at the end of the board and understood, with a clarity that was almost restful, that the whole of what he had to bring to a boundary in the fifteenth week was going to be a question asked out loud, and that it was not going to be asked about a floor or a lip or a post, and that the answer was going to cost this Hearthhold the only piece of wood it had ever been able to name.

He said nothing yet. He was going to say it in a room, in the morning, and he was going to be prepared to be told no, and he had a week of practice at it.
