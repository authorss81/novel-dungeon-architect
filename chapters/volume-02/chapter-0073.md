# Chapter 73 — Wet

On the Wednesday of the thirteenth week the school came through the corridor at a walk as it had come through for four days in seven, and there is a thing nobody outside that building knows about a school corridor, which is that it is not a corridor. It is a queue with opinions in it, and the opinions travel at about the speed of a nine-year-old.

Ilsa Norr stood at the school door and counted it in the way she counts it, and then she went back along the corridor about forty paces to where the wall was and put two fingers on the stone at about the height of a child's shoulder, and left them there, and thought about a column on a dowel for about ninety seconds.

Dalla Sen came and found her there at the fourth bell, which is the hour Dalla Sen came and found people.

"You have touched that wall every morning since the seventh week," she said.

"I have touched that wall every morning since the seventh week," Ilsa agreed, "and I have never once known what I was touching, and yesterday I found out that the man who drew the plan that this building is was thinking about this wall, and that he wrote *wet* in a column about it, and that the weather has been on the same side every single day since the collapse and has not once given me a day to myself."

"Entered," said Dalla Sen.

"Write this part too," said Ilsa Norr, and made her do it, and the part was: *the schoolteacher, on the fourth bell of the third day, with two fingers on the corridor wall, said that she had touched it every morning since the seventh week and had not known what she was touching, and that she now does, and that she is not sure that is better.* And Dalla Sen read it back and Ilsa Norr made her read it twice, which she has been doing since the seventh week and which nobody in that building remarks on any more.

---

Here is the arithmetic, and it is short, and Ilsa Norr did it standing at her own door with a piece of chalk.

The school runs four days in seven. The four days are the days Renn Vale holds the wet seam, because the corridor is open on those days and shut on the other three, and a school cannot be in a shut corridor. That has been true for two weeks and it was agreed in a room in nine minutes between school crossings, and Ilsa Norr agreed to it herself, out loud, in the worst hour of her week, and asked for the words *nobody asked me* to be in the book, and they are in the book.

And the plan says the school is kept on *wet*.

"And I have had the wet days in front of me for a fortnight," she said, "in Renn Vale's own hand, at the fifth bell, every day, in the same line as her seventh term and the lip — the wet seam, four days in seven, the corridor open, the school through, the water in. And I read that list at the fifth bell every day of the second week and I read a line in it that said the same thing as a line in a drawer of mine, and **I did not see it, because I was looking at it as a rota, and it was not a rota.**"

She put the chalk down.

"So the question I have to ask this room is not *is the plan right about my school*. **The question is which of the two of us is the independent one.** Either a woman I have never met wrote *wet* in a column thirty years or more before there was a school in this Hearthhold, in a season when this corridor flooded, and the water comes up it four days in seven now for exactly the same reason it did then, and every day this week I put nine children through a door that the weather decides. Or the weather does not decide anything and a rota does, and the rota is four days in seven, and it is four days in seven because a woman is standing on a piece of fill with a bad boot, and **that is what has been keeping my school since the sixth week of the thirteenth, and it is a pair of boots, and the boots are the whole of the plan.**

"And I have been saying in this room since the sixth day of the twelfth week that the school used to run when I said it ran, and I said it in the anteroom and I said it in my own words and I put it in the book, and I said it as the finding of a woman who has been running a school under a collapse for two months. And I cannot say it this week. Not honestly. Not when there is a page in a store that agrees with a rota on a landing, and I cannot tell which of the two wrote the other one down."

---

"Which is why it needs saying out loud," said Dalla Sen, "and then it needs saying out loud again, because the second one is the one that costs you."

"I know it is," said Ilsa Norr.

"Say both."

"That is what I have just said." She was standing very still by the school door with her hands at her sides. "Say it in the book, both halves, in my words: *the school now runs on the wet days, the wet days are Renn Vale's days, the page and the rota agree, and the schoolteacher does not know which of them she is obeying, and has stopped being able to say in this room that the school is hers.* And put the time on it, and read it at the fifth bell on the last day of the week, once, and not every day, because I have had my cost read on a schedule before and I am not having it read on a schedule twice."

"It goes in at the fifth bell on the last day of the week," said Dalla Sen, "in your words, once, and not every day, and I am putting under it in my own hand that the schoolteacher has been the only person in this Hearthhold who could say *this is mine* about a room, and has said it four times in eleven weeks, and has withdrawn it once."

"Mikka, are you going to say something," said Dalla Sen, "because you have been standing at that frame for a minute and a half doing arithmetic, and it is your arithmetic and everybody can see it."

---

Mikka Hale had been standing at the frame with a cloth that she was not using, and she came three steps out into the room, which is as far as Mikka Hale has come into the middle of the anteroom in thirteen weeks.

"Right," she said. "Then here it is, and I have been at it since last night, and it is not about the school and it is about a book, and it is the worst hour of work I have done in eleven weeks and I have done a lot of them.

"I started the book. Monday. The one I announced in the anteroom with the corridor shut, the one with the date and the name and the pails. I have got three days of it in it and I have read a week out at the fifth bell as I said I would, and I want to tell this room what is on the page before somebody looks over my shoulder, because it is a bad page and I made it.

"Monday. Four people carried water up the middle passage and as far as the top of the nine steps. **Tuesday. Four people. Wednesday. Four people, and one of them was fourteen with a splinted ankle who stood at the top of the nine steps for two hours and handed pails on to people who could carry them, because a pail does not need to be carried to be counted, and I made him do it, and he did it, and his name is in my book as a carrier's name and he is not a carrier, and I am not going to forget it and neither is he.**"

Pell Roke, on the bench at the top of the nine steps with his crutch across his knees, said nothing at all, and Mikka did not look at him while she said it, and everybody in the room understood that she had not looked at him on purpose.

"So: the date, the name, the pails. Three columns. And on Monday night I sat down and I read what I had written, and I read a line of it, and then I did a thing I want to admit to in a room.

"**I went and got the plan's description out of Dalla's book and I copied the shape of it.** Not the words. The shape. **How many, and when, and what is done.** And my book is now three columns and the third one is empty and I filled it in, because I have got three days and I know exactly what is done, it is water, and I wrote *water* in it forty-one times in three days.

"And then I sat there at the second bell on Wednesday night with a good full third column, and I understood what I had done, and I want to say it in this room before Dalla reads it out and makes it sound better than it is."

"Go on," said Dalla Sen.

"**I have built a page with a room on it and a count and a condition and no work in it.** And it is not nine lines in a store, it is three days on my own board, and the count on my lines is one, because there is one person carrying the water on any given day, and the condition is *wet*, and the work is *water*, and I have written it down in my own hand in a book in my own kitchen and **I have become a woman who keeps a schedule, and I did it in three days, and I did it because somebody showed me a better way of keeping a building and it is nine hundred years older than me and it is on a dowel.**

"And the whole reason I started the book was the opposite. I started it because a favour is a loan with no date on it. I wrote that in this room. *A favour is a loan with no date on it and this room is being kept on favours and one day somebody is going to stop and they are not going to tell us.* That was the argument, and I have been right about it, and it is still true, and I have gone and put a date on it anyway."

"Read it," said Dalla Sen, and read it, and Mikka Hale made her read the last sentence twice, and the second reading was in a voice nobody in the anteroom had heard the clerk use before.

"Then what is in the book now," said Bel Ordry, from the side.

"**The same thing, with a date on it.**" Mikka said it flatly. "That is all I have got. It is the same favour and now there is a day it happened on. And a day is not a favour. A day is a **shift**. And a shift needs a person on it, and a person on a shift is a person who can be asked why they were not there, and that is not a favour any more, that is a rota with a cook's handwriting on it, and it is my handwriting, and there is no difference at all between my book and the ninth line of a thing I have not seen in my life, and I built mine."

Nobody said anything for a while. Down under the floor the pump came on and did its twenty-six strokes and came off after six minutes, and the check went to the one note it has gone to all winter, and a woman with a bad foot stood at the top of the nine steps with a book and a boy of fourteen on a bench and eleven weeks of the same sentence in the landing's chalk, and the dip held its four inches under the line a slinged woman marked there in the ninth week and has held them ever since.

---

"Stop there," said Renn Vale, from the upper landing, and it was the first time all afternoon that anybody had heard the note in her voice that the crew had learned to listen for, which is not anger and is not alarm and is a woman deciding that a thing has gone past the point where it can be improved.

"Mikka, your three columns are yours and I am not going to take them off you and I am not going to argue with a word of them. Keep the book. Read it out. Put the dates in. Say the pails. You are right that a favour with a date on it is not a favour, and I have been wrong in this room about plenty of things in eleven weeks and I would rather you had said it than me.

"And I am going to say one thing about your *when*, and then I am going to go and stand somewhere else.

"Your *when* is *wet*. On the days the corridor is open. Which is four days in seven. Which is my days. **So a book of favours written by a cook in a kitchen is now going to have a rota in it that is made of my boots, and the water is going to arrive on the days I can stand on that fill, and if one morning in March there is a person on that fill who is not me, then your book records it as a wet day, and nobody reading it in a year will ever know that the word *wet* means a woman with a bad foot on a piece of ground that has moved.**

"That is what I am objecting to and I am not objecting to the book. I am objecting to the fact that a condition has stopped meaning the weather and started meaning a rota, and that happened in this building on the fourth day of the eleventh week, when a woman said *not Friday, now*, and everybody in this room knew it at the time and nobody wrote it down, and now there is a page in a store with a word on it that means the same thing, and that is how you find out you have built a cage out of a duty and thought you were building a hearth."

"Wrote that," said Dalla Sen, "in Renn Vale's words, in her own hand, with the time on it, and read out again at her request, and I am adding in my own hand that the *when* column of the kitchen's book of favours and the *when* condition on the ninth line of a document in a locked store now mean the same four days out of seven, and that this is a fact about a rota and not about the weather, and that it has been a fact about a rota for a fortnight and this is the day it was written down."

---

Marek Venn had been standing at the end of the tally board for an hour with a blank slate and had understood, some time around the third reading, that the thing in the room was going to be decided by a woman with a cloth in her hand and not by him, and that this was the fourth time in thirteen weeks and that he was getting better at it and worse at liking it.

What he could not put down, and did not write, and did not say to anybody, was this.

Eight rooms. A count and a condition. **No work in the third column at all.** And the reading of that, the one every person in that anteroom had walked past twice in an hour, was that whoever made that list thought the keeping of a room was *how many people are in it and what the weather is doing*, and that everything else — the keeping, the sweeping, the counting, the reading out, the arguing, the standing on a lip at five in the morning — was not the list's business. It was the list's *given*.

And every one of them in that anteroom had spent the last hour arguing about the third column, and he had understood in about nine seconds that the third column was the entire content of their lives, and that the man who made the list had not needed it, and that this was either a very old civilization being right about something or the oldest surviving instruction anybody in this Hearthhold has ever read.

And then underneath that, in the place where he kept the things he was not allowed to say: *the anteroom is on that page.* Line four, or line five, or six. It did not matter which. It is a room in this Hearthhold with a count and a condition, and it has been in this book's continuous use for eleven weeks with about nine people in it, which is more than any line on that page has ever had, by the look of the numbers, and **the five of them are not on the page anywhere, and the stair is not on the page except as the way down to a room nobody is in, and the page does not know they came.**

He put the slate down on the shelf, blank side up, for the fourth time in twelve days, and stayed where he was.

And at the top of nine steps, on the bench, a boy of fourteen with a splinted ankle said the only sentence in the whole of that afternoon that nobody in the anteroom had thought of, and he said it into the room without raising his voice, and Dalla Sen wrote it down before she had decided to.

"**The ninth line has got a one in it.** Nobody has said out loud all day that the ninth line has got a one in it. It has got a count and it has got a *none* and it has got nothing in the work column, and it has got a person in it, and if it is *none* that means there is no day, and if there is no day and there is a person, then that is not a room that nobody works in. **That is a room with a person posted to it and no work, and we have all been in this room for two hours talking about whether anybody does anything in it.**"
