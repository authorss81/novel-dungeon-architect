# Chapter 197 — Every Number In That Is True

A Friday, and the corridor open, and the fortieth morning of the school in the room at the kitchen-line end, and the nine went up the nine steps at the first bell and round the party wall and into that room. The anteroom was then emptied of every person in it, last one first, and it stayed emptied until the reading was finished.

The anteroom's terms were said before it was emptied and not after, and the count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change, and the outer service door was the one a body went out of, and the fifth of those six terms was in a room with both the people it names in it, and the pump was attended. Dalla Sen entered that the anteroom had been emptied at the first bell for a reading that was not going to take place in it, and that the reason was given in a room before anybody emptied anything. A room emptied for a thing that then happens somewhere else is a room emptied for a plan. There was no plan and no document and no heading, and she entered the emptying anyway, because she enters the things she does.

Renn Vale put her ear to the party wall at the first bell, the way she does on an open morning, for about as long as a sentence takes to say, four times over, and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, and left it there. A figure of nothing went into the book against it, and into the space where a reason goes the fact that this was the seventh morning out of seven, and that she had stopped expecting it to be anything else, and that this was a change in her and not in the wall.

Then the room decided where the reading was to happen, and it took the whole of the space between the first bell and the second, and the reason was given in a room by the people who had the reason.

"**Not in here,**" from the fourth step. "**And I will give the reason before anybody gives a better one. A number said out loud in this anteroom becomes a number this room has agreed to, and a cook in this building said that to me on the third day of last week and she was right. And one of the two figures on that stranger's sheet is a ten for this room. A figure of ten read out in the room it is a figure of is a figure this room has said about itself, and this room has never counted anybody and it is not starting on a Friday morning because a piece of printed paper is in the building.**"

"**And the second half of it,**" from the step, "**which is the half I entered in this room's own book on the sixth day of the thirty-fourth week, and I am entering it again in the same words because I want it to be findable. The room that can hear a comparison is a room that has not had a stranger's seal in it. The sheet has a wax head at each end of it and a seal number under a clerk's name and it has been lying on that table since the fifth day of the thirty-fourth week. It stays on that table.**"

"**Then what is being read,**" from the bottom of the steps.

"**Six rows on a slate in your hand,**" said Bel Ordry. "**Six rows of a district, written out by you on the fourth day of the thirty-fourth week and read back against the sheet by a woman who reads walls and did not look at the sheet while she did it. A slate has no wax on it. A slate is a thing anybody in a room can pick up. And this morning you are going to read it out again from the same hand, and Jo is going to check it again against the same hand, and neither of you is going to look at the sheet.**"

"**And the half against that, which is mine and which I am obliged to put in the room,**" and he went on, "**is that a copy is a copy and a copy can be wrong, and that the whole of what was just found out about a piece of paper is that a copy of a true thing is still a true thing and not a count. So the room is going to act on a copy today, on a slate, in a room with no seal in it, and the reason it is going to do that is that a room will not act on a document. And I do not know whether that is a victory or the most expensive kind of losing, and I have been in this building thirty-seven weeks and I have not found out.**"

"**Then it is a common landing,**" said Renn Vale, "**and there is no wax on a common landing, and there is a wall in a common landing with five and a half shores and a spreader under the head of the sill and the sixth place empty and a brace of a school's own beech pushing on it from the other side, and a bad repair on one face only, and nine children on the other side of two feet of it, and we are going to read a number out loud underneath nine children, and I would like somebody to tell me that this is a good morning to do it on and I have not found anybody.**"

"**I will tell you it is a bad one,**" from the wall, with his back against it. "**And the room is going to do it anyway, and I am going to say why in one sentence and then I am going to stand out of the way of it. A figure about a room that nine children are in is not a figure that ought to be read in another building.**"

"**Then the common landing,**" and she said it as though it had been decided by somebody else, "**and the anteroom stays empty, and the two of you go up with the slate and the page, and the page goes in the coat and the coat goes up the steps, and it does not leave this building and it has never left this building and it is not leaving it today.**"

Bel Ordry took the coat off the hook and did not take the page out of it, and put the coat over his arm, and the two of them went up the nine steps together with a slate and a closed page, and the corridor was open and the nine were not in it and the anteroom was empty behind them.

Emrys Callow read the six rows out loud in his own mouth, from the slate, in the order Dalla Sen had written them in, and not in an order that made them better. Jo Verrick stood beside him with her own hand on the slate and checked every figure as he gave it, and read each one back against the page in the coat without opening the page further than the coat allowed, because the page is in four parts and only the first part was out.

"**One, a dwelling room over a cookhouse, permitted eight. Two, a store with a dry vent, permitted twelve. Three, a room with a hearth and a floor of beaten earth, permitted six. Four,**" said Emrys Callow, and his voice did not change, "**a room with nine hearth stones in it, permitted ten.**"

Jo Verrick checked it. "**Ten,**" she said. "**And our first column for that room is ten. It has been ten since the second week and it is a permission and it has never held a count of anybody, and I have read that room's floor sixteen times and I could not tell you how many children were on it this morning, and those are two different instruments and this morning is the first morning I have been in a room where both of them were being read out at once.**"

"**Five,**" said Callow. "**A hall with a double door, permitted twenty. And six,**" and he stopped for about a second, which nobody in that landing remarked on, "**a room with a pump in it and a bench along one wall, permitted ten.**"

"**Ten,**" said Jo Verrick. "**And our first column for that room is ten. Same figure, same kind of figure, made in the same shape, for the same reason, and the reason ours is there is a reason and it is not an accident, and a correct reason is not a defence and I am not going to enter it as one.**"

"**And the four with nothing on the other side,**" said Callow. "**One, two, three and five. Four of the six have no counterpart here, and that is not a fault in either document, and this boundary has sixteen rows and the district has more rooms than that, and the difference is a different size of claim and not a mistake in either of them.**"

"**And the rest of it,**" said Dalla Sen, from the landing, reading the legend off her own slate, "**in my own hand and not improved. No surveyor of that office has entered any room shown. Every figure is the last certified permitted occupancy of that room. And then, printed on the sheet itself, in the fourth line of its own legend, the sentence that the office which printed it certifies permissions and does not count.**"

"**Then the whole of it is true,**" and he did not come down the steps.

"**Every figure on it is true,**" from the landing, with the slate in both hands, "**and no surveyor who made it has ever stood in one of the rooms it names, and the word *falsified* is in this clerk's book in this clerk's hand from the fourth day of the thirty-fourth week with the objection to the entry written underneath it, and I am not striking the entry and I am not striking the objection, and I am standing on a common landing with a slate in my hand and I am telling this room that neither of them is the finding.**"

"**Then somebody had better give the finding,**" and he put the slate down on the landing when he had finished with it, "**because I have read six numbers out loud underneath nine children and I have not said one thing that was not on a piece of paper.**"

And Nella Bray said it.

She had been standing at the head of the nine steps with her slate under her arm the whole of it, and she came up the last two steps, and she did not come further, and she said it into a landing with five and a half shores in it, and she said it in her own mouth and she said it the way a woman reads a line off a counter.

"**Every number in that is true and it is not a count, and I have been telling a room that for thirty weeks and nobody wrote it down.**"

Nobody on that landing spoke for about as long as four strokes of a pump, and there was no pump on a common landing, and the silence was the whole of the time there was.

While it was being read, the rest of the building went on being a building, and the clerk entered that afterwards because she could not enter it at the time. Nine children were in a room on the other side of two feet of wall being taught something by a woman who had not asked what was happening on the landing, and the elected keeper of this boundary was on the step above the anteroom with his hands behind his back, and he had not come up. A cook was at a pot with two days in it. Six people who are not this building's were in two rooms off the far end of an anteroom that had been emptied and was being filled again, and one of the two rooms was in a page nobody could read, and the six of them did not know that a sentence about documents had been said on a landing eleven feet above their heads. And a woman who certifies the air in nine rooms of this Hearthhold was on the third step with a valve board against her hip, and she had heard all of it, because the common landing is not a place people cannot hear from.

Then Bel Ordry gave the half against, and he gave it in the standing position at the top of the nine steps, and he gave it before anybody could improve on it.

"**A sentence in a room is a room,**" he said. "**It is not a letter and it is not a form and it does not travel. And in about ten weeks a man in an office four days up that passage is going to open a file about the ninth district and the sheet is going to be in it, and he is going to write that a district's cleared-risk map is unreliable, and he will be right about the reliability and wrong about the reason, and the sentence he writes will be about a map.**"

"**And then,**" he said, "**the map gets reprinted, and a reprinted map has a new edition on it, and the new edition has a room in it that somebody has put a figure in, and the figure will be a count, because that is what a count is for, and every count in that district from that morning on will be a number that a printer chose in a town this building has never been to. So a true thing said out loud on a common landing this morning is the beginning of a worse map, and I do not know of any instrument that stops that.**"

"**There is one,**" from the second step, and she did not come up any further. "**and I know it because I have watched it be done twice, and neither time it was done in a room.**"

"**Then the room gets the instrument,**" from the head of the steps.

"**Two seals,**" said Nella Bray. "**Withdrawn, at a counter, by a person, on a form, for reasons the person has said out loud in a room first, and the reasons have to be good enough that the person saying them does not lose their hands. That is how it is done. Not a sentence. A withdrawal, at a counter, by somebody who can be refused, and the refusal is a thing that has to be asked for out loud in a room as well, because the office will not refuse you in front of you.**"

"**And what does a person do when they are refused,**" from the top of the steps, and he asked it as a man who has watched it happen twice and not helped.

"**They come back the next open day with the same reason,**" said Nella Bray, "**and they are refused again, and they come back, and nobody in that office is a villain, and every one of them is a person with a wage and a family and a form that says what a building is permitted to hold, and the whole of the mechanism is that a person has to be embarrassed enough to keep turning up.**"

"**Then none of that is done today,**" and she said it from the fourth step with the slate under her arm, "**and it is not done this week by anybody in this building, and it is owed, and it is owed out loud now, in a room, by the person who has just named it, and I would like a clerk to enter that it was named and that nobody has started it and that the naming is not a plan.**"

"**Entered,**" and she wrote it standing up, which she has not done in a room in three weeks, "**in a counter clerk's words, and entered that it is owed, and entered that nobody has started it, and entered that the naming of a thing is not a plan and that this building has been confusing the two instruments for thirty-seven weeks and that a man in a room four days up that passage is going to be asked to stamp two pieces of wax and is not going to know that he is the last person in this story who can.**"

The clerk entered the length of it while the landing was still being talked over, and the length of it was eleven minutes, and she entered the eleven minutes as hers and not as the reading's, and entered the reason, which is that a good part of it was spent getting six rows onto a slate in her own hand and in the order they were written in, and that getting them onto a slate is one of the two jobs she has and the reading is not either of them.

Jo Verrick had not moved off the wall.

"**I have read that wall seventeen times,**" and she did not take her hand off it, "**and it is a quarter of an inch every time and it is the same every time, and I could not tell you how many people are in that anteroom this morning and I have never once been asked and I would have said no if I had been. And the string is not a figure of people. And I have been two different kinds of reader in this building for thirty-seven weeks and nobody ever put the two on the same table, and this morning somebody did, and one of them is on a piece of paper with wax on it and one of them is a piece of string in my apron.**"

Marek Venn had been at the top of the nine steps through all of it with his book shut under his forearm, and he had understood the sentence on the landing before the woman at the second step had finished saying it, and what he understood was not a fact about a district.

It was the reason a carpenter had given in a clerk's book the previous evening, in his own hand, in nine sentences, and it was the reason from the other end. A route-holder's hours go in a register against a named route, and a register shows them, and anybody can look at the register and see that I was there. That is a post. And a clearance figure is a permitted occupancy, and a permitted occupancy is what a room is allowed to hold and not what is in it, and that is a post too, and it is the same post, and it has been in two documents for thirty years and one of the documents was a page in a coat on a table in this building with no name anywhere on it.

The man who gave up a wage on the first and the fifteenth on Thursday evening had worked out where his own hours were counted, and the room he was standing in had just been told, by somebody who has never met him and does not know he exists, that the thing that counts his hours is the same kind of thing as the thing that counts a room. Two of them. A sheet with wax on it and a register four and a half days up a road. Both of them showing anybody who looks that I was there.

He looked down at the anvil end of the anteroom. Iven Tallo was standing at it with the chisel on it and his left hand open and flat, and he had not said one word on that landing and he was not going to. Nobody in that anteroom was going to thank him for it, and there was going to be no word for what he had done, and he had asked for that himself in a room on Thursday in a woman's mouth, and a woman from a counter had seen to it.

Marek Venn stood at the top of nine steps and understood that the sentence which had just ended a thing in this building had been said by a person who did not know she was saying it twice, and that it had been said twice because a carpenter had spent a fortnight not being asked.

Then Renn Vale went down to the party wall and put her ear against it on the anteroom side, and was there about the time it takes to say a sentence, four times over, and came back and gave the room what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, with seventeen people in the anteroom and a wax head lying on a table six steps away.

The sixth bell came and the clerk read the day's figure and the total out loud, both of them, and then read the count out loud after them, all three in one breath, and gave the reason herself: a room which has just been told a true number is not a count is owed three true numbers of its own.

Four today and two hundred and ninety-four since the keeper signed. The boundary is at four hundred and eleven and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen. Wen Pask is one hundred and thirty-two days and one hundred and thirty-two nights into a count that is his own, and it is not going to be one hundred and thirty-three, and she has been promising a room she would go on writing it down for forty-one weeks running, and nobody has thanked her in any of the forty-one, and she read it out and turned the slate face down over it.

The board was not walked, and the reason is that a room had spent the morning finding out what two of its own numbers were, and a board is a column, and a clerk does not walk a column in the same hour as a sentence like that one.

The pot is two days and the seam gave nothing on three mornings running and gave one this morning, and the four households at a second gate have nothing, and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody, and the anteroom of this building has a figure of ten on a stranger's sheet and no figure of its own and never has had one.

Then the anteroom filled up again, the nine came down the nine steps at the fourth bell and went out at the far door, and the terms were said again before it filled and not after. The count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change, the outer service door was the one a body went out of, the fifth of those six terms was in a room with both the people it names in it, and the pump was attended.

And at the seventh bell the anteroom had a hole in the corner of its floor and a trestle with two slates gone out of it into two pockets, and a chalk line on the table with nothing under it that has been there since the fifth day of the thirty-fourth week, and a stranger's sheet with a wax head at each end of it lying where it has lain for a fortnight. Nine sets of small footprints were in the dust of the floor going round a corner. A man with a shut book under his forearm was standing at the top of nine steps, and nobody in the building was saying one word about what had been read out loud on a common landing. The only thing that had moved all day was a woman from a counter up two steps, and a clerk with a slate in her hand, and a foreman who had read six numbers and one sentence.
