# Chapter 50 — The Second Threshold

Nobody crossed the stair until it had been read, and the reading was not the crew's, and getting the room to understand that took the better part of two days and most of what was said in it was said by people the crew had never once asked anything.

Nera Oduya did the air. That was hers and had been hers since the ninth day and no line in any record had ever moved it, and she did it standing at the valve with the cloth on the string and the board behind her and four of the four people who keep a room standing within reach of her, which was a thing she asked for and a thing Jo Verrick thought was a courtesy and had to be talked out of.

"You are not a courtesy to the room," Nera said. "You are the stop. If I say the word then the nearest of the four has to be able to be at the cloth, and the one who can be at it fastest is the one standing next to me, and that is not a courtesy, it is the difference between a stop and a hope."

Then she read her part, and she read it as conditions and not as a certificate, and she made everybody hear the difference.

"I am not certifying that stair," she said. "I have never been down it, I cannot see the bottom of the shaft, and there is a reading on my board in a column nobody reads that I do not understand, and if somebody in this room wants a piece of paper with my name at the bottom of it saying the air in that stair is good, then they want a different air engineer and I will not be offended and I will not write it. What I have instead is four things, and they are conditions, and a condition is something somebody does, not something I promise."

She held up the cloth.

"One: the stair is on the boundary's air and it is read at the second bell and the fifth, by a person, out loud, to a person, and not to a page, and the rota for that is Mikka's and it is people and not jobs. Two: the stair-side grille stays shut. It has never once been opened and it is not going to be opened, and the reason is in the twenty-ninth day and the reason has not got better with time. Three: on any day the stair is used, the pump is attended, the outer service door is open, and one of the two of us is able to stop it. Four: the bypass hatch stays latched. It has stayed latched through every turn for nine weeks and the reason is that the stair pushes through that shaft, and if anybody in this building ever wants to open it, the answer is no, and it is mine to say and not yours to decide."

"Read the fourth of them again," Sefu said, "and say whose air that is."

"It is mine," Nera said. "It is not a room anybody sleeps in, it is a shaft nobody can see into, and it is the only piece of this work that I will not let anybody else have an opinion about at the same time as me."

Then Sefu Aran read his paragraph, separately, and had it marked separately, and would not have it argued with, and it had one sentence in it that was new and that he had read out twice to make sure of it.

*That a route in this boundary is opened and shut by the people who keep the rooms it is under, and by nobody else, and that six people from above have built a part of it and do not hold any of it, and cannot open it, and cannot close it, and cannot be asked to be on a rota that would let them, and that this is not a courtesy to them and it is not protection for them and it is a boundary.*

"That is mine," he said, "and Jo has it marked separately, and I have asked for one more thing in it and Dalla has written it and I am going to read it out."

*That the day this route is first used for anything other than air and its own testing, it is on the clerk's page, with the name of the person who shut it and the name of the person who opened it, and the reason, and the date.*

"So somebody can see when it is being used and how often," he said, "and so that a route that is quietly becoming a habit is a route that somebody can point at, and a habit with a date on it is a thing this boundary can vote about and a habit with no date on it is just a thing that happened to everybody."

---

The occupancy check was not done by anybody from above and it was the part of it that actually mattered, and it went wrong twice in the ordinary way and it was passed on the third attempt by six people who were going to sleep over it.

Ilsa Norr and Mikka Hale and Renn Vale and the woman from the wash rooms walked it, room by room, on the day, with Dalla Sen and a slate, and they did not take a number first. They took the number at the door.

"Twenty-nine was a photograph," Ilsa said, when Jo Verrick offered the count from the counting day. "I am not going to be photographed at my own door. We will count what walks."

They counted thirty on the day of the check, and it was thirty, and the count is in the ledger in Ilsa Norr's own words and Dalla Sen's hand, and there is no number on any page in this building that purports to know how many people live here, and the reason is on the page in a schoolteacher's handwriting and it is four words long: *a building does not announce itself.*

And then the terms, on Renn Vale's slate, read out one at a time, and agreed out loud one at a time, because that is the only way a slate gets marked.

Four at a time, and four minutes, and nobody on the stair in the six minutes after the lever comes off.

A bearer at the near lip for the whole of any crossing, and the bearer is named on the day's board, and the bearer is a person of this boundary, and the bearer is not a person who is doing something else.

The reader at the plinth, and the reader at the string, and they are two people for eight crossings, and a person who reads the plinth and cannot leave it is no use as a reader.

The plinth reader's word is a warning and not a stop, and the stop is the cloth and the cord and any of the four.

The closure: any of the four may shut it, and the shut carries a reason in the person shutting it's own hand, and it is shut until somebody looks at the reason, and if it stays shut then that is a decision with a name and a date and it is read out at the next bell.

And the sixth, which is Mikka Hale's, because she asked about a handcart on the second morning of the route's life and was refused in reasons and not in laughing, and asked that it be written down that she had asked once: *the route is not for a cart. Four at a time is a rule about people, and every number in that route is a number about bodies.*

Renn read those six out and Ilsa said yes to the third of them and Mikka said yes to the fourth and to the sixth, and the woman from the wash rooms said yes to the sixth and made them repeat it, and Dalla read the whole slate back and Sefu marked his own paragraph separately, and there were eleven people in the anteroom and every one of them said the word out loud on their own line, and Jo Verrick's board has six names on it and none of them are the crew's, and the crew's names are on the page that says what work was done and by whom and at whose cost, and there are two pages and they are kept apart, and the page about the work is the crew's and the page about the route is the boundary's, and Dalla Sen read that aloud as well because she said in the twenty-fourth day that a decision that does not say where it stops has been made to last.

---

It was shut once, in the middle of the check, by the person nobody had spoken to for seven weeks, and the reason was written in her own hand and the check waited.

The mother had gone off again in the night, which is what a body of seventy does sometimes, and the woman was going to be at the second-bell cover check and then at the fifth, and she was not going to stand on a stair between them, and Mikka's own term from the thirty-eighth day said the words *no work while the covers are being lifted* in Mikka Hale's own hand, and nobody had to be told anything.

So she shut it, at the top of the three steps, with her own hand on the cord, and she said the word, and Isel Ferrow at the top of the nine steps rang the bell, and the anteroom cleared to the outer step in a minute and a quarter, and the four people went back to their rooms, and the pump came off the lever and the corridor emptied and the anteroom had its ten back, and the check stopped.

Dalla Sen wrote it down and read it back: *Shut by the woman from the wash rooms, at the top of the three steps, on her own hand on the cord, and by her own word. Reason in her hand: I am not on that stair between the second bell and the fifth and the terms say so in Mikka's words and I am not going to be a person who breaks a term to make a check finish.*

Nobody in the anteroom said the word *careful* to her. That was arranged before the check by Mikka Hale, in the yard or the corridor or wherever it was she said it, and it is not in the ledger, and the reason it is not in the ledger is that Mikka Hale said that a rule you have to write down to get obeyed is a rule somebody will break, and a rule you say out loud to one person in a corridor gets kept by about four people in a room, and that is the whole of the civic technology she has.

It cost the boundary a second pump interval, which cost a half-day of a shutter route that pays Jo Verrick, and it cost a day of Ilsa Norr's school, and it cost about four hours of six people's lives, and it is in the ledger, and nobody in that ledger has any way of making it come out even, and it is the first entry in it that is a cost with no offsetting line under it at all.

They opened it at the fourth bell, because the woman came and looked at the reason she had written and the reason had been answered — a second pair of hands in the wash room for the fifth-bell check, arranged by Mikka at about the third bell, with a name attached to it, so that it would be a person and not a rota — and she untied the cord herself and re-tied it herself and said the word and it went on.

"The first time anybody shuts that thing," Renn Vale said, afterwards, to nobody, "it was shut for a reason nobody would have thought of, and the reason was a woman who is not on any page, and it cost us a half-day and a school day, and it was right. Write that down, Dalla. That is the one I want read in a year."

"It is in," Dalla Sen said.

---

And then the hand-over, which was not a ceremony and had no speech in it, and which took the form of a page and a slate and a valve board and an hour.

Nera Oduya's board stays hers. Jo Verrick's board stays hers, and the plan in Jo's hand is the plan, and the six names on the route's slate are the boundary's, and Renn Vale's slate is the route. Dalla Sen keeps the ledger and the closure dates. Mikka Hale keeps the pot, the nineteen days, the flue wall she chose to watch, and the cover rota. Isel Ferrow has the mark and is not to be first at anything for a month, and is, as of the fourth of this month, the first reader of a stair in this Hearthhold, and has been told so by name and has not been told what to do about it. The woman from the wash rooms lifts the cover at the second bell and says a word and shuts a route, in her own hand, with a reason.

And the crew.

Marek Venn read the tally out, because it was his to read and not Tams's, and Tams had said so at the fourth bell and had made him say it out loud in front of the room first, and he read it wrong twice and Tams corrected him twice in front of everybody and let him finish both times.

"Thirty-eight and a half hours," he said, "of work given, owed to nobody, on a project that is not a maintenance party and is not on a rota and is not a charter, done by six people who are going to leave."

"And the obligation," said Dalla.

"And the obligation," he said, "is this, and I am going to say it badly and then Jo will say it properly. The shore on the common landing has to be looked at. The line that holds it wears. The stair's air has to be read twice a day. The pump's seat has two cleanings on it or fewer. There is a length of timber under six feet of water with a number chalked on it and there is no post under the near landing's lip and there is no fourth wedge for the school-door head. And the six of us have a maintenance obligation on that list and it is bounded and it is written and it is not ours beyond the writing."

Jo Verrick read the obligation in her own hand, and it was one paragraph, and it ended with a sentence she had insisted on and Dalla had resisted and Sefu had overruled Dalla on.

*That the six of them hold the maintenance of what they built until the boundary can do it without them and a person named by the boundary has said so out loud, in this room, with the reason; and that until that is said they are not tenants, not neighbours, and not residents, and no hour of it is owed, and the page that says what work they did is the crew's and the page that says what the route is is the boundary's, and the two pages are not the same page and are kept apart by the clerk and read back separately.*

"Thirty-eight and a half hours is not the whole of it," Tams said. "It is the part I can count."

"Nobody is asking you to count the rest of it," Marek said, and then, because he was who he was, and because he had been the man who wanted to hold a thing himself for nine weeks: "The rest of it is a man on a lever for three weeks and a woman with a bell and a boy with an ear and a woman who said no in a room. I do not know what to do with that and I have stopped trying, and I would like it read into the record that I stopped trying, and that I stopped trying on a day in the ninth week because a woman at a threshold said no to six strangers, and not because I understood anything."

---

The plate went with them, because evidence goes with the people who found it, and it was tied into a bundle of work cloth on the sixth morning with the corners knotted off by Mikka Hale's hand, and Dalla Sen wrote the transfer out in full, and it is the last entry in the ledger and it is the shortest.

*One Cairn plate, gray-green, wrapped, carried since the sixth week by the crew of the Marrowgate public-works survey, on a spread of work cloth, under a bench and then on a shelf. Unexplained. Not identified, not used, not opened, not claimed. It goes with the crew because the crew found it and nobody else has ever been asked about it. It is not evidence of anything this boundary has decided and it is evidence of something nobody in this building has read yet, and both of those are in the same line.*

The brass survey rule went into the same pocket as the plate, and it had not said a word in nine weeks, and it did not say one now, and Marek did not put his hand on it, and there is nobody left to tell whether that was restraint or luck.

On the anteroom bench there was a bare place where the plate had lain, and Jo Verrick left it bare, and Tams Quill put the tally board back over it, because the bench was needed and the board was the record and the record was the right thing to have where the evidence had been, and nobody in that room said anything about that either.

---

They came out of the Cinder Steps in the ninth week and it had been nine weeks, and the question of going back down had never once been put to them by anybody in this Hearthhold, in nine weeks, by anybody at all.

The stair went down. Four treads, and then dark, and then the shaft, and the shaft went down to the pump gallery, and the pump gallery went down to the room they had all come out of, and it was not being looked at this month by anybody, and it was not being looked at this month because it could not be, because there is no second ladder and the first one has six weeks of standing water on its stiles and a dead man's rope tied round it four times.

So: nobody in that anteroom was going to walk that route to the bottom, and everybody in it knew where it went, and the group that had come out of a hole in the ground had a second way out of the ground that could be shut by a woman with a reason in her own hand, and that was as far as anybody had taken it.

Sefu Aran put both hands on the wheel and read the route's terms once more, at the fourth bell, on the last morning, in the order a person leaving would find them, and then the ledger was shut, and the review line was named, and the review line was the ordinary one and not the long one, and Dalla Sen read it out before she wrote it and the room took it apart twice and it came back with two changes and it went in the way that review lines go in, with a name and a time and Jo's hand under Dalla's.

The unresolved list did not go away. It got its ninth item and kept the other eight.

*The bronze delivery seat, which no one would release.* The timber for the school-door head, which no one in the building had, and which was now one wedge and a length short instead of only a length short. The carpenter's wage for a man a guild would not pay. Nineteen days of meals, given and not waived. A sealed liter of verified condensation in a stoppered jar. Iven Tallo's bond, suspended and named. A form unsigned in a Marrowgate office that named who answered for a tunnel collapse, to which six people had now added a floor of a school and a lip with no post under it and a dead man on a stair. And the guild's door, locked, with an order on it that had never once opened anything, and a clearance office forty days of walking away that had not yet heard about a shore, and would.

And the two questions that nobody in that room could answer and that the page carried anyway, because a page that only carries the answers is a page that has stopped being a record and become a notice.

*Who keeps the Hearthhold rooms, and why do the oldest plans in this building show homes and not treasure?*

*And if a route like this one is built by a rescue crew and kept by the people who sleep under it, is it a public thing, or is it a road with a name on it and a lock on the end?*

Neither of them got an answer. The clerk wrote them down because Dalla Sen had said in the twenty-fourth day that a decision that does not say when it is looked at again is a decision that has been made to last, and a question that is not written down is a question that has been made to have never been asked.

---

And in the morning the route was used, and the first thing that happened on it was not a crossing.

Renn Vale went down the three steps to the service bend, on the fourth morning, with her coat on, and the crew were not on the stair, because the crew were not on the stair on any morning and had not been since the terms were read, and she untied the cord herself and retied it herself the way her mother and Sefu had taught her in nine weeks of watching two people do it, and she checked the shore's foot with the back of her hand because that is what you do with a thing you keep, and she looked at the black cord where it came over the top of the wall and bore down on the stone, and she put her thumb on the bright inch of band that a fortnight of a new line had already begun to put there, and she worked out, without being told anything, that in a year there will be four of them, and she had no idea at all what the four inches in that hollow meant and had not asked and was not going to, and she said nothing about that at all, and then she said, out loud, to nobody, the thing she had said in the fortieth day, in the same words, because it was still true and it was the only thing she had that was worth saying to a mechanism.

"It is a stone in a hole, and my mother is dead, and it is not a code."

Then she went up the three steps and tied the bell rope's other end to the newel so that a person could not reach the cord without passing the bell, which is Renn Vale's invention and which nobody has argued with, and which is the first thing in this Hearthhold that has been built by a person who was not asked to build anything and has not been paid and is not going to be mentioned in a hearing.

And the four at a time went over, and the four came back, and the line came tight through the block and the shore took it and the wall did not come out, and the lip did what it did, and there was a woman under it, because there is no post under it and there is no timber in this building for a post and the timber for the post is under water with a number chalked on it.

At the top of the nine steps, on the bench that used to be under a wrapped plate, Pell Roke sat with his crutch across his knees and his ear against the stone of the step above the stair head, and he called the interval eight seconds before the wheel every single time, in a voice that did not carry and did not need to, because there were two people on the plinth now and one of them had learned it off him, and the page in the ledger that carries the plinth rota carries his name in the margin in Tams Quill's hand and not in the rota, because a rota is a duty and the margin is a debt, and Tams had asked which of the two it was and Pell had said it was a debt and had not been argued with.

And the wheel turned twice under the floor, the way it had every week for nine weeks, and it went on under the conversation in the kitchen line the way weather goes on under a conversation, and the pot was on, and the anteroom had its ten, and the school corner floor was laid and the grit had not come up once since the floor went over, and a corridor that opened on the days six people agreed to open it was shut, and the whole of the boundary went on being lived in by people who had decided, out loud, one at a time, in a room with a lamp in it, that a thing they slept over was theirs and was not the crew's and was not anybody's, and who could shut it, and who had said out loud that they would say so again in the spring.

It was the first useful route anybody in this Hearthhold had ever had, and it was four people wide, and it had a woman under it.

It was also the first route anybody in this Hearthhold could close.
