# Chapter 71 — The Terms

Thirteen weeks is a long time to refuse a thing, and Ilsa Norr had refused twice in front of the same people and both times had given a reason better than stubbornness, and on the Monday morning of the thirteenth week she came into the anteroom with her apron on and a key already out of it and asked for the room.

"Not the anteroom," she said, before anybody offered it. "Say that back to me, Dalla, because I know what this room does to a sentence. Not the anteroom, and not the kitchen, and not the corridor with a door open onto the corridor."

"Write it as she said it," said Dalla Sen.

"Write it as I said it." She put her hand flat on the end of the bench. "The school store is the room. The key is mine and it has been in this apron since before the collapse and it turns this week and not one day before and not one day after, because the day a thing is done is the day it is done and not a day anybody is generous about."

---

She had the store emptied before she said a single term, and that took the whole morning, and the first cost was announced before the work and in the same breath as the work, because that is how she does it and has done it since the seventh week.

"Nine boxes off that shelf, and the two beech lengths are the props under it, and the shelf is dressed stone and has dropped twice, and the second time it dropped it came down onto a corner of one of these and I have had the corner mended with a bit of a crate and I am telling you it is mended and not fixed." She said it standing up, at the first bell, to a full anteroom. "So: a day of school, and the day is today, and the nine children come down the nine steps and sit in the anteroom and the anteroom holds ten including one litter for no more than two hours, and I know that, and I am going to make the keeper say it, and then I am going to make him put six of us down the nine steps to listen."

"Flagged," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel. "Ten including one litter, two hours, the outer service door open, the pump attended, and Nera or I able to stop it. Six of you down the nine steps, and stay there until I say the room is back under its number, and Ilsa, I am not arguing with you, I am putting you down the same steps as the rest of them."

"I am not going down the steps," said Ilsa Norr. "I am carrying nine boxes."

---

That is what the morning was, then, and nobody got to the terms until about the second bell, and the terms were said standing in the middle of a floor that had four of the people who would be in the room on it and about nine who would not.

Two of the nine boxes were carried down by nine-year-olds in twos, at a walk, one child at each end the way Renn Vale does it with the school through a two-foot-eight door, and Tams Quill counted them on the bench at the top of the nine steps and got to nine, and then went back and counted them again and got to nine, and wrote nothing with his right hand at all, and entered with his left that he had counted them twice because he had been told once and had not believed it.

Box four had the mended corner, and the mend was a strip of crate board with four small nails through it, and Ilsa Norr put her thumb on it in the middle of the floor in front of eleven people without saying anything about it, and a man from the middle passage who had come up to be shown the boxes said, quite quietly, that the crate came off a pallet that came up the middle passage, and Ilsa Norr said, "Then it is a crate from this road, in this store, holding this corner together, and it is eleven weeks old and it has done more work this month than a great deal of the timber we have been counting." And nobody laughed, because it was not funny, and because she was right about it.

Nera Oduya said hers first, and she said them in the air engineer's voice, which is the voice of a woman naming a condition.

"I am going to be the only person in that room who cannot be trusted with what is on it," she said, "and I want that said out loud, in this room, at this hour, before it is unrolled, and not afterwards, because afterwards I would be able to say that nobody warned me and that would be a lie. Dalla, put it in the book in my words with the time on it, and put it before the terms and not under them."

"Wrote it," said Dalla Sen, "and read it out."

"And I am going to say why in this room rather than have it looked for afterwards, because you will all look for it and you should find it here. It is not that I am careless. It is that I am the only one of the five of you who knows what a plan is for."

Nobody in the anteroom said anything, which was the correct thing.

"I have been in this building eleven weeks," Nera said, "with four air cards and a valve and a shaft I cannot certify and a rate I will not publish, and I have said out loud in this room that a rate I cannot stand behind is a rate that kills somebody, and I have refused to publish one and put a watch on it instead, and I have stood at the top of three steps and named a person for a post I am going to give away, and I have watched a bar come back off a cold wall four mornings running with an answer I cannot classify.

"And in eleven weeks the only thing I have ever wanted is a piece of paper with somebody else's carefulness on it. That is not noble. It is what an engineer is when she has run out of instruments and has not run out of questions. So put it in the book that I said I could not be trusted, and let the reason stand next to it, and let whoever reads it in a year decide for themselves whether a woman who says that about herself in advance is being careful or is buying herself permission to want something."

"Then we do not open it," said Bel Ordry.

"We open it," said Nera. "I have said I cannot be trusted. I have not said I am going to behave as though I cannot be trusted, and if I start refusing things on the grounds that I might want them I will be doing the one thing in this building I have never done, which is to refuse a work on the grounds that I would like to be the one doing it."

---

Ilsa Norr then said the rest of the terms, and she said them the way she had said her refusals, which is that she gave the reason before she gave the answer and gave the reason better than the answer deserved.

"Five of us," she said. "Me, because the key is mine and it has always been mine. Dalla, because there is no version of this where the boundary's record does not say what happened and because I have stopped pretending that a thing can be in a room and not be in a book. Nera, because she has just asked to be in it. Mikka, because the water is hers and because she has been counting this building out loud for three weeks and has the best right in this Hearthhold to be in a room where somebody wrote down what this place was for. And Renn, because she keeps the road and the shore and the line and because if this plan turns out to be about a passage then she is the person who has to stand in front of it."

"And the five of them are not in the room." She did not raise her voice and did not need to. "Marek, Leena, Iven, Pell and Tams. And I want the reason said, because I have been told *no* by better people than me three times in eleven weeks and I owe them one.

"It is not that I think you are going to take something. It is that a document you have not been in a room with is a document you can still argue with, and I have watched this building lose the ability to argue with things at about the rate it acquires them, and by the fourteenth week there will be four people here who cannot be contradicted on any subject because they are the only ones who have seen it. **I want five of us in the room and five of you outside it, and I am aware that is the shape of a thing done on purpose, and that is the reason to do it on purpose.**"

"And the rest of it," said Dalla Sen, pen up. "You have not said the rest of it."

"The plan does not leave the store," said Ilsa Norr. "It is not copied and not drawn and not traced and not described line by line, and if one of us tries to remember the shape of it in four years and gets it wrong that is our problem and not the store's.

"What leaves the room is a description. In the words of the five of us. And I will correct it, because it is about my school and my corridor and my corridor's water, and if I do not correct it in the same hour then I have let a stranger's memory of my building stand in a book.

"And one more, and it is the last one and I have thought about it for two days. **If one of us cannot carry what is in there, that person says so out loud on the way out, in this anteroom, and does not say it in the store and does not say it in a margin and does not put it in a letter. Out loud, here, and then it is the building's problem and not mine.**"

"Terms read and entered," said Dalla Sen, "in Ilsa Norr's words, in her own order, and read out again at her request, and the air engineer's condition read out first and separately because she asked for it first."

---

They went in at the third bell and the door shut, and it was a door in a wall and the wall was about a foot and a half of dressed stone with a lintel over it that three wedges and two shoring lengths were holding up, and one wedge short, and the refusal of the fourth was Renn Vale's and was not withdrawn.

Marek Venn put his slate down on the shelf above the bench, blank side up, where it had been on Friday, and did not pick it up again that morning. It was the third time in eleven days. He noticed himself doing it and then he stopped noticing himself doing it, which he took to be the whole of what any habit was.

There was nothing for him to do. That was not a punishment. It had never been a punishment. It was a man standing on the side of a wall that has something on the other side of it, and it is the oldest condition there is, and he had spent thirty-two years being the man on the side of the wall with the instrument on his side.

Through the stone he could hear nothing, which he had expected. What he could hear was more specific than nothing: the scrape of a shelf's dressed stone on dressed stone as a thing came off it, twice, and then a pause long enough to be a person deciding something, and then a sound he had not heard through a wall in his life before — a hard dry rolling, four inches of wood turning on a dowel, going round, and round, and round, and it did not stop for forty seconds.

He knew what that was. He had unrolled survey drawings in worse conditions than this, on a worse table, with worse light, and the moment the dowel started to turn his whole body had started to arrange itself around a problem, because his hands knew what came next: weight on the near end, two fingers on the roll to stop it springing, and then the flat of the paper down and the corners squared and a weight on each corner before you let go of anything.

Thirty-two years. And on the other side of a wall in this building there was a woman who had never unrolled anything in her life, and she had the key and the terms and the right to be wrong, and Marek Venn put his left hand flat on the stone of the wall and took it off again and did not write a single thing down.

The thing he had wanted, standing there, was not the document. He had no claim on the document and would have been the last man in that building to argue for one; the document was Ilsa Norr's and it had been hers before his boots were on this level. What he had wanted was to be one of the five people in the room, and he went looking for the honest version of that want for about a minute and found it, and it was not flattering.

It was this: that in eleven weeks in this Hearthhold he had been outside more rooms than he had been inside, and that on the sixth day of the seventh week a woman at a frame had told him he would be more useful standing at the far end of a room than anywhere near her housing, and that he had been rather proud of that, and that a man who is proud of being sent out of rooms is a man who has turned being sent out into a virtue, and that a virtue is a thing you can be praised for, and that is the last thing anybody who is leaving should be doing in a building where a clerk has just written down a word she did not know she was writing.

So: the wall, and the smell of old paper coming through stone, and a man on the other side of it with a slate he was not going to use, and the eleven words he had learned in a corridor in a corridor of a different kind.

What came through the wall after that was voices, and none of them words. That is a thing people do not believe. A room with five people in it makes a noise like a kitchen, and this one made a noise like a kitchen for about forty minutes, and then it stopped, all at once, the way a room stops.

---

They came out at about a quarter past the fourth bell and the anteroom had filled again behind them without anybody noticing, and the crew had been on the other side of that wall for forty minutes with nothing to do, which was a thing Tams Quill entered on the board under nothing at all, in his left hand, in the column with no heading, with the reason against it, because he had learned in eleven weeks that an hour nobody can account for is an hour somebody will be asked about later.

Ilsa Norr came out last and stood in the middle of the floor and looked at the room the way she looked at a shelf that had dropped.

"Right," she said. "Before anybody in this anteroom asks me a single question, I am going to say the thing that governs all of them.

"**I have not got anything to hand anybody and I have not got anything to keep.** I have got a description that five women will give in their own words, and I am going to give my part of it now, because it is about a school and it is mine, and I am not going to let a clerk's hand be the first place my school appears in a sentence.

"Sit down if you are sitting, because this is going to take a while and I am not saying it twice and there is no second reading of it in a store with a locked door."

And in the anteroom of the Hearthhold air-gate, in the thirteenth week, with a pump hissing on one note under the floor and a pot standing at fifteen days in a kitchen and nine children sitting on the bottom of nine steps because their teacher had emptied her own store, Ilsa Norr began to describe what was on a dowel, and Dalla Sen wrote it down in her own hand, and Marek Venn stood at the end of the tally board with a blank slate under his arm and understood, for the second time in thirteen weeks, that the sentence he had come down a shaft to learn was the one everybody else in the room was already saying out loud.
