# Chapter 184 — The Third Shore

The fifth day of the thirty-fifth week is a Friday, the corridor is open, and the school ran for the thirty-fourth morning in the room at the kitchen-line end. Same room. Same nine. It is also the day of the week in the first column of a printed schedule that came down this passage five weeks ago, and a man who keeps a page asked in this room on Tuesday for that room not to be full today, and the woman who keeps it did not do it, and on Wednesday she told a room that a keeper who empties a room of nine children because of a sheet on it has let the sheet pick the day. So the nine are in there this morning on purpose, and the clerk entered the purpose before anybody said it out loud, and nobody in the room contradicted it. A keeper emptied that room on Wednesday morning at the second bell and filled it again at the fourth, and wrote one line about it in a document, and that line is still the only writing any person has put into any document in this building this week.

Twelve readings went into Jo Verrick's own book at the first bell, taken with a piece of chalk and a piece of string on the party wall in the way she has taken them every morning for as long as she has been taking them. The figure was a quarter of an inch. The string was where the string was. The eleven before it were the same figure, except for two nothings in the thirty-second and thirty-third weeks. A wall that agrees with itself eleven times is a wall a person trusts for about four days.

The line of nothing under the string was the width of two fingers along the join where the lime repair stops and the old stone starts. It came out of it four times in the time it takes to say a sentence and stopped for a count. There is a folding rule on Jo Verrick's belt and the belt stayed where it was.

"**Twelve readings and two nothings,**" she said, "**and the figure is a quarter of an inch and it is the same as the eleven, and I am going to say the thing I have been not saying since Sunday, which is that this morning I counted a face.**"

Nobody moved.

"**I have read this wall for thirty-five weeks and I have read one face of it,**" Jo Verrick said. "**The face in this room. And at the second bell this morning I went round to the far side of it, which I have not done since I came into this building, because a man and his five have been standing in front of it for two days and I went and looked at what they were looking at.**"

"**What is on that side,**" said Renn Vale.

"**The same wall. The same join. The same line at the same height, and I could put my thumb on the same string and my thumb would be in the same place.**" Jo Verrick took her hand off the wall. "**And it is not the same gap.**"

The pump got two strokes in and nobody used them.

"**On this side the line is about a finger wide. I would not call it less than a finger, because a finger is the only thing in this building that is calibrated and a finger is mine, and I have said out loud in this room before that I will not make a number out of myself.**" She had her tape in her hand and did not use it. "**On the far side it is two fingers. It is the two fingers I have been writing down since Sunday, and it is the two fingers the woman who keeps that room has been kneeling next to. They are not the same two fingers. They are the same join on two faces of a wall two feet thick, and one of them is twice the other, and I have been reporting one of them for a week as though it were the join.**"

Marek Venn came down the nine steps at the second bell with his book open for the first time in three days, and the reason he came down was not curiosity at all. A foreman had asked a clerk for a number on Tuesday, and the clerk had not had one. The number was the only thing in this building Marek Venn could give that nobody else could give. He had spent three days off that wall on the grounds that a man who measures a wall on the morning strangers arrive is a man showing them what he is for. By the third day he could see that the sentence had been mostly pride with a working edge on it, and that he had used the one to get the other, which is the oldest trade there is and the one he is worst at.

"**Then tell them,**" said Renn Vale, "**because you are the only person in this building who can tell them, and because they have been arguing about hours since Tuesday and a man who is going to be asked for hours deserves to be asked about a wall first.**"

He got down onto the common landing with the five of them and stood in front of the wall for about a quarter of an hour and did not put a hand on it and did not put a tool on it and did not put a mark in it, and when he came back up the steps he said three things in the order he was sure of them.

"**One. The wall is not moving,**" he said. "**I want that first because everybody in this room is frightened of it and it is not what is happening. The reading is a quarter of an inch and it has been a quarter of an inch for eleven readings and the wall behind that join is nine hundred years of stone and it is standing where it was.**"

"**Two. The bad repair is only on one face.**" He put his knuckles on the trestle, the hand that will not close. "**A man who does this work did it from the kitchen-line side, because the kitchen-line side is the side that gets looked at, because that is where a room's people are. Nine hundred years of builders on this side never touched it, because there was nothing here to touch. So the repair was a skin on one face of a good wall, and the skin has let go on the face it was put on, and on this face the join has opened about half as far. That is not two walls. That is one wall with one bad face, and it has been one bad face for two hundred years.**"

"**And three,**" said Renn Vale.

"**And three is the one I would like a person to be frightened of,**" Marek Venn said. "**The third shore from the left has lost about a finger of its length at the head and its foot has not moved at all. A shore that has lost length at the head with its foot still on the floor is a shore being crushed at the head, and it is being crushed because there is nothing spread under the head of that sill, and the man who set these six said out loud in this room on Monday that the correct answer was two foot plates and a spreader and a shore a person could take out with a wedge in four minutes instead of forty. He was right. Nobody has acted on it, and it has been nine days.**"

Nobody said anything, and the pump got four strokes in.

"**I did not know it on Monday,**" Marek Venn said, "**and I have been on that landing twice since and I did not look at the heads of the shores, because I have spent three days in this building convinced that the interesting thing in that wall is a gap and not a piece of timber. So the half of that is mine and I would like it in the book before anybody is kind to me about it.**"

Dalla Sen entered it in his words and entered the half against it in the space where a reason goes, and entered that the wall is not moving, and entered that a man who has spent three days not looking at a wall came back to it because a stranger asked for a number.

Then Iven Tallo came down off the anvil end and stood where Jo Verrick had been standing, on the anteroom side, with his right hand holding the chalk and his left open and flat against his thigh, and he looked at the head of the third shore for a long time without touching anything.

"**Nine days,**" he said. "**I named it on Monday and nobody acted and I have spent nine days being right and not saying anything, which is the most useless thing a man can be in a building.**"

"**The half against your own half,**" said Bel Ordry, from the step.

"**I have no half against this one. I would like somebody in this room to notice that.**" Iven Tallo did not put the chalk down. "**A carpenter can be right and say nothing and it is still a thing that cost somebody a finger of a shore. On Monday I said the fault and I said the reason and I said the reason there was a correct answer with iron in it and I did not say *therefore the head of this shore is worth a look every day*, and that is a sentence I had in my mouth and did not use, because I have spent thirty-five weeks in this building finding out what happens when I hold things back.**"

Then Mero Senn said the sentence from the doorway, once, and the room let him finish it before anybody answered.

"**A plan that depends on a day of the week for the safety of nine children is not a plan,**" he said. "**And here is the plan, and it is a good plan, and I want a person to say out loud that it is a good plan before I say what is wrong with it. The third shore comes out at the second bell, and the nine are in the room on the other side of that wall from the first bell until the school is finished, and nobody is sent anywhere, and the wall is not being pushed while it happens, and the whole of it is done in a morning.**"

"**That is a good plan,**" said Renn Vale, "**and I will say it, and I have already said it, and here is what is wrong with it. It is a good plan that depends on nine children being in a room on the other side of a wall while a piece of timber comes out of it.**"

"**Then that is my sentence and you have said it,**" said Mero Senn.

"**It is your sentence and I have said it, and I am going to give the other half of it myself, which I have not done in four days.**" Renn Vale came down the four steps. "**The half against waiting is that the third shore has lost a finger of its length in nine days and nobody has looked, and a piece of timber that is getting shorter is a piece of timber that is being eaten by the stone it is standing on, and a foreman of nine years standing on that landing knows that and I do not, and I have been sitting in this room on the grounds that a room should be careful about a day of the week.**"

"**Then the day of the week is not a reason and the school is not a shelter,**" said Ilsa Norr from the kitchen-line doorway, where she had been standing since the first bell with a slate and nine children behind her and had not come further in.

"**It is neither. Here is what I am, and I am saying it before the work starts, because afterwards it will be a thing that happened and now it is a thing somebody chose.**" She put the slate on the sill, which is not where she puts it. "**I am going to be in that room for the whole of the morning. I am not going to be on that landing and I am not going to be within reach of the anteroom, because there are nine children in a room and I am the one who goes in when something happens. And I am not one of the two people who agree that the wall can take it. I have been a person who agreed about a wall twice in this building and I am not going to do it a third time with my hands full of nine other people's children.**"

"**Then nobody is agreeing to anything,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**Then the room is not agreeing to anything and one person is choosing,**" said Ilsa Norr, "**and I would like the clerk to write it in that shape, because in four days somebody in this room is going to read that a wall was worked on while nine children were in the room next to it and is going to want to know who decided.**"

"**You decided,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and I am entering that you decided, and I am entering that a physician said a plan that depends on a day of the week is not a plan and a woman who keeps a room gave the other half of that in her own mouth, and I am entering that I was in the room for both halves.**"

Renn Vale then emptied the anteroom for the length of the work, and said the anteroom's terms before she emptied it, and the count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change.

"**The anteroom is out of it and it stays out of it for the length,**" she said. "**Not because ten is a low number. Because a room that a body has to cross at a moment like this is not the room to stand in, and I have been counting nine people twice a morning through that room since the second week and I am not doing it today.**"

The clerk entered that a room's rating is a number said to a page and is not a count of the people who cross it, and that this boundary has been careful about that distinction for thirty-five weeks, and that a woman who keeps a corridor has now been the one to say so out loud for the first time.

The third shore came out in eleven minutes by Emrys Callow's own count, with four of his five on it and Marek Venn's coarse left hand on the head of the fourth, and nobody watched that hand and it did not close.

Leena Orr read it. She came up the nine steps from the lower level on the side of the waist this boundary is on, with her right shoulder in a sling and a rule in her left hand, and she is the only person in this building whose whole trade is reading a thing that has already been built, and a shore is a thing that has already been built.

"**The head was a finger shorter than it was on Monday,**" she said, "**and the foot has not moved and the mark in the flag is where the foot was. I have read six of these in my life and four of them were like this. The head is crushed, and it is crushed because there is nothing spread under the sill, and the stone it is standing against is taking it an eighth of an inch at a time, and that is not a fault in the timber, it is a fault in the ground.**"

Dalla Sen read that back out of the book. Then Iven Tallo read his own sentence from Monday — *a shore with no foot plate crushes its own mark into a floor and takes the floor with it when it lets go* — against the two figures a woman had just given. The two were the same fact said from opposite ends. The clerk entered that the carpenter's sentence of nine days ago had named the head of that shore in the wrong half of its own sentence, and that it took a person who reads finished things to say which half, and that a man who builds things and a woman who reads finished things had both been in this room at the same time on a Friday morning and had not said anything to each other, and that the clerk entered the not saying as well.

"**The point at the low end is not on this side of the waist,**" Dalla Sen said, before anybody had asked her for it, "**and I am entering that I entered it before it was asked for, and I am entering that no name went on it.**"

Then Nera Oduya came down four steps and stood in the anteroom doorway and said four numbers, and did not say a fifth, and the clerk wrote the four.

"**I certify nothing and refuse nothing,**" she said. "**I have said that in this room twice this week and I am saying it a third time on a morning when it is more useful and not less. The air in the room at the kitchen-line end is what it was at the fourth bell on Wednesday, and it will not be that again after this morning, and I am not going to give you a number about it because a number about it is a thing you would put in a column and there is a woman in this building who has just told you what happens to columns.**"

Nobody asked her about the sentence she wrote a fortnight ago. Dalla Sen entered that she had not asked, and entered that it was the fourth time in a fortnight she had noticed herself not asking.

Then Emrys Callow said the number, and he said it himself, in a room, out loud.

"**Five and a half,**" he said. "**Five and a half shores on a wall that the district book says has six. That is a figure about a piece of timber and not about a room and not about anybody's body, and I have checked with the man who chose the wood and the man who chose the wood is not here.**"

"**And the district's book is not this boundary's book,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and a figure in it was entered on Monday by six people who did the work and who have gone back up the passage, and this boundary is not going into a competing figure, and I am going to write five and a half in this book as a fact about the length of a piece of wood and I am going to write beside it that the number in the district book is not corrected by us and is not contradicted by us, because those are two different acts and a clerk who does both at once is making a document.**"

"**Then somebody has to carry it up the passage,**" said Callow.

"**Yes,**" he said, "**and I am the man who should, because the timber is the district's and the man who chose it should hear it from the crew that found it, and because if I do not go up it in the next two days then a man in an office is going to find out about it from a book in a district, and I have been in a district's books for nine years and I know exactly what a fact does to a piece of timber once it is in there.**"

"**Then the terms come first,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and the terms are for the sixth day, and I am not going to write a word about a crew on a piece of paper on a Friday afternoon with a number on it that five strangers and nine children have an interest in.**"

By the sixth bell the pump had run its last length and stopped, and the clerk read the day's number and the total out loud, both of them, and walked the board at eighteen entries. The count was read at one hundred and nineteen. Four today and two hundred and fifty-four since the keeper signed. The boundary is at three hundred and seventy-one and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.

She read them out because a piece of timber came out of a wall in this room and a boundary's own record ought to be heard by the room that took it out.

No sixth piece of work has been asked of anybody, and the seventy-one on the crew has not moved since the second day of the thirty-second week. The reason is in the book. The reason is that a wall gave a number this morning, and a room is going to have to decide what to do with a number before it can hand five people a piece of work at all.

The pot is seven days. The seam gave one on Thursday and not two, and a cook said the arithmetic out loud in the middle of a morning's work and would not say the word, and the four households at a second gate have nothing, and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody. And the count. Wen Pask is one hundred and nineteen days and one hundred and nineteen nights into a count that is his own, and it is not going to be one hundred and twenty, and she has said in twenty-eight weeks running that she is going to keep writing it.

And in the party wall, on the far side of a room nine children were sitting in, the line of nothing went on being the width of two fingers. It went on doing the shape of a closing that nobody in this building can name, because a carpenter named the shape on the seventh day of last week and the room could not name the subject of it. Nobody put a hand in it. Nobody put a tool in it. Nobody went through it, and nobody went near it, and the lamp on the flags of the anteroom floor stayed unlit where it has stood since that Sunday.
