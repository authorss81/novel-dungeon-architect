# Chapter 26 — The Shared Corridor

The day after, the work was baskets.

Not decisions. Not a plan. Every basket in the building had to come out of the lower corridor's dip, because the dip was where the pump's rest interval put two inches of water and the fallen parapet had taught everyone in it what two inches of water did to a floor people had to walk in a hurry.

They started at the sixth bell. Jo worked from the clinic end and Tams from the kitchen end, and they moved a household's life one basket at a time up onto the kitchen-side wall, out of the fall line, and onto boards so the baskets did not stand in their own water.

Oren took the bearing work. He had spent his whole life in other people's buildings knowing which wall to lean on, and he went at the waist the way he went at everything, without talking, and found the thing that nobody had thought to look for.

The cracked lintel had not dropped straight down. It had dropped and turned, and the stones of its bed had keyed against each other on the left and opened on the right, which meant the right-hand jamb was now carrying a piece of the door head it had never been asked to carry.

"Two fingers of daylight between the jamb and the wall," he said, and put his hand in it. "Nobody has to prop this from underneath. It has to be strapped from the side, and the strap has to go down past the jamb, not across the head."

"Can you do that?" Marek asked.

"Not with what we have." Oren took his hand out and wiped it. "I can show a body how to do it in a morning. I cannot do it in a morning, because the hands have to be on both sides at once and one of them has to be holding a child out of the way."

So the corridor kept its narrow door, and the narrow door was marked on the floor in chalk with a figure beside it that told a person their own width, which Dalla thought was the most useful thing anybody had written in two weeks.

Tams ran the corridor's test loads after that, because the corridor had to be walked with weight before anyone would let a person walk it in earnest. He made dead loads out of stone sacks and set them along the kitchen side, out of the fall line and clear of the dip, then walked them himself, twelve times, in the pattern of somebody carrying a full basin and not looking down, because that was the load that broke a corridor: not a heavy thing, but a heavy thing carried while hurrying. Pell called the water mark from the top of the waist, because a man carrying a basin at that speed does not look at a drain, and the only person watching the drain was a fourteen-year-old on a step.

On the seventh pass a sack slid, and the floor under the dip gave a sound like a hand clapping once, and everybody stopped.

"It is the drain," Jo said. "The channel is under it and the fill has been washed out from both sides, and when the water comes up it goes into the fill and the fill is what moves."

They got the fallen parapet out in pieces, which took most of a morning and a half and used the hand line and four people and no idea at all that it would be easy. The slab was eleven feet of old stone in one piece, and it could not be lifted, so it had to be rolled, and rolling it off the drain was the work of the day.

Tams and Oren rolled it. Jo cleared the channel. Mikka, who was not on the crew list for any of this and had come anyway, put a stop before it rolled and stood with her hand flat on the stone to keep it from starting early, which nobody had asked her to do and which was the sort of thing that had been happening around this corridor for a hundred years.

Under the slab the channel was full of stone dust and a season of silt. They cleared it by hand, an hour and a half, and then Jo built a cover.

"That is not a repair," she said, when Iven looked at it. "It is a lid. A lid keeps a child's foot out of a channel that has washed its own fill away. It is not a repair and I am not calling it one."

"Nobody is calling it one," Iven said, and went and found the only two sound boards in the boundary work area and helped her set them, and then went to the kitchen and came back with a third board that did not belong to the corridor and that Mikka released in writing.

Then they marked the new escape path.

Marek had wanted the old one. The old one ran down the middle of the dip, because that was where the floor was flattest and the school door was at the end of it. The new one ran along the kitchen-side wall on the safe strip, which was eighteen inches narrower at the waist and a foot and a half off the drop, and everybody who used that corridor had to be told the difference out loud, individually, which took a whole second morning of Mikka saying it at the kitchen line and Ilsa saying it at the school door and Dalla saying it to every person who came through the gate.

"Nobody is going to remember a chalk line," Mikka said. "People remember a person telling them."

By the second afternoon, the corridor was a room that could be walked. Not rated. Not certified. Walked.

Which is where Nera and Marek had their argument, in front of everybody, on the third afternoon, and it went the way arguments go when both of them are right about different halves of a thing.

Marek had the sketch. It was the lower-corridor sketch, drawn with his left hand on a board, and it had a number on it.

"Six people in the corridor, for up to half an hour, while the pump is running, with a named attendant and the school door as the relief," he said. "That is a family, a stretcher-bearer pair, and a child. It is the smallest useful number. It gives the Hearthholds somewhere to put people during a repair."

"No," Nera said.

She had the dyed cloth in her hand and had been holding it up to the waist for some time.

"Say why," Leena said, which stopped Marek from answering first.

Nera set the cloth down. "The school door is the corridor's relief. It is the only one. When it was four feet, the corridor could hold people and still move air. It is two feet eight inches now, and you are proposing a room that depends on a door that is propped by nothing and held by a man's shoulder."

"It is propped," Tams said. "Oren has a line on it."

"A line is not a wall. A line tells me where the door is. It does not tell me how much air the door can move." Nera turned to Marek. "And you have not included the six minutes. Your half an hour is measured from when? If it starts when the pump starts, then most of it is one rest interval, and the six minutes are the part that matters, because in them the water is up, the drain is under load, and the air in this corridor has nothing to push it anywhere except through a gap in a propped head."

"Then say what the interval is."

"The interval is the pump's. Twenty-six strokes, six minutes, nineteen strokes. You cannot have a corridor occupancy that ignores when the pump rests, because that is when the air is worst and the water is highest, and that is exactly when a family would be standing in it." She put both hands flat on the board. "I will not certify a number of people in that corridor at all. I will certify a condition. One. The corridor is attended the whole time anybody is in it. Two. The corridor empties when the pump rests, and it empties when the stair turns, and it empties when the cloth moves, and it does not matter whether anybody thinks the room is safe at that moment. Three. Whoever is in the corridor leaves by the kitchen line, because the kitchen line is the wider way, and the school door is not the escape while the head is cracked."

Marek felt the plan come apart and, underneath the coming apart, the much better plan underneath it.

He pushed back once, because he had to, and because the thing he was defending was true.

"You are describing a corridor that can only be used when nothing is wrong," he said. "It is raining when the pump rests. Then the people who need a place to stand for half an hour cannot have it, and we are back to a corridor with a person on a litter in it and nine children on the steps and nothing to be done about it. A room that is only safe when nothing is wrong is not a room. It is a schedule."

"Yes," Nera said. "It is a schedule. And the schedule is honest, which is more than the number was."

The room was quiet for a moment, and then Leena said the sentence that turned it, which she had been building since the anteroom test and which she delivered without looking at either of them.

"Then make the schedule the room," she said. "Not a number of people. A time. Nobody may stand in that corridor unless the pump is running and no watch is empty, and everybody leaves when the lever comes off, and the coming off is announced by the same bell that already exists."

"Twenty-six strokes, the six minutes, nineteen strokes," Tams said. "Thirty-four minutes of pump in a forty-minute cycle, and the six are the part with the water up and the door a door you cannot get a person through. So you get one long use, one gap, and the whole of the opportunity is the next start, and the next start does not come early."

"Write the intervals," Dalla said, and wrote them, and added them to the board: *the corridor may be used only while the pump lever is moving. The lever coming off empties it.*

Marek stood back and let the other plan win, and it was not a defeat in the way he usually lost. The interval schedule was better than his number, because his number had pretended the corridor had a capacity and the schedule admitted that the corridor had a *condition*, and a condition is something a person can be taught.

They wrote the sketch up by lamplight.

The lower corridor, one strip, kitchen side, marked safe strip in chalk, with the drained channel under a two-board cover and the fallen slab gone. The waist at two feet eight inches, marked with its own number. The school door as relief and not as escape while the head is cracked. The kitchen line as the way out. The clinic door opening inward, held by a hand, with a named person holding it whenever anybody was carrying the basin or a cot. The pump rest as the emptying time. And five lines of limit under all of it, in five different hands, because each of them belonged to the person who would be answerable for it.

The cost came last, because it always did.

The safe strip was boards, and boards were Jo's shutter-route stock, and Jo's shutter route was therefore finished for the season. The boundary workbench lost its working surface, and Jo's repairs would now be done on an upturned crate in the anteroom, which meant that the anteroom's ten people included whoever was doing carpentry, which meant the board had to change. The wet seam had to be watched while the corridor was in use, which meant a sixth watch on any shift with people in the corridor — a name, an hour, and a person who could not also be at the pump or the clinic return. And the bedding stones of the old lintel could not be reset without timber, so the narrow door stayed narrow, and every plan in the world was now working around two feet eight inches.

Jo wrote her own loss into the ledger, because Dalla had made it a rule that people wrote their own costs.

*Jo Verrick — shutter route closed for the season. Boundary bench unusable. Half-day of corridor strip work. Three half-days of pump cleaning so far, and a fourth owed.*

Dalla added the new watch, and the name beside it, and looked up to see whether the person she had written would accept it.

Jo read it and nodded once, and went to find a second pair of hands for the wedge set before morning.
