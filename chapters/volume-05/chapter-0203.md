# Chapter 203 — Two From A Haunch

The seventh day of the thirty-ninth week is a Sunday, and a seventh day is not a crossing day, so nobody came up the nine steps this morning and nobody went down them, and the arrangement has stood since the thirty-third week and has never once been tested, because there has never been anything to test it with. The school did not run and lost nothing. There is nobody in this anteroom who will say how many of them there are, and there has not been anybody who could since the twenty-ninth week, and this is the tenth seventh day in a row and it is the first one on which that has been an inconvenience instead of a position.

So the morning had nothing come into it, and the room did the only thing a room can do with a morning that nothing comes into, which is to look at what it keeps.

Jo Verrick had the frame at the first bell, a finger and two-thirds, and the party wall was a quarter of an inch, the twenty-third reading, and the same as twenty-second, and the string was where the string was. She put her thumb under the string and the thumb came out of it right. She gave the four refusals about the line along the join in her own words before anybody asked her for them, and Dalla Sen entered all four as four refusals and not as one, for the twenty-third morning running, and the reason the count is rising is that the clerk has stopped trying to make it sound like one refusal.

"**I am going to say one more thing about the frame and then I am going to leave it alone for a week,**" Jo Verrick said, "**because I have found something on it and I have had it for a day and I have slept on it and I am not going to have it any longer than that. There is a second mark on the head of that frame and it is not chalk, and I have told this room that already and the clerk has entered it, and I am now telling them what it is and what it is not, in this order, and I would like all of it entered and none of it improved.**"

"**Entered, in the words of the woman who reads it,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**It is a groove. It is not a scratch and it is not a drip. It is about the width of my thumbnail and it goes the whole width of the head and it is under the chalk, which means it is older than the chalk by a long way, and the chalk is a fortnight over five weeks and the groove is under it.**"

"**How do you know it is under it,**" said Bel Ordry, from the top of the nine steps.

"**Because the chalk has a lump in it,**" Jo Verrick said. "**Where the chalk went over the groove it went on thick and it has taken the shape of the groove underneath it and it is holding on both sides of the dip, and that only happens if the stone moved after the chalk went on, and the stone cannot have moved and taken a groove with it, so the groove was in the stone before the chalk and the chalk has settled into it.**"

Nobody said anything for a bit, and the pump did not run, and it was the first seventh day in five weeks that nobody in that room had wanted to hear the pump.

"**Now the part I am not going to say,**" Jo Verrick said. "**I have been reading this frame every morning since the second week. I did not see that groove on Monday and I did not see it on Tuesday and I did not see it on Wednesday and I did not see it on Thursday morning with my thumb under the string. I saw it at about two o'clock on Thursday afternoon and I have not been able to account for that and I have not told anybody until this morning, and a person in a year is going to want to know why a woman who reads a thing every morning did not see a thing for five weeks and I would like the answer in the book in my words and it is this: I did not see it because I was reading it. I was reading the frame's head against the string, which is what I do, and the groove is under the chalk, and the chalk was in my way, and I have put my eyes exactly where they have gone every morning for thirty-nine weeks and they went to the wrong place because the wrong place was correct.**"

"**Entered, in the words of the woman who reads it, and entered that she gave the reason before she was asked and not after,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and I am entering the two of those in the same place on purpose, because a clerk who puts a reason after a fact is doing it in a different order from the one the fact was given in and somebody in a year would read it wrong.**"

Renn Vale came down the four steps at the second bell, which on a shut morning is her bell, and there is no shut morning and no open morning on a seventh day and she was on the landing at the first anyway, and she said the thing she had gone to the second gate to say, and she said it in about ninety words and then stopped.

"**I said the term is six pails a day and I have not been able to keep it for four days and I am not able to keep it this morning, and I said it as the keeper of a term and not as a woman in a room, and I said it at the gate and not at the top of the steps.**"

"**And what they said back,**" said Mikka Hale, from the kitchen-line doorway.

"**That is theirs,**" said Renn Vale. "**And I am not going to put it in this building's book, and the reason is that what a person says to a keeper at a gate is not the keeper's to enter, and I have been in this building thirty-nine weeks and I have watched four people enter things that were not theirs, and I am not doing it on a Sunday for a term I set myself.**"

"**Entered, in the keeper's words, and entered that she was at a gate and not in a room, and entered that she has declined to report what was said to her,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that the clerk did not ask.**"

"**You did not ask,**" Renn Vale said.

"**I did not ask,**" said Dalla Sen.

Then the anteroom's terms were said, all six, by a person and repeated by the clerk, and they are said on a seventh day as well, and they were said here at the second bell, which is when they are said on a shut day, because a seventh day is not a shut day and is not an open day and the only honest hour for them on it is the one a shut day uses. The count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change. The outer service door was the one a body went out of. The fifth of those six terms was in a room with both of the people it names in it, and he named it and read nothing, which the clerk has now entered five days running. The third of the six was six pails a day and it was said and it was false and everybody in the room knew it was false and it went into the book in the space where a finding goes. The pump was attended, and stopped, and had been for a day and a night.

And then Bram Sallow came out of the second of the two rooms off the far end of the anteroom, past the pump, and stood in the doorway, and nobody in that anteroom had seen him come out in nineteen weeks.

He was a man of about thirty with the look of somebody who has been asleep in the day, and he did not come further into the room than the door frame, and he did not ask anybody for anything, and he stood there while the terms were being said and did not interrupt them, and when they were finished he stayed where he was.

"**I am going to say one thing and then I am going back in,**" he said. "**I have slept in that room nineteen weeks and for sixteen of those weeks there was a noise in the corner behind the pump every night from about the second bell to the fifth, and it was not the pump. It was underneath the pump, in the wall, and it came up through the floor of that room, and four of us sleep with our heads on that side.**"

Nobody in the anteroom said anything.

"**It has not been there since Friday,**" he said. "**And I want it entered that I have come out here to say that and not to ask for it back.**"

"**Entered, in his own words, and entered that he asked for nothing,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that his name is in this clerk's book for the first time in nineteen weeks, and entered that naming one of them is not a way of knowing how many there are, and entered that the clerk has thought about that and is entering it anyway because he is a person who came out of a room and said a true thing and she is not going to pretend she did not hear it.**"

"**Nobody is going to restart the pump,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and I want that said before anybody has to ask, and I want the reason said too, and the reason is that this room has a stopped pump and a groove under a chalk line that a woman found a day late, and neither of those is a reason to put water back into a wall.**"

Renn Vale went and stood by the kitchen-line doorway and looked down the nine steps and did not say anything for a while, and then Mikka Hale came out of that doorway with a cloth and went down four steps out of sight and came back up them with a jar in each hand, and put them on the trestle, and they were not full and they were not empty, and there was lime in the water.

"**Two,**" she said.

"**Say the rest of it,**" said Dalla Sen, because that is what a clerk does.

"**Two jars. The seam is in the haunch behind that frame, that is the frame the pump is bolted to, and it has given me nothing for fourteen mornings and it has given me two this morning, and I am not going to say the reason and I am going to say what I would do if I were wrong.**"

"**Say the reason,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**No,**" said Mikka Hale. "**The reason is that there was a head of water in that haunch for sixteen weeks and there has not been one since Friday morning at about ten minutes past two, and the joint has been weeping the water it did not have somewhere else for sixteen weeks and there was nowhere else for it to go, and now there is. I have not measured that. I have not measured a single thing about it and Jo has not put a rule on it and nobody in this room is going to be asked to.**"

"**Then it is a guess,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**Then it is a guess with two jars in it,**" said Mikka Hale, "**and I have not been able to keep this pot for one day for a fortnight and it is now two, and I would like somebody in this room to tell me whether I have been paid for a pump or not, because I have not decided and I am not going to decide it out loud, and a cook who decides that out loud is a cook with a seam in her arithmetic and a seam in her arithmetic is a person who can be managed by anybody with a pencil.**"

Nobody answered her, and the room went on, and the pot was two days, which was one more than it had been, and the clerk entered the increase in the space where a finding goes and the guess in the space where a reason goes, which is the first time in this building that a guess has been entered in the space where a reason goes and the clerk entered the decision to do it as her own.

"**I am entering a guess in a column made for reasons and I want it in the book that I did it on purpose and that I have thought about it for about a minute and that the minute is the whole of the thinking, and that a clerk who puts a guess in a findings column this week is handing whoever finds this page a number they did not earn.**"

At the sixth bell the day's figure and the total went into the book and neither was read out, and the reason went in beside them: a seventh day is a day nothing crosses on, and on a seventh morning a room finds out what it has been hearing for sixteen weeks, and a clerk who reads a total out loud over that is telling a room the morning is over, and this one is not.

One today and three hundred and seven since the keeper signed. The boundary is at four hundred and twenty-four and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.

The count went in underneath that on its own line, in the ordinary hand, and was not read, and no reason was given for that either, and the line has no heading over it and is not going to get one. Wen Pask is one hundred and forty-eight days and one hundred and forty-eight nights, forty-four weeks of a clerk saying out loud that she is going to keep writing it down. It is not going to be one hundred and forty-nine.

The board was not walked, and the reason was entered, and the reason was that a man has come out of a room and said a true thing and a clerk has entered a guess in the wrong column on purpose, and a board is a column, and a clerk does not walk a column on a day when she has just put something in the wrong one.

The crew are at seventy-five and a half and did not move, and the reason for the day is in the book, and the reason is that five men have not been asked in nineteen weeks and there is a stopped pump and a wet haunch and a man who has just told a room that he did not want the noise back, and there is nothing on that table a crew could be asked to carry.

And at the seventh bell the anteroom was a room with a hole in the corner of its floor and a trestle with a book on it and two jars on it that had lime in them, and a pump that had not run for a day and a night, and a groove in the head of a frame a hand's breadth under a line of chalk that a woman had been looking straight at for five weeks, and a man who slept with his head on that side of a room standing in a doorway having said one true thing and asked for nothing. The pot was two days. The seam was at four threads and had given two and had not been measured by anybody. A groove in a stone is a thing a wall does when it moves, and this room has one wall it reads every morning and one frame it reads every morning and one wall it does not read at all, and on Wednesday the corridor opens, and a man with a wooden tally comes up the middle passage on a day when everybody in this building has already said no to one thing.
