# Chapter 77 — The Third Time

The third time happened on a day nobody was standing there, which is the sentence Renn Vale had put in the book in the eleventh week and had called a term, and she had said it as a rule about posts, and it turned out to be a rule about Saturdays.

A post is only a post while its condition is happening. On the four days in seven she holds the wet seam the corridor is open and the waist is a post and Renn Vale is on it with her bad boot and her slate. On the three days in seven the corridor is shut, the waist is not a post, the school is not coming, the water does not come, and **nobody is standing on a piece of ground that has moved three times.**

It moved again on the Sunday, and the Sunday was the last day of the thirteenth week, and the way the boundary found out was that Bel Ordry's bar ticked in the throat's north-east cheek on the Sunday morning at the second bell, and the crack that had opened along the left side of the fill at the waist on the Thursday had gone nine inches further along the side of the corridor in one night of frost, and neither of those two facts was known to anybody who was standing in the same room, and that is the thing about them.

They were put side by side on the Monday morning, the first day of the fourteenth week, at the first work-bell, and the room was told that they were on the same day.

"No," said Nera Oduya. "Before anybody in this room has a thought about that, and before I have one myself, and I have had four. **We write two lines, the day, and no word between them.** I am not going to have a Tuesday in this book where a bar said something and a floor did something and a person put those two things next to each other with their eyebrows."

"Two lines and the date on both," said Dalla Sen, writing.

"**And one more thing I will say and then I will go up three steps and not come down for an hour.** Bel Ordry, on the Sunday: what did you get."

"Three," said Bel Ordry. "Same as the fourth morning. And a slide on the third, and the two other places on the cheek where it ticks when the bar is cold. Bar cold, wall cold, wall about four degrees at that height, and I will write it again exactly as I wrote it the first time because the second time a thing happens it is the first time it is a pattern, and a pattern with two dates on it is worth more than a sound with one."

"Six mornings," said Nera. "Three nothings, and three of something I cannot classify, and the two nothings are not the beginning of the three. **The classification does not change because it has happened three times.** Write it."

"Written," said Dalla Sen, "and the two lines are on the same page and there is nothing between them, and I am putting under them in my own hand that I have put them on the same page deliberately, because a reader in a year is going to want them near each other and I would rather they were near each other for a stated reason than for a reason somebody made up afterwards and then wrote down as a finding."

---

Renn Vale had it from the common landing at about the third bell and she came down three steps, which she does, and stood on the floor of the anteroom, and said the arithmetic of the corridor in about eleven seconds flat.

"It is a hand's breadth again," she said. "Along the left side, over about five foot this time, which is a foot more than Thursday. The third wedge is up **a quarter and a bit**, which is not a number anybody has before and is not a number I am going to give you, because a quarter of an inch is inside the noise in this cold and a quarter *and a bit* is the same quarter with a bad temper.

"**The corridor is shut. It is shut for the three days in seven as it was, and it is now shut for the fourth day as well, and I am not having anybody tell me it is a different corridor on a different day.** Nothing goes through it. Nobody goes over it. That is a keeper's order and it has a reason in my hand and the reason is that the ground has moved three times.

"And I am not standing on it, and I want that said, and I want the reason said, and the reason is the ninth week: **a person with a hand on a fill has made a decision that the ground is fine.** I have not put my hand on that fill since the sixth week and I am not going to now, and that is not caution, that is the only instrument this building has ever had for that ground, and I am not spending it."

---

Which left the water.

Twelve pails on an open day, eight on a shut day, and on a shut day the water comes up the middle passage and down the nine steps and cannot be put through a corridor that is shut, so it goes over the waist, by hand, in pails, at a walk, past ground that has moved three times, and it goes over the waist because there is no other way and there has not been for a fortnight and everybody in this building has been pretending the two-crossing arrangement was a piece of good fortune instead of a piece of arithmetic.

"It is the pair-of-shoulders column," said Mikka Hale, from the frame. "Tams. It is your column with nothing over the top of it. Today it is two people and one at a time and it is not nine steps, it is a corridor, and one of the two places is standing on the fill.

"And I want it read out that the answer is yes. I put it in the book in my own words on the last day of the last week and I said it was a rota with a cook's handwriting on it and I was right, and here it is, and the *when* in it is *wet* and today is wet and it is happening.

"Right," she said, to the room. "Somebody is going over that waist and it is not going to be the nine children and it is not going to be a woman on a string, and I am asking for it to be asked for by name."

"I am not going," said Renn Vale.

"Nobody said you were. I said somebody and I have said what it is not, and I am putting the two names in the book first because I learned that off you in the seventh week and I am not going to be the one who forgets it in the thirteenth."

Two names went in the book, and then a third, and the third one was the one nobody had expected and it was the one that decided the morning.

"Not him," said Iven Tallo, when Dalla Sen read the third name back. "**He is twenty-two and he has no bond and you are not putting a man with no trade on a piece of ground that has moved three times so that a room full of people can feel better about itself. That is what a body is for in a bad argument. Take it out of the book in my own hand, Dalla, because I put it there and I want my own hand on the removal.**"

"Struck in your own words at your own request," said Dalla Sen.

"Then it is me and Jo," said Isel Ferrow, "because Jo has priced every step of this and can say what it costs, and I have stood on that stone six times a week for three weeks, and this is the same job with a different floor."

"**It is not the same job.**" Iven Tallo said it hard. "On the stone I hold a rope and go back. On that fill I put my feet on washed-out stuff over an eleven-foot shaft and carry something I cannot put down if it goes. Say that out loud to the room, Isel, because the room does not know it and the room is about to agree to it, and agreeing to it is the easy part and the easy part is how this building gets people hurt."

"Then say it and price it," said Jo Verrick, "and say it in the morning, not the afternoon, and I will do the arithmetic and he will do the walking and nobody will be asked to do it in a cold afternoon at the end of a week because it seemed like the only thing anybody could offer."

---

They went at the second bell on the Monday with the eight pails a shut day gets, in two crossings of four, and it took one hour and fifty minutes, and Iven Tallo priced it at the frame afterwards, out loud, with the time on it, which is the way he does everything and the reason this room trusts him.

"Eight pails, in two crossings of four. Two people. One pair at a time, at a walk, no hurry at any point because the only thing worse than a slow crossing is a fast one. The nine steps to the school door, the door itself at two foot eight, and then about forty feet of corridor where the floor is a fill that has moved three times and is currently under nine inches of standing water from the pump, which is **the pump's water**, which I want in the book, because that is the water that is coming up, and the water that is coming up is the water that took the packing out of the waist in the ninth week, and I put the packing in, so this is mine twice.

"Price. **A day and a half of a shutter route for Isel, who is standing at a fill instead of at a door and is not being paid for either.** A day of my own trade unpaid, which I have thirty-one years of, and which does not go in the crew's column because it is my choice and the crew's column is for work somebody asked us for and nobody asked me. A day of school. And a day of a pot, because the water arrived two hours late in the day and Mikka ran it thin, and the number is on the board and the number is **thirteen.**"

"Thirteen," said Dalla Sen, and read it back, and it went round the anteroom and everybody heard it and nobody said anything, because the number had been nineteen before any of them came up a shaft and had gone down on every single day since, and the last time it had gone down by more than one in a day was the thaw in the eleventh week, when it went from nineteen to sixteen in one Tuesday afternoon and a woman carried what was in the trough by hand, and that was a worse drop than this and it was three, and this is the first time it has gone down by more than one on a day where everybody could see why.

---

Marek Venn went down on the Tuesday, inside the terms, with the terms read at the school door, on a day the school did not run and in the pump's window, and went as far as the fixed first landing and no further, which is the rule and has been the rule for eleven weeks.

He took the bar that lived on the near landing's step in his left hand, because his left hand is the one that works, and he went down the three steps and the service bend, and he put his left palm flat on the dressed stone of the far wall of the throat at about the height of a man's chest, which is the height Bel Ordry's bar is put at, and which he had worked out in the eighth week by asking Bel Ordry and not by guessing.

He put it there for as long as he had ever put it anywhere, and he waited, and he listened past his own hand the way he has listened since the seventh week, and the cold in that throat was four degrees and the difference between his skin and the stone was a hand's breadth and it was, as it had been for four weeks, the loudest thing in the room.

Nothing came.

He went back up and up the nine steps, and he did not write *null*, and then he did write it, and the doing of the two in that order took about ninety seconds on the common landing.

"Right," he said, to Tams Quill. "Tams. Give me the board and the ordinary column and give me the chalk and I am going to write **null** in the ordinary column, in my own hand, with the date, and it is the eighth, and it is going in the column with the other seven and not in your column with no heading, because I have been wrong about the cold for a fortnight and I am putting it back where the other seven are, where a person in a year can find it and count it."

"Eight," said Tams Quill, and took the board, and watched him write it, and then said, "Now I am going to move it."

"Tams—"

"I am going to move it and I am going to say why and you are not going to like it. A null in the cold is a different instrument reading a different room, and that is in the book from the fourth day of the eleventh week in your words. **So the eighth goes in my column with no heading with the temperature on it, and it is the second one there, and the column now has two, and a column with two in it is a column.** And I am not going to head it, and I am not going to let you head it, and in four weeks there will be four and it will be a bucket and not a column and that will be the end of it, because a bucket is a thing you can put a thing in and a column is a thing you can add up and I have not got a heading for a thing I can add up and I am not going to invent one in the middle of a fortnight."

Marek looked at the board for a while, and the two of them were the only two people in that anteroom who had said nothing at all for two minutes.

"Second one," he said.

"Second one," said Tams Quill, "and the temperature, and the reason in your words, and I am writing *the same reader, the same place, the same hand, six days after the first, and he came back for it on purpose and did not have to.* And the count of nulls stays at eight and the eight are all in the book and all of them say the same thing, which is that Marek Venn has put his hand on that stone eight times in thirteen weeks and got nothing eight times, and there is not one entry in any column of that board that anybody in this room is allowed to build on."

---

On the Thursday of that week the corridor came back for a single crossing and went shut again, and the first thing the room did with the four inches it had gained was put it on the record wrong on purpose, and Bel Ordry did it, and Dalla Sen caught it, and it is in the book because being caught is the point of doing it.

"Slump, third event, along the left side, about five foot, in the words of the keeper and a carpenter and a pump maintainer, all three of whom stood on it at separate times in one day." Bel Ordry read it back. "**Measured: nothing. Measured by: nothing.**"

"You had three of us stand on it and nobody measured it," said Jo Verrick.

"I had three of us stand on it and nobody measured it," Bel Ordry agreed, "and that is the truest sentence anybody has put on that page in a fortnight, and I would like it read out, and I would like a person forty days away to be able to read *measured: nothing* and understand that it is not that nobody could. It is that we have three boots and a bad one and a cracked stone and a wedge that is telling us, and the wedge is up a quarter and a bit, and that is the finest instrument in this Hearthhold and it is a piece of split hardwood with a number on the end of a public-works inventory."

"Read out," said Dalla Sen, and read it out, and the corridor gave up its one crossing and shut again, and two people had carried four pails each over a fill that has moved three times on the shoulders of a man who priced it in his own hand, and the number in the kitchen had gone to thirteen, and under the floor the pump was run through the whole of its cycle — twenty-six strokes, six minutes off, nineteen — and the check kept to its single note, and the water in the dip stood four inches under a chalk line that has not moved since a woman with a slinged shoulder put it there, and on a cold wall a bar had said nothing three times and something three times with a slide on the end of the something, and the page that said all of it had a second page laid against it, and the second page had a date on it, and there was nothing between them.
