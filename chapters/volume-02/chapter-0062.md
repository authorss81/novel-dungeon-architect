# Chapter 62 — Asking

They went down the nine steps at the fourth bell in the cold, and the cold came up the stair at them, which was the first thing nobody had expected and the thing that told them the week was different from every week they had been here.

It came up because the stair is a vent and has been a vent for nine weeks, and a vent that has been breathing out from a warm building for nine weeks does not have a cold in it, and this morning it had. It came up the nine steps and along the common landing and under the school door in one long lazy push that smelled of wet stone, and Ilsa Norr, who was at the front of it because it was her day's work, said, "That is the shaft breathing out," and then said, "It is not, is it. It is breathing *in*."

"It is breathing in," said Nera Oduya. "It is four degrees and it is eleven feet deep and cold air is heavy, and the shaft has stopped being a chimney. Everything I have taught this room about that stair this winter was a warm-weather lesson and I taught it in the autumn and I did not say so, and that is on me and I am saying it now while it is in front of us and not in a week when it is in a report."

Renn Vale said nothing. She had come down the nine steps behind Isel Ferrow with her hand on the rail and she had felt the push come up past her, and her face did not change, and Marek Venn — who was at the back with his left hand on the wall, and who had learned in eleven weeks to read the back of a person's neck almost as well as he read stone — saw it change and said nothing, which was the correct thing and cost him something.

---

Four stops, and Iven Tallo named them before anybody asked him to, because a carpenter who has been asked to look for wood is the only man in that building who is not a person doing a favour.

"Right," he said, at the top of three steps with the cold still coming up past them. "Hardwood, eleven inches, seasoned damp, sound. I am going to walk this Hearthhold and I am going to look at every stick of wood in it and I am going to say no to most of them, and I am going to say the reason out loud, and if anybody in this corridor tells me I am wrong I will take it and I will be glad to be wrong in the first hour of a day.

"And I want one thing said before we start, because it is the thing that has gone wrong in every workshop I have ever stood in. **The best wood in a building is almost never the wood anybody can have.** It is the wood somebody put where the damp is, and it is there because it is the piece that had to survive, and the piece that had to survive is the piece that somebody needed to be able to forget about. So: the better it is, the more certain it is that it is doing something, and I am going to ask what before I ask whose, and I am going to ask it out loud so that a person has the chance to stop me."

Nobody stopped him, because there was nothing to stop him for yet.

---

**Bel Ordry's two rooms were above the clinic return, and they were reached by a ladder off the corridor that nobody else in this building had any business on, and Bel Ordry came down it in the cold without a coat and stood at the bottom of it in the corridor with his arms down.**

Bel Ordry was thirty-five and he had held nothing in eleven weeks and he now held a bar against a wall every morning at the second bell, and he had a key to nothing, and nobody in that building had ever asked him what he was going to do with the day.

"Two rooms," he said. "One with a bed in it and one with a stove in it and no bed, And there is wood in there. There is one piece of wood in there and I have known about it for eleven weeks and I have not mentioned it, and I am mentioning it now because that woman at the frame asked for eleven inches and there is a floor-board in my mother's room that is a single piece of oak the length of a door and it is the only sound thing of its size in this Hearthhold and I have turned it over every morning in my head for eleven weeks and I have never once turned it over with a chisel."

"Bel," said Mikka Hale.

"I am not finished, and I have a floor in there that has been cold since the collapse in a way I have never asked anybody about."

"You are not finished, and I am glad, and I am also going to say the thing nobody wants, which is that it is a floor, and a floor is not a store of floor, and if you take a floor out of a room in the twelfth week then there is a hole in it in the twelfth week and a draught in it in the twelfth week and you are the person the draught arrives on, because you are the one who lives there." She put her hand on the door frame. "So: a floor, and a room, and a man in it, and December. In a Hearthhold with a saw and a carpenter and a spare month, yes. In this one, no, and the reason is not hardness. The reason is that you would be spending your mother's floor on a pump seat and you would be cold in your own room by the end of the month, and a man who is cold in his own room stops being a person who can stand on a two-foot-eight gap at the waist and hold a bar against a wall every morning. That is the whole of it and I have said it and I would say it again."

Bel Ordry looked at the floorboards of the corridor for a while.

"Yes," he said.

"Yes," Mikka agreed. "Now the other half, which is the half that matters, and Bel, look at me, because I am going to say something and then I am going to stop and you are going to take it or leave it.

"That oak is yours. It was in her room and she is not here and there is no other claim on it that I know of, and if you cut eleven inches out of it this afternoon then it is a gift. And **a gift in this building is a lock.** A thing that is given to a Hearthhold by a person who lives in it is a thing that can be asked for back, and a thing that can be asked for back is a thing somebody can say *you have had enough out of them*. I have watched four households in a second gate carry water up four steps all winter on the strength of a piece of paper a courier withdrew, and I am not saying that about you. I am saying it about the arithmetic, because the arithmetic does not care who is kind."

"I am not going to be thanked for it," Bel Ordry said.

The corridor was quiet for a moment in a way that had a shape to it.

"Say the rest of that," said Renn Vale.

"If I give it and somebody in this room says *thank you, Bel*, then in three months there is a line in that clerk's book with my name on it against a thing this Hearthhold has got, and I will have become a person who gave something to this boundary." He said it without any heat at all, which was how the frightening things got said in that building. "I have lived in this Hearthhold for eleven weeks and I have held nothing and I have been given nothing, and it has been the best eleven weeks of my life, because a man who is given nothing cannot be asked for anything. And I am not going to trade that for a floor and a line in a book. So: cut it, and put the eleven inches in the pump, and **do not thank me, and do not write my name under it, and let it be a thing that is simply in the pump.**"

Dalla Sen said, from behind them, "I am going to have to write something down about that, and I want you to tell me now whether you want me to write it in your words or in mine, because they are not the same sentence."

"In hers," said Bel Ordry. "It is hers. I only said the shape of it."

"It is not mine either," said Mikka Hale. "It is his, and I have said his and not mine, and put that in as well."

---

**The school store was a box of a room off the corridor on the school-door side, and Ilsa Norr unlocked it herself and stood in the doorway and let nobody past her, which is how she does it.**

"There," she said. "Two lengths. They were a door lintel in a door that is not this door, and they have been in this box since before any of you came up a shaft, and they are beech, and they are sound, and they are the best of the two-foot lengths in this Hearthhold and I have never once used them and I have thought about them every week for eleven weeks.

"No."

"Ilsa—"

"No, and I am going to say it in one breath so that nobody spends a morning working round it, and then I am going to shut the box, because a shut box is a decision and an open box is an invitation.

"They are the props under the school store's shelf. That shelf is dressed stone and it has dropped twice since the collapse and the second time it dropped it came down onto the top of nine register boxes with a corner off one of them. Take the two lengths out and the shelf comes onto the floor and the register comes with it. And the register is nine boxes of the only continuous record this building has kept about itself, and every one of them is somebody writing down how many children came, and how much fuel there was, and what a lintel was, and it is the only thing in this Hearthhold that anybody can be shown in a year and be believed.

"Twelve days ago a clerk from an office forty days of walking away asked to see what is in that box and I said no in this room in front of those people, and I said it because that plan on a dowel is a decision about what this building is. And that answer would have been a lie if this morning I had handed two lengths of beech out of the same box to a pump. You do not put a thing on the table in a room and then say the table is a wall."

She shut the box, and the key went back into her apron, and nobody said anything about it.

"And I want it said that I would have said yes," Ilsa said. "If the shelf had been sound. That is not me being hard. That is me telling you exactly what the reason is, because the reason is a shelf, and the shelf is a thing you can fix, and I would rather you knew that about me than thought I enjoy saying no."

---

**They got to the wash-room end at the second hour and there were eleven of them in a corridor two foot eight wide at its narrowest, and Mikka Hale went ahead because the water is hers, and Renn Vale stopped the whole party twenty feet short of the door.**

"Right," Renn said. "That is as far as this goes and I am stopping it, and I am not being asked and I am not asking anybody to agree with me.

"There is a rack in that room. I have seen it. It has a piece of wood on it that has been in a damp room for thirty years and it is the only piece of wood in this Hearthhold that a seat could sit in and stay put, and Mikka has seen it too, and Mikka came past me to say it, and neither of us is going to say anything else about it, and neither of us is going to be asked."

"Renn—"

"And here is why, and it is not the floor." She said it to the corridor and not to Marek. "The floor of that room is refused and it stays refused and that refusal is older than any of you. And the woman in that room has been asked for nothing and has said yes to the two things she was asked for, and one of those two things put a dead man's mother under a slab. So if anybody walks up that corridor with a list of what they want out of that room, then that list is the first thing she has ever been asked for by anybody in this building, and I am not going to be the reason it is a list.

"I will go to her door. I will ask her. I will stand on her step while she says whatever she is going to say, including no. I have not done it and I am not going to do it in a corridor with eleven people behind me, and I am not going to do it this month while there is a pump with no seat in it and a clerk with a book. **When I do it, it will be me, at her door, and you will all be told what she said and none of you will be in the room.**"

"That is five things now," said Isel Ferrow, from behind, "that this boundary wants, and one woman has just refused to go and ask for a single one of them."

"That is five things now," Renn agreed, "and I have got four reasons and one of them is that I am frightened of a list, and that one is mine and I am not putting it in a book."

---

**The last stop was under the floor of the anteroom, and nobody opened it, because there was no reason to open it, because the woman who had put the pump there eleven weeks ago could stand at the frame and say what was under it.**

"There is a bed," said Jo Verrick. "Two lengths of elm, laid on the stone, running front to back under the frame, and the frame sits on them, and the housing bolts down through the frame into the bed. And it has been down there since before any of us and it has been under standing water the whole time, and it is elm, and elm takes water and holds it and does not go brittle, and it is the best-conditioned piece of timber in this Hearthhold by about thirty years.

"I have known that for four weeks. I have not said it out loud in four weeks. I am saying it now because I am tired of a search and because I have just spent a morning being told no four times by better reasons than mine, and a woman who has not said her own piece out loud is not searching, she is waiting to be asked."

Iven Tallo had gone very still in the middle of the anteroom, and Marek Venn, who knew that stillness, understood that it was the stillness of a man who has been handed a thing he wants and is looking for the line that takes it off him.

"Then it is the bed," Iven said slowly. "That is the answer. That is the piece. And I am not going to let anybody put a chisel near it, and I am going to say the reason before anybody else does, because I am the one who would want to.

"The housing has to sit square on that frame. It bolts down through the frame into the bed, and the bed is what takes the pump's whole weight and the whole of the load of that water on top of it, and the seat has to seat against a housing that has not moved. You take eleven inches out of that bed and the two bearers it lies on are eleven inches short across the width, and the housing settles a half inch on one side the first time the pump comes on hard, and everything Jo has said about a set face becomes a fair copy of a crooked room. And a seat in a crooked housing does not wear out. It gets loose in three weeks, and a loose seat does not announce itself, and Jo is going to be standing at that frame with a flat hiss and no instrument in the cold in three weeks with a head that has come up by itself."

Then Tams Quill, from the bench with the tally board in his left hand:

"Read the carried column."

"Tams—"

"Read it. I have been asked to read it every week for five weeks and I have read it four times and nobody has ever asked me to read it at the moment it decides something, and this is the moment it decides something, and I am a man with one hand and a column and I am not going to be told to wait."

Longhand, in his left hand, with the whole anteroom quiet behind it.

"Carried. One item, in Oren Sill's words, and I wrote it longhand because I was frightened of losing it. *You do not pack a shore with wedges. Wedges take up slack. A shore is packed with a face, and a face is made by putting the right piece of wood in the right place and then leaning on it until it goes in. So: a piece of the packing offcut, sized, set at the toe, and then a man on the head of the timber with his whole weight and his own knee, and the wood goes in under him or it does not go in at all, and if it does not go in you take it out and cut a different one, because a shore packed with wood that was not sized is a shore that has a spring in it, and a shore with a spring in it puts its head out four months later and takes a person's hand off at the knuckle.*"

"That is a shore," said Iven Tallo. "That is a shore, and it is not a seat, and it is not the bed, and it is the only sentence anybody has said to me in thirty-one years that has ever told me why I was wrong about a piece of wood. A bed is not a shore. A bed is packed and then it is **finished**, and a finished thing is a thing that can be taken, and Jo has just told me the pump needs a bed for thirty years, and I have just told her she cannot cut it, and those two sentences are the whole of this morning and they are both true and they were both said by the people who knew."

Iven put his hands on his knees.

"So the answer is no. That took one day, which is what Ilsa asked for, and the answer is no, and I would like it written down that four of us walked a Hearthhold in the cold looking for eleven inches of wood and did not find eleven inches of wood, and that nobody lied to us, and that everybody who said no said no for a better reason than hardness. That is the first search I have ever been on where that was true."

---

They came up the nine steps at the third bell with the cold coming down at them, and Sefu Aran took the answer on the wheel in one sentence and then asked the question that turned the day into the week.

"Write that down as a keeper," he said, "and I want it in the book that anybody can read, because it is not a complaint about the Hearthhold and it is a fact about the reach of it.

"There is seasoned hardwood in this building and there is seasoned hardwood at the middle gate, four days on a road a courier has just walked in both directions, and there is a saw at the middle gate and a stock and a water-lock keeper with a name. And between this pump and that store there is four days of road and a corridor with two places in it that are two foot eight wide, and one of those two places is standing over an eleven-foot shaft on ground made of fill. So: **I cannot get a plank to my own pump, and neither can any of you, and that is not a shortage of planks. That is a shortage of reach.**

"And the middle's water-lock is dry, which is why a courier stood in this room and withdrew an order, and the timber that would put that lock right and the timber that would put this seat right are the same timber, and the middle knows that, and that is why nobody has sent anything up the middle passage in six weeks, and not one word of that is anybody's fault."

Sefu's hand went onto the wheel.

"So I am the keeper of a boundary that cannot get a plank from a road four days long, and I am not going to pretend that is a thing I can hold. It is a thing I have to say out loud, and I have said it, and it is in the clerk's book now, and in a year somebody can read that this Hearthhold was asked a lawful question by a man who keeps its gate and could not answer it because of a corridor."

---

Marek Venn had done most of the day with a slate under his arm, and at about the fourth bell he stopped being useful and became a man walking behind a search, which is a thing he had been in this building for eleven weeks and had never once noticed being.

The sentence that stayed with him was not the one about planks. It was the one Sefu Aran had said at the top of three steps before anybody went down: *the crew asked for by me by name.* Eleven words, said to a room full of people who had spent eleven weeks not being asked for anything, and three people had said yes, and the work had been arranged, and nobody had been given anything, and nobody was owed.

Thirty-two years old, a civil engineer for Marrowgate Public Works, trained in shoring and drainage and evacuation planning and the writing of documents that other people have to sign, and the whole professional apparatus of his life had been the production of a sentence like that one. He had never said it in a room. Not because he did not know how — because there had always been a form to put it in, and the form was easier, and the form had a line for the person who maintained the route and a line for the date it was inspected and no line at all for *would you come and look*, and so for eleven weeks he had written four forms worth of nothing and one slate with *null* on it and had never once said, out loud, in a corridor, *I am asking for your afternoon.*

He got it from a man on a wheel who had a store he could not get to. And he understood, standing there in the cold with a slate he was not going to write on, that this was the thing he was actually going to have to learn, and that it was the thing he was worst at, and that no woman at a frame and no man on a wheel was going to be able to teach it to him.

He was going to have to be the one who said it out loud, badly, to somebody who was allowed to say no.

---

The tally that night gave the crew's column as **forty-one and a half**, which is three of them for a day, on a named search, asked for by name by the keeper, and the boundary's as **sixty-five**, which is six people a day and Isel half and Mikka's flue wall for the day, and Tams Quill read both and read the finding, which had not changed in four weeks.

"The half that gets counted is the half that is ours," he said, "and I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to shut the board, because it is the eleventh week and it is getting cold and I have a right hand that opened this morning for twenty-two seconds and I would like to be allowed to stop.

"I have got a new column and it has got no heading. I have got a line in it for a search that cost three of us a day, and a line in it for a day when a man stood in a cold corridor outside the anteroom and a woman at a frame told him no, and neither of those is an hour of anybody's life. **A day is not an hour and a day is not a thing anybody in this building has been counting, and there are going to be more days than hours before this is over, and I have left the column blank on purpose because I do not yet know what to call the thing in it.**"

And the check hissed under the floor, thin, and there was no wood, and the search was a day spent finding out that, and the day was not nothing, and it was in the book in three hands.
