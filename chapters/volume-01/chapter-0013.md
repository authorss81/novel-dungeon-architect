# Chapter 13 — A Charter Is Not a Signature

By late afternoon, Dalla opened a new two-hour record interval at the work-bell. The preceding maintenance interval had been reviewed with the pump stopped, the air cloth steady, and the water below the sill. Sefu counted the same ten occupants; Renn and Mikka stayed on the landing, and the courier remained beyond the incline. The work sheet had acquired more corrections than signatures.

Marek spread it across the anteroom bench and weighted the corners with two smooth stones. Jo’s cleaning notes ran down one side in cramped, forceful strokes. Nera’s air limits occupied the other. Dalla’s shift record crossed the bottom edge. Sefu had written the boundary conditions in the narrow space between them, and the courier’s documents waited on a separate board where their seals could not be mistaken for the pump’s condition.

Marek had read the old Cartwright route charter twice. It was written for the inspection incline: a narrow surveyed line, a hand-operated upper catch, a licensed witness, and a stated date and occupancy. The service shaft, the air gate, the anteroom, and the Hearthhold rooms beyond had no place in it.

“The charter is clear,” Marek said. “It does not cover this route.”

Iven tapped the page without touching the blue seal. “It was never meant to. The guild calls the inspection incline a route because it maintains the incline. That does not make every adjoining work a guild line.”

“The order calls the service works associated,” the courier said.

“Associated is a word for a hearing, not a hinge in the door.”

Sefu looked from the charter to the deeper gate. “The old charter gives your guild conditions for its door. It says nothing about who sleeps beyond ours, who enters a kitchen, or who may refuse a stranger at a clinic bench.”

He put his finger on the blank lower half of the page. “A route charter has to start here. Anyone affected can refuse. A room keeper can refuse. The boundary keeper can refuse passage. I can stop the gate, but I cannot make a resident accept a route, and no resident can make me open a mechanism I believe will fail.”

Nera adjusted the dyed cloth above the air valve. “I can certify the air path for a named occupancy, time, pump condition, and stop condition. I cannot certify the character of the people using it.”

“And I can explain what the guild can freeze,” Iven said. “The surface door. Guild-financed equipment. The route witness privilege attached to my name. The inspection record until the hearing. I cannot freeze the Hearthhold pump, the residents’ tools, or their consent. The guild can refuse parts, refuse a record, and refuse rescue. Those are real costs. They are not permission to take the room.”

The courier’s expression did not change. “The guild can also challenge any claim that its work has been absorbed into a local route.”

“That is a claim to be heard,” Iven said. “Not a fact to be signed by the person who repaired the pump.”

Marek looked at the blank sheet. A charter was supposed to turn a physical route into a durable agreement. He had spent most of his life believing a properly framed document could keep a repair from becoming a favor, a danger, or a private claim. The Hearthholds were asking for something more difficult: a document that admitted it had to be corrected by the people who used it.

“Write a single agreement,” he said. “We will name the anteroom, the service shaft, the pump, the air gate, the maintenance shifts, and the people who can stop the work. No one has to guess which rule applies.”

Dalla sat beside the repair ledger. “A single agreement can still have blanks.”

“Not if we fill them.”

“The blanks are where people have not agreed.”

Marek looked at her. “Then we make them agree.”

“You can ask. You cannot make agreement into a consequence of needing a pump.”

Renn remained at the landing lantern bracket, one hand on the door rail. “The four of us can agree to our work. Renn and I can say when we will watch the door. Jo can say when the pump check needs cleaning. Mikka can say when the kitchen cannot spare her. None of us can sign for a resident who has not entered this room.”

“Nor can I sign for every Hearthhold,” Sefu said. “The charter can be an agreement at this boundary. It becomes a wider public charter only when the people who use the route ask for it.”

Marek’s pen rested above the paper. His right hand could not hold the nib squarely. He had already dictated most of the heavy sheet to Iven, but this was the part he wanted to control himself. He could make the words clean if he were allowed to choose them.

Leena’s voice came from the cot. “Do not dictate a promise to an injured woman.”

He looked toward her. She had her left hand on the blanket, the right arm secured in its sling.

“Write the limits you can prove,” she said. “Leave the rest visible.”

Marek moved the pen aside. “All right.”

He began with the route record. The six-person crew had reached the air-gate anteroom through the physical service bypass. The anteroom had passed the declared twelve-person test and received a working limit of ten people, including one stretcher, for no more than two hours, with no open flame, the outer service door open, the pump attended, and an evacuation to the upper landing if air reversed or water reached the sill. The Hearthhold Middle gate remained closed. The returning stair remained closed to use. The pump drew flooded sump water and required an attendant.

Sefu added that the route was not public passage. Nera added that her air approval ended when the air, occupancy, or stop conditions changed. Iven added that the gland, drive-dog key, timber lever, and maintenance record were local work, not a transfer of route custody. Dalla added the delivery-check cleaning, the cost of Jo’s deferred route work, and the rule that no person could be assigned a shift merely because they were present.

The courier watched the legal distinctions take shape. “The order does not recognize this sheet as a charter.”

“It is not asking the order to recognize it,” Marek said. “It is recording what the people using the route have actually agreed to do.”

“Without guild consent, the inspection line remains closed.”

“Keep it closed.”

The answer came from Sefu, not Marek. He had one hand on the civic wheel and the other on the bench. “The guild line is yours to close. This room is ours to maintain or refuse. You may ask us to hear a claim. You may not turn that ask into a key.”

The courier looked at the deeper gate. “Some residents may hear it.”

“Some residents may,” Sefu said. “Not all of them. Not by your order.”

A pump stroke sounded below the floor. Everyone waited until the delivery check had passed its uneven beat before the conversation resumed.

Dalla drew a line down the page. “Now the hard part.”

She wrote *status of the six* beneath the route description.

Jo leaned over the bench. “If they are beneficiaries, they get a room, water, or a share because the route exists. If they are claimants, they get a say because they repaired the pump. If they are temporary workers, they get a shift and leave when the work ends.”

“They have already done work,” Iven said. “That gives them a record, not a percentage.”

“Work can be a claim,” Jo replied. “The guild thinks so. Dael’s order thinks so. Iven’s signature proves that even a bounded repair leaves somebody ready to call it theirs.”

Iven’s jaw tightened. “I did not claim the pump.”

“You claimed the work,” Dalla said. “That matters.”

Marek saw the trouble in the word *claimed*. The crew had carried out the repair, but the residents had supplied the route, the air knowledge, the pump parts, the room, and the permission to stay. The repair had not made the outsiders owners. It had made them difficult to remove without answering the question of who would do the next shift.

“Call them temporary maintainers,” Marek said. “They do work under the sheet. They receive no ownership. They can withdraw when the work ends.”

“Can they withdraw in the middle of a pump cycle?” Leena asked.

“Then the pump stops,” Nera said. “The water may rise, but nobody is trapped by a promise.”

“And if we withdraw the maintenance agreement while they are in the anteroom?” Sefu asked.

“New admission stops,” Dalla said. “People already inside leave by the outer landing. Consent cannot become a locked door.”

Sefu nodded slowly. “That part I can support.”

Marek wrote it. The line was not elegant, but it was clear: withdrawal stopped new work and new admission, while the escape route remained open until the room emptied.

Jo tapped the word *temporary*. “That is not my whole status. I am Jo Verrick. I carry the tool baskets. I do not become a blank space because I am helping a crew repair a pump.”

“Your name is beside the shift,” Dalla said.

“Then put the limits beside the name too. I am not responsible for the route after the pump stops.”

Iven looked down at the page. “And I am not the author of the Hearthhold pump merely because I fitted its seal. The housing, the plinth, the air baffle, and the boundary wheel were built and kept here. I translated the repair. I do not take the old craft from the room that taught me what it does.”

Nera glanced at him. “That is the first useful sentence anyone has said about guild work today.”

“It is late,” Iven said. “But it is useful.”

Marek looked at the residents’ names. Renn had written *door and upper landing* in the margin. Dalla had written *record and tool inventory*. Jo had added *pump check, one trial shift*. Mikka had not yet put her name down. She stood at the threshold with a bowl in her hands, outside the counted room.

“I can provide recovery water and meals for the next work period,” she said. “That is not a charter promise. It is what the kitchen can spare while six people eat here.”

“Does that make us beneficiaries?” Marek asked.

Mikka gave him a tired look. “It makes you people who need food. If you become beneficiaries, the Hearthholds will spend the next year explaining why you deserve meals you did not pay for. If you become claimants, I will spend the next year explaining why a stranger’s repair gives them a claim on our kitchen. Call the work what it is.”

“Labor supplied for a temporary route,” Dalla said, writing.

“And the cost returned when it is possible,” Leena added. “Not a blank forever.”

Marek had wanted a clean agreement. The page before him was becoming a map of uncertainty: a named shift beside a named limit, a door kept open beside a gate that might close, a meal promised beside a line still open for refusal. It was less satisfying than a charter because it allowed people to remain unpersuaded.

The courier lifted the old route charter. “If this is the document, Dael’s office will treat it as an unauthorized local work sheet.”

“Call it that,” Sefu said. “Then the guild can answer for the line it closed. We will answer for the room we maintain.”

“And if the guild refuses parts?”

Jo’s mouth tightened. “We clean the check more often.”

“And if it refuses records?”

Iven looked at the locked incline. “We keep our own copy and state that it is our record, not the guild’s.”

“And rescue?”

No one answered at once. The pump knocked again below the floor.

“We do not count on rescue,” Marek said. “We do not pretend that the order is harmless because we can live beside it. If the guild refuses support, we keep the route within local resources or we withdraw under the same stop conditions.”

Sefu looked at him. “Withdraw to where?”

Marek had no clean answer. The Cinder Steps lay behind the service bypass, with water rising behind a temporary flood gate. A retreat was not an escape. It was a cost, but it was not a forbidden one.

“Named in the route record,” Sefu said. “Not promised as a safe place.”

Marek wrote it as a fact: *retreat route physically continuous; condition and load not currently certified.* The paper did not become less dangerous by admitting it.

The page was full enough to use and unfinished enough to be honest. It named the anteroom, the pump, the air limits, the maintenance shifts, the consent boundaries, the stop authorities, and the cost already incurred. It did not name a permanent route, grant ownership, create a public passage, or make the four volunteers spokespeople for every resident.

Marek read the last lines aloud. “No resident is admitted by presence. No work is assigned without a name. No air approval is civic consent. No guild order opens the Hearthhold gate.”

Sefu took the sheet and read it again. “No route is kept open by the person who first made it useful.”

Nera touched the edge of the page. “And no air approval survives a changed condition.”

Iven looked at the order on the bench. “And no repair signature becomes a custody transfer by being convenient.”

Dalla closed the ledger over the draft. “Then we have a maintenance draft, not a charter.”

The word *charter* had sounded final when Marek first picked up the pen. Now it sat on the page as a question with several people holding different edges.

The pump’s delivery check gave a single heavy knock.

Jo looked toward the service well. “If the next cost is another half day, we decide who pays before I start it.”

Marek marked the blank. The room had a record now.

It did not yet have a promise.
