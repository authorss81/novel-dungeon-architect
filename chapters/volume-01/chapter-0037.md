# Chapter 37 — The Turn in the Middle of the Work

The rule Nera had not put on the board turned out to be the only thing that mattered, and the crew found that out on the third morning, four minutes into a period that had been planned to last an hour.

They were on the near side, and the near side had nothing on it. That was the point. Step one of the plan was a sounding: Nera's draw cord with a stone on the end, dropped into the throat over the near lip, to find how deep it went, what shape the bottom was, and whether the far wall could be got at. It set nothing. It changed nothing. It began where the near side was found and it was designed to end in the four minutes of any turn, and Jo had written that on the board in four words and had read them out at the threshold: *every step ends as found.*

Marek had fought for that clause for a quarter of an hour and had lost the fight the way he kept losing, by being right, and by being right in a voice that made the person who needed the clause uncomfortable.

Jo put the stone on the line herself and Oren held the belay and Tams had the slack, and Iven watched from the step with his mouth shut.

The draw cord went down about eight feet, and stopped.

Not slack. Stopped, with a jar in it, the way a jar goes into a hand that has found the edge of a table.

"Steady," Oren said. "Nobody pull."

They did not pull. They counted, and the count came to a number, and the number was not a depth.

"Eight feet of line and a catch," Iven said. "And eleven feet of shaft, by Nera's count, which I have no way to check from this side. So the stone never reached the bottom. It caught on the band on the way down, which is the one thing on this stair anybody has been able to see, and it is a thing a line snags on." He looked up. "Which means the far wall is eleven feet down, and this building has eighteen feet of line in it and every foot of it is spoken for, and I still could not tell you whether there is an eye in that wall, because the light does not go where an eye would be."

"The hand line is on the school-door head," Tams said. "The draw cord is eight feet and it is Nera's and it is on the valve board and I have written it on the board, and eight feet is not eleven."

"Eight feet is not eleven," Iven agreed, "and it is the only line in this building that came free, and I am telling you the same thing I told you in the anteroom, which is that the length of the line is not the problem. The problem is that the light does not go where an eye would be, and if there is a fixing down there then eight feet and eighteen feet and forty feet would all find the same water." He did not take his eyes off the lip. "Get it up."

They were getting it up, and the stone was coming up the throat slow, catching on the bright band on the hollow's front face, and Oren was taking the weight hand over hand, and Tams had the slack in his left and the belay in his right, which was his habit and which nobody had ever thought to change, when Nera's voice came up the string.

"Out. Now."

They were out in fifty seconds. The belay came up behind them with the stone on it and about two feet of line still out, because Oren had stopped paying it out on her word and had taken the weight instead, which is what the four minutes were for and what the four minutes had been bought with.

The wheel turned twice. The cord held. The hatch stayed latched. And this time the anteroom did not come back to ten for nineteen minutes, and Dalla's four minutes and six seconds became four minutes and fifty, and the reason took the better part of an hour to find, because nobody guessed it and everybody looked.

It was not at the stair.

"Sefu," Nera said, at the wheel. "Get the wash rooms. Now, and say why on the way."

"Grim," Sefu said to the two people nearest him, and went, and came back with Ilsa and Mikka and the woman from the wash rooms, and Ilsa was carrying a slate and Mikka was carrying grit in the palm of her hand, and neither of them was shouting.

"The corner," Ilsa said. "The cover is up."

Jo went to the school corner first, and everybody else went when they were told to, and the anteroom got its ten back with the door shut and the queue held back, and the school corner door stood open, and it was nine children and a teacher on the floor and grit in a line.

The cover had lifted at the kerb on the wash-room side. Not much. Two fingers at the far corner, and the near corner still seated, and the whole thing still in its frame, and a wedge of daylight in it like a mouth. And out of the joint, along the dressed kerb, in a line about two feet long and about the width of a finger, the floor of a school corner that had been swept that morning was white with grit.

Nobody moved in it. That was Mikka's doing, standing in the doorway with her arm out, and the children went and sat against the far wall in a row, and one of them was holding his hand flat on the joint, which was the thing Ilsa had described in the fourth week, and which now had a reason.

"Do not touch it," Ilsa said, in her ordinary voice. "Nobody touches it. Pell, the bench. Take them to the bench and sit down and do your thing."

She did not cry and she did not raise her voice, and Marek understood, watching, that this was the second time in six weeks he had seen a teacher not raise her voice, and that the first time had been on a school floor with a hole in it.

Nera went down on one knee at the kerb with the dyed cloth held low, and did not put a hand near the grit, and after a while said, "It is air."

"I know it is air," Marek said. "Look at where it is."

"Do not tell me what I can see. Tell me what you see, so that I can decide whether it is the same thing I see."

Marek crouched where the grit had stopped and looked along the line of it without touching anything, because he had learned in the sixth week what his hands were for and what they were not for.

"It is not a leak," he said. "A leak makes a bloom. It widens and it goes soft at the edges and it comes out from under a thing and it wets the stone. This is a line. It has started at the kerb under the cover and it has run with the joint, and it has run to the wall, and it has stopped at the wall in a straight edge, and it has not run under the bench or out into the middle of the floor." He sat back on his heels. "Water does not do that. Air moving through a void does that. It goes where the void is."

"And where is the void?" Nera said.

"Under the far end," Jo said. "Under the wall plate, by the store. That is where I found it on the second day. That is the gap two fingers wide under the wall plate, and it is not the channel, and it is bigger than the channel, and it goes down into the old wash room."

Sefu said, "The old wash room has people in it."

"It has a family in it," Mikka said. "The woman who lifts our cover at the second bell. And her mother. And there is a bed in there and no other room in this building with a bed in it."

The anteroom was full and the school corner was full and the wash rooms were on the other side of a wall, and the fifth-bell cover check had put a woman in that room and nine children in the next one, and the crew was on a step eleven feet from all of it with a draw cord they were paying back onto a belay.

Marek turned round.

"Sefu," he said. "Can you feel it?"

"No."

"Neither can I." He said it plainly, in front of the room, because it had stopped being something he protected. "I have written the word *null* five times since the twenty-first day and I have never once had anything come back that I could use, and if anybody in this room has been waiting for me to develop a sense for this, they should stop waiting. What I have is a rule and a lamp and a woman who counts."

"It is enough for what it is for," Nera said, and did not soften it, and it was not a kindness. It was a statement about tools, and she made it the way she made most of her statements, which was once.

They went at it in the order Jo's board said, and the writing turned out to be the part of the morning that mattered afterward.

She put the stop line first, because the standing rule was that a stop carries its reason and a person may leave at any time and the clock does not stop, and Jo had argued that rule into the page herself and was not going to let the worst morning of the month be the morning she forgot it.

She wrote: *fifth bell, turn called by Nera Oduya from the string. Party out in fifty seconds, off the step, clear of the lip, draw cord up. Reason in her words: air condition in the shaft. Nobody argued, nobody asked, nobody stayed.*

The reason was a category and not a reason, and she knew it, and she wrote it down anyway so that the two people who would correct it would have something to correct.

Then Sefu disputed a word.

"Forty minutes and fifty seconds is not a stop. It is an evacuation. Say what it was."

"I said what it was. I said *turn*."

"You said *turn* and you wrote fifty seconds, and fifty seconds is the party, and the room was shut for nineteen minutes, and those are two different lengths of time belonging to two different people. Write both. The nineteen belongs to whoever counted it."

"Nineteen minutes is mine," Mikka said. "I timed it on the pot, which is the only clock in this room that nobody argues with, and you can check it against my tally. The tally says the pot was not on at nineteen, so the nineteen is until the pot could go on, which is not the same as nineteen minutes of nothing."

"Write it as the time until the pot," Jo said, and wrote it, and Sefu read it and did not sign it and did not refuse it, which from Sefu was the same as agreeing to the correction and not to the sentence.

Leena disputed the order of two lines, because Leena disputed the order of two lines as a matter of professional principle and never as a matter of temper.

"You have the turn above the clearing," she said. "The turn was called, and the party was out in fifty seconds, and the school corner found the grit at about the fourth minute, and you have put the school corner before the clearing. In a record that a person will read at two in the morning after a turn, the order of the lines is the whole of the record. It is the only thing that tells them what happened first."

Jo swapped the two lines and read them both back.

Nera disputed the reason.

"Air condition in the shaft," she said. "That is a category, not a reason. A reason is *the cloth moved and the pressure came up under this floor*, and if the line reads as a category then the next person to read it will not know whether to send people to the stair or away from it, and they will be one of my neighbours."

"Write what you told me," Jo said, and Nera told her, and Nera said it in short words and did not use the word *gradient*, and Jo wrote it in the plainest hand she had.

And then the tally of what it had cost, which Jo read out because she had learned that a cost said out loud in a room becomes a decision and a cost written quietly becomes a number.

Tams had hurt his hand.

He was sitting on the step with it held in his other hand and he had not said anything about it while the room was doing its work, which was the least Tams-like thing Marek had ever seen him do. The line had caught on the bright band in the hollow's front face and come up with a jar, and he had taken the jar on his right, because his right had been the belay hand since the first week, and the stone came up faster than his hands expected.

"Two fingers," he said, when he was asked. "I cannot make a fist. I can hold a slate and I can count and I can tell you a thing is wrong, and I cannot pull on anything for a while, and I do not know how long a while is, and I would like nobody to decide a while for me."

"You cannot work the lever," Jo said. "It is a two-handed pull. You have said so yourself since the second week."

"I have."

So that was the cost, and it was the cost that mattered, because the lever was the corridor. The lever was the interval, and the interval was the only condition that made that floor a floor, and it ran on Tams, and Tams had two fingers that would not close.

"I can take it," Oren said.

"You are at the frame," Jo said.

"I can take it and somebody else takes the frame, and I have held both in the first month with a different pump and a worse plinth." He looked at the two of them. "And I want the frame held by somebody who is not going to fall asleep, because I am going to be at a lever for six minutes at a time for nine days thinking about my hands, and a bored person does not drift."

"Tams," Marek said. "Are you—"

"I am not on the frame and I am not on the lever and I am not on the near side," Tams said, "and I am going to do the water record and the count and the reading of the mark to Isel, and Isel is bad at it, and somebody has to be at it while he is bad at it, and that is a job and I would like it written as a job and not as a favour to a man with a hand."

Nobody argued. It went on the board in his own words, because Dalla had made that a rule in the twenty-fourth day and he had fought it and then stopped fighting it.

And then the condition, which was the last thing and the thing that changed the plan.

Nera and Jo went at the school corner floor with a lamp and the lift bar, and lifted the cover, and looked down the channel and down the void, and the channel was dry and clean and doing exactly what a week's-old drain does.

The void was not clean.

It was full to about two feet below the wall plate of grit, deep and pale and dry, and the air was coming up through it and the grit was coming up with the air, and it had been doing both for years. It had stopped at the wall plate and run along the joint, because the joint was the path and the void under the middle of the floor was packed hard and had never once let a breath through it.

"This is not your floor's fault," Nera said, to Ilsa, who had said nothing since she had sat the children against the wall. "The floor is laid as it was laid. The void is the old wash room's floor, and the wash room's floor is grit and no channel, and the water comes up out of that end of this building and goes into the channel, and everything fine has gone to the channel and everything coarse has stayed. So the only mouth in this school corner is the end, and it is under my mother's wall, and it has been breathing into this room since before the school was a school."

Renn was in the doorway.

Not in the room. In the doorway, with her coat on, the way she came in.

"My mother washed in that room for forty years," she said, "and she never once opened the floor, and she put a piece of a stair back in a socket by hand in the cold because there were litters waiting upstairs and nowhere else to put them. And now you are telling me the room she washed in has been breathing into a room with nine children in it."

"Yes," Nera said. "I am telling you that."

"Then say the rest of it. Say what it is going to mean about a project that has two people in it standing on a stair."

And Nera said the rest of it, standing at the valve with the cloth in her hand, with the record open on the bench and the woman from the wash rooms in the doorway with grit in the palm of her hand.

"First: the plan on the board is wrong and I am the one who has to say so, because the plan assumed the stair's air went down the service bypass and up the shaft and out at the throat, and the school corner is on the same pressure line, and the school corner is a mouth. Second: that floor has to be opened, traced, and given a way to be shut or drained, and it is not the crew's to do at the crew's convenience, because it is under a school and under a wash room. Third:" and she stopped, and it was the only time in six weeks that Nera Oduya had taken longer than her answer needed, "I am not going to promise anybody that opening that floor will make the stair's condition better, and anybody who says it will is selling. What I will say is that a room with nine children in it is breathing out of a hole, and that no plan gets to be designed as though that is a small thing, because a small thing is how the first forty feet of this building were built."

Nobody spoke for a while, and then Jo picked up the board, which was hers, and read the plan from the top, and put a line through the second step, and wrote underneath it: *withdrawn. The step assumed the school corner floor was a room and not a mouth. It is a mouth. The mouth is opened and traced before anything is built, and the mouth is a room people use, and Jo Verrick writes it, and the date is on it, and the people who keep that floor are asked before it is done.*

She read it back, and Ilsa asked for one word changed, and Jo changed it, and Dalla copied it into the ledger. They went down through the kitchen line for supper with the corridor shut, and the anteroom took its ten back at the lever, and the school corner cover sat in its kerb with two fingers of daylight under the near edge, and nine children on a bench had all watched it and would remember it.
