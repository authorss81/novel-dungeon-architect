# Chapter 29 — A Full Room, Again

The second test was run on a wet floor, on purpose, by people who had learned from the first one.

Tams carried four buckets of sump water up from the gallery and spread it along the corridor's full length before anyone was allowed to stand in it. It was the wrong thing to do to a floor and the right thing to do to a floor, and Dalla wrote both halves of that sentence into the record, because the record was the thing that would stop the next engineer from re-inventing a clean corridor. The crew's five liters stayed on the case board where they had been for six weeks, the sealed condensation liter stayed in its jar, and a named work period meant the kitchen's provision was the crew's drinking supply; nobody poured a bucket of sump water into a skin, and nobody said that was an unnecessary caution.

They used a litter that had done clinic work two days before. Its legs were fouled, its frame heavier by a hand's width of wet canvas, and it could no longer be lifted by two people at the corners without someone dragging a foot. That was the stretcher condition, and nobody had invented it: it was simply the litter on the shelf.

Nineteen people had wanted to be in the test. The declared number was nine, plus one litter, plus the five watches at the boundary, and Mikka said the arithmetic out loud before anyone could make it larger.

"Nine is what the strip will take," she said. "Fourteen is what will actually turn up. Write fourteen as the interruption and write nine as the room, and when fourteen people come, the last five go somewhere with a name on it."

The plan got its terms. Occupancy: nine in the corridor including one litter, the interval of the pump, and no more. Duration: two pump intervals, about thirty minutes, with the corridor emptied completely at every lever-off. Gear: ninety kilos along the kitchen-side strip, out of the dip, on boards. Exit: the kitchen line only, and the school door recorded as a relief, not an escape. Stretcher: four bearers, wet frame, dragged feet allowed for and counted. And the interruption rule from the first plan, unchanged and now with a number attached: a person may leave at any time, the clock does not stop, and Dalla writes down who left and how many remain.

They ran it at the second bell, with Renn on the landing, Mikka at the clinic return, Dalla at the threshold, Sefu at the wheel, and Nera at the valve.

For eleven minutes the corridor did what a corridor does when nine people are standing still in it with ninety kilos of stone on boards beside them. The strip held. The litter came down the kitchen line on four shoulders without anybody's foot going into the dip, and Leena called the minutes and the bearers from the anteroom bench, because a person who has been carried out of a room is the only person in the building qualified to say how long it took. A boy of six came out of the gate with his mother, counted himself in without being asked, and immediately asked whether he was allowed to go and see the grate, and was told that the grate was not part of this and that he could go and see it afterwards.

At minute twelve the delivery check chattered.

Not the ragged double knock they had all learned to live with. A hard, tearing chatter that came up through the floor and up the shoring lengths, and Jo was at the waist before it finished, and she said one word.

"Stop."

The lever came off. The corridor emptied through the kitchen line in under a minute, because it had been emptied at every other lever-off all week and the muscle memory was better than the plan. The six people who had not yet been counted as inside the room stood in the kitchen doorway watching, and the boy with them.

"Sediment," Jo said, from the floor. "Not a hard set. It has got under the lip and it is packing, and if I had run two more strokes it would have seated and I would not have known until the water came up through it."

"How long?" Marek asked.

"Forty minutes if I do it properly. Twenty if I do it the way I would do it on a Tuesday."

Nobody said the second number out loud, and Jo did not offer it, and it sat in the room anyway, because the third cleaning was the one where a person starts cutting corners and then blames the seat.

They cleaned it properly. Jo had the housing open in nine minutes, Iven had the frame, Oren and Tams took the load, Pell called the water from the stair head, and Nera held the air with the pump chamber isolated. The sediment came out in a grey plug and the bronze lip showed its scrape, and Jo wiped the seat and looked at it for a long time.

"It is not worse," she said. "It is exactly as bad, and it is exactly as bad again in about four cleanings."

"Then we should plan for a replacement and stop pretending," Marek said.

"Plan for it in the list. There is no bronze in this building."

Jo wrote the cost in herself, on the corner of the slate, the way she always did, before Dalla could get to it: *fourth cleaning. Half a day of a route that is already shut for the season. Nothing deferred this time, because there is nothing left to defer.*

The pump went back on for the second interval and the corridor was refilled to nine, and the boy was counted back in without any discussion at all, and at minute twenty-two something came through the floor that nobody had planned for.

A slow, low grinding, out of the direction of the pump, under the corridor, near the channel.

Nera's hand went to the stop handle above the valve before the sound had finished. She did not call it. She opened her hand, looked at it, and then said one word.

"Out."

The corridor emptied. Mikka rang the clinic bell once, Renn called the door clear, and Dalla rang twice from the threshold, and the anteroom cleared to the landing with the litter first and the boy second and the last two of the corridor party third, because Ilsa had said in writing that the class pattern was a class pattern and a room that empties badly was going to find out about it.

The grinding went on for eleven seconds and stopped.

"That is the stair," Marek said.

"That is the stair," Nera agreed, "and it is not the first time this week, and the water in the channel went the wrong way for it."

She was crouched by the drain cover with two boards lifted and one hand in the channel, and the water in it was moving uphill, slowly, toward the pump gallery, which was the direction the water always went, except that the pump was not drawing and the channel had been falling to the pump gallery all week.

"The stair pressure is pushing air down the service shaft," she said, "and the shaft comes up under the school end of this corridor, and the air is bringing grit up with it. That is what is in the channel. Not silt. Stone."

Nobody had thought about where the corridor's air went. It went to the pump gallery, because the pump gallery was the lowest pressure in this part of the building, and the corridor was a pipe with a door in each end and a warm room in the middle of it.

Marek got out the board and drew it, and the drawing was not what he had drawn the first time, because now there were two arrows and one of them was not a person.

Nera went back to the test with the numbers and a new condition, and this is the part that went into the record as the change: the corridor may be used while the pump is running, and the pump running was what pushed the corridor's air out rather than letting the stair's air in. So the corridor is a bellows, and the bellows is run by a machine with a scored bronze seat and no spare, and the moment the machine stops, the room begins to breathe toward a stair that nobody is allowed to touch.

They ran the last interval anyway, because there were eleven minutes left on the board and eleven minutes of evidence was eleven minutes more than they had.

Nine people. Ninety kilos. A wet floor, a fouled litter, four bearers, a child who wanted to see the grate, and a woman of sixty-three who walked slowly and had to be counted as a person moving rather than a person standing, which is the distinction a load test exists to make. At minute thirty-one the litter came down the kitchen line and turned at the point where the strip narrowed, and the four bearers got it round, and the strip's chalk line at the waist moved three-eighths of an inch while it was happening and came back when it was gone.

Marek put his left palm on the wall and found nothing useful, as usual, and said what he could see instead: "The fill is washed out under the strip from the waist to the second bench mark. It does not mean the corridor is finished. It means the corridor is honest about how much it has left."

Sefu read the result aloud at the threshold and wrote it where it could not be moved: *nine in the corridor including one litter, for the interval of an attended pump, with six watches filled, the lever announced, and the school door not counted as an exit.* Below it he wrote the limit, and Dalla wrote it in her own hand, and nobody added anything, because there was nothing left to add.

*This is not a rating. It holds while the pump runs and the seat holds. Neither has a known end.*

* * *

Nera stayed at the valve after the others had gone down.

She had been doing this for two weeks, standing at the valve with a strip of dyed cloth in her hand, and it had taken her until now to admit that the cloth had not been telling her about the anteroom. It had been telling her about the building.

The cloth above the valve responded to the anteroom. The air behind the clinic return responded to the corridor. And the thing that connected them, which she had known since the seventeenth day and had filed under wet seam, was not a wet seam. It was a shaft. The mineral line beside the pump housing was a pressure-fed line, and it carried water when the stair pushed and air when the stair pulled, and the corridor's low end sat directly on top of it, eleven feet of stair-pressure shaft with a drain cover over the top of it.

Which meant the corridor was not a room with a hazard nearby. It was a room on a hole.

She had known the line was there. She had known it carried the pump and the air baffle and the service bypass and the stair. What she had not put together, in six weeks, in all the watching of cloths and stopping of valves, was that the one place she had certified as safe to stand people in for eleven minutes at a time was standing on the one part of the system she could not see into, and could not watch, and could not maintain, and did not own.

She wrote it on the valve board in her own hand, in the plainest language she had, because it was going to have to be the first thing anybody said in the morning.

*The corridor's air is the pump's air while the pump runs. It is the stair's air the moment it stops. Any future use of that corridor is a use of a hole we have corded off, and it cannot be described as a room until somebody can see down that shaft.*

Then she hung the cloth on its hook, and put the valve in its attended position, and went to tell Sefu before the shift ended.
