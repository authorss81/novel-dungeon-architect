# Chapter 56 — The Reckoning

Tams Quill read the tally out in the anteroom at the fifth bell in the tenth week, and he read it in the order he had invented, and he read two columns that had not been read out together before, and the room found the second one harder than the first.

"Received," he said. "One length, five foot nine, and it is a shore now, and the number is still chalked on the end of it under a face, and it is not ours.

"Spent. One nine-metre line on a ladder foot. One cracked lamp. One pry bar. One screw jack. Two refusals of salvage, and a third refusal is not written yet and I will not pretend it is. One check, in the way a thing is spent when it stops being the thing it was and starts being a noise you listen to. The anteroom's four foot eleven cut into packing sizes, and what is left of it is a two foot ten and a handful in Jo's basket, and the basket is Jo's and it was Jo's before I got here.

"Held by the boundary, out of a work nobody outside this room is on the rota for: two shores on one landing, a bell rope, a restriction cord, a keeper's cord of forty feet that a keeper released and that is wearing a band into a wall, and a ledge of cut stone under a school's floor that a schoolteacher's floor now lies on top of. And a length of timber that was in a man's hand on the seventh week and is not now.

"Carried. One item, in Oren Sill's words, and I have read it out four times this week and I am going to read it out every week for as long as I am in this building, and that is a promise and I do not make many.

"Water and wedges and the stores are the ninth week's and I read them out on Monday and the reading has not changed, so I am not going to read them again. One thing has changed and it is the pot, and it has not changed in my favour: nineteen days of meals, given and not waived, not down by one, and I am putting them into arithmetic as well as words now, because a thing that is not hours can still be a debt. And a seat with two cleanings on it or fewer, in Jo's own hand, and a check that is a hiss."

He put the chalk down, and picked it up again, which was how Tams ended a figure.

"Now the two columns," he said, "and I have never read these two together and I have been waiting all week to do it and I would like the room to understand that I have been frightened of it."

---

"Mine," he said, and turned the board, "is thirty-eight and a half. It has not gone up. It went down. We built two shores instead of one and it went *down*, because I am not on the lever, because I am not allowed on a bar, and because a man who was on the lever for three weeks in the seventh week is under a stone and is not on this board anywhere, and the board does not have a column for that and I am not going to invent one at the fifth bell on a Thursday.

"Theirs," he said, "is fifty-one."

The room made a noise.

"Fifty-one," Tams said. "Forty-six when I read it in the ninth week and forty-six when I read it before the shore went in, and it is fifty-one now, and I have checked the arithmetic four times because I did not believe it either, and here is where it comes from, and I am going to read the items and I am not going to round them.

"A day and a half of Jo's shutter route, gone in the seventh week and not recovered, and the route was already shut for the season, so that is a day and a half of a woman who has already lost it for the year. A half-day of it again for the closure in the ninth week. A day of Ilsa's school for the shut corner and a day again for the route check. Nineteen days of meals, given and not waived, which do not go in a column of hours at all because they are not hours, and I am putting them in anyway tonight because I have decided that a thing that is not hours can still be a debt. Mikka's pot, twice. A watched flue wall that Mikka took on as a cost of her own choosing and would not let anybody pay her for. Two hours of a clerk's own evenings. Four hours of Renn Vale's, which she keeps in her own head and which I have now put on a board, and she can strike it out if she wants it struck, and she has not asked me to, and I would like her to say in this room whether she wants it struck."

"It stays," said Renn Vale, from the upper landing.

"Then fifty-one," said Tams, "and the finding is the same finding I had in the ninth week and it has got worse and not better. Their hours went up five and ours did not go up at all. The half that gets counted is the half that is ours, and I have stopped trying to divide it, and I am not going to stop saying it, and the reason I am saying it tonight is that there is a woman on a bench downstairs with an order for a slab, and somebody in this room is going to be tempted in about nine minutes to offer those hours, and I am putting the number on the board first so that whoever does it has to do it in front of fifty-one."

---

Then Sefu Aran asked for the leaving, and asked for it in the anteroom and in front of the clerk, which is a thing he had been asking for since the seventh week and had never once got.

"Read me the sentence about the spring," he said, "and then read me what it is going to cost, in the words it is written in, and not in yours."

Marek Venn had it in his own hand on a slate, and he read it out, and the room had heard it four times.

"That the five of us will leave this boundary, and that the leaving is a conversation in the spring, and that the spring conversation is with the boundary's air engineer by name, and that the air hand-over is a named thing with an hour on it, and that the five of us do not go north until it has happened."

"Say the second half," said Sefu. "The part you wrote that I made you write again."

Marek looked at the slate for a while.

"That there is no hour of the maintenance we hold that is owed, and that the maintenance ends when the boundary can do it without us and a person named by the boundary has said so out loud, in this room, with the reason."

"That has not been said," said Sefu Aran. "Say the number."

"It has not been said."

"So the obligation is open, and it is open in the direction that you are leaving and not in the direction that the work is done, and I want both halves of that on one line, and Dalla will read it back, and Tams will read it back, and I will say yes to it once, and once is enough for me."

---

Nobody was prepared for what happened next, and what happened next was five words from a schoolteacher, and she said them standing in the anteroom with a slate under her arm, in the flat voice she used for a thing she had decided on the way into the room.

"Who keeps the Hearthhold rooms?"

The wheel went round twice under the floor and nobody heard it.

Ilsa Norr let it stand for about as long as it takes to boil a pot, and then she said the rest of it, and she was not asking anybody to agree with her.

"Nine weeks," she said, "that question has been asked about the *route*. It has been asked about the stair and the shore and the line and the bell and the door, and eleven people have stood in this room and said the word *maintain* out loud about every one of them, and Renn has a road, and Jo has a pump, and Mikka has a pot, and I have a floor.

"Nobody has asked it about the rooms.

"And I want to say why I think it has not been asked, because I have thought about it for two days and I am not going to put a question in a room without saying what I think is underneath it. I think it has not been asked because every one of us in this building thinks, in a place we do not say out loud, that somebody is already doing it. That is what you do. You have been in a room for nine weeks with people in it and you assume the walls are somebody's job. And the walls are *not*. The walls are nobody's job. There is a floor under nine children and it is mine because I said so out loud in the ninth week and I paid for it, and if I had not said so out loud and paid for it, nobody would have laid a floor, and there would have been a hole under a school, and it would have been *nobody's fault*, and nine children would have been on the other side of it."

Nobody in the anteroom said a word.

"So I am asking it out loud," Ilsa said, "and I am not going to be let off, and if somebody in this room knows the answer I would like to hear it, and I have got the rest of the week to be disappointed."

---

Corva Nye had the answer, of a kind, and she gave it the way she gave everything, which was as a fact about a form.

"I have a line for it," she said. "I do not have a line for the answer. Let me show you the shape of what I have got, and then you can all be unkind about it."

She put the form on the bench, flat, and did not read it out, because a form is not a record and she knew it.

"There is a line for the holder of a route. There is a line for the charter, and the number of the charter, and the date it was inspected. There is a line for the keeper of the *workshop*, which is a store and a man with a key. There is a line for the *office*. There is a line for the *guild*. And there is no line anywhere on this for a woman who teaches nine children and pays for a floor out of her own hand and shuts a door because she decides to."

"The form has a line for the owner of a building," Sefu said.

"It has," Corva Nye said, "and that is the one everybody uses, and it is the one that puts a name on this boundary inside about four years, and I have watched a name get put on a building nine times in six years and every single one of the nine was done by a good person filling in the only box."

"Write it in the margin of your own," said Dalla Sen. "You said you would, and you did it for the route, and I am asking for the same thing twice, and I would like it in the same hand and the same ink and no more readable than the other one, and I would like the clerk to read it back before it goes in the file."

And Corva Nye wrote, in the margin of a Cartwright form, in her own hand, that a route in the Hearthhold boundary is maintained by the people whose rooms it is under and held by nobody, and that she was told so by the keeper and understood it and thinks it is the best answer she has ever been given.

And, in the same margin, on the same afternoon, she wrote that the form has no place for a keeper of rooms, and that this is why forms fill with owners, and that a clerk filling in a form is not a criminal and neither is anybody in the room.

---

And then somebody proposed opening the school register boxes, and the refusal was immediate, and the refusal is in the ledger for good.

"I want to see them," said Corva Nye, "and I am asking properly and out loud, and the reason is not curiosity. I have filled in a form with three lines for a route and I have written in the margin that it does not have the right lines, and I would like to know whether this building kept its own records about itself, because if it did then there is a ninth line on somebody's old form somewhere and I can find it, and I can spend a year of my life finding it instead of four years."

"They are in the school store," said Ilsa Norr, "in a locked box, and I have the key, and I have had it since before any of you were in this room, and the answer is no, and it has been no since the ninth week, and I am saying it again in front of the same people so that the second no is a fact and not an echo."

"May I say why," said Corva Nye, "so that I know whether it is a door or a wall?"

"You may say why you want to and I shall not answer it, but I will tell you what is in the box without opening it, because I have never needed to open it to know what is in it and I am tired of people thinking the key is the interesting part."

Ilsa Norr put the slate down on the bench, which she did not have to do and did anyway.

"Nine boxes," she said, "and they are school register boxes, which are waxed board and they nest, and they are on a shelf in the store and they have been on that shelf for longer than the school has had nine children. Attendance rolls. A fuel book for a stove. Two lengths of wood that were a door lintel and are not a door lintel any more, because the door was a different door. A box of copper coins that is four inches deep and is not worth anything. One box of maps of the ground above, which are not any good and which my predecessor said to keep because somebody would want them.

"And one plan, rolled on a dowel, with a tape round it, in a box of its own, and it is the only one of the nine that is on its own, and it is the only one I have never opened, and I am going to tell you why.

"It is on a dowel. You roll a thing on a dowel when you expect to unroll it again, in a hurry, by somebody who knows how, and nobody in this building has ever unrolled it because I have never let anybody, and I have had the key since before the collapse and I have not needed it, and that is not secrecy. I will tell you the reason and the reason is the reason and not a wall.

"Everything in that other eight is a thing somebody did and finished. Attendance. Fuel. A lintel. Coins. A map. Somebody did it, it was kept, and it is done, and nobody has to decide anything about it again.

"**That plan is a decision about what this building is.** And it is the only old record of this building that anybody in here has ever seen, and every one of us has been living on the story we have been told, which is that these places were built to hold treasure, and I have had that key in my apron for nine weeks while five strangers slept over my floor. I am not opening it in the week after a man died under a stair, on a pump that has started making a noise it did not make before, because if I open it and it is a house, then I have to sit here and be the woman who found out. And I am not refusing you out of anything but that. I am refusing you because I would have to be somebody else on Friday, and I have not the week for it."

"That is a yes in three weeks," said Sefu Aran.

"That is a yes in three weeks," Ilsa said, "with a name and a date on it and the room in it, and the room does not get to be anybody but the people who live here, and I will say the terms when I say the date, and one of the terms is going to be that the crew are not in the room when I unroll it. That is not me being hard. That is me knowing what is on a dowel."

---

And then Talla Roke said the thing that made the question more expensive, and she said it the way she said everything, which was once and without ornament, and she said it to the anteroom and not to anybody in it.

"I have carried a salvage pick through eleven Hearthholds in four years," she said, "and I have slept in nine of them, and I have been given the story in every single one, and the story is always the same and it is always given to you by a man with a licence. The story is that there is gold in these places and the Cartwrights found it and the good people took it and now it is being robbed. I have never once seen a vault. I have seen a kitchen with a blocked drain. I have seen a school with a stone floor and a stove flue that goes into a seam. I have seen a room with a line of pegs in the wall at the height of a person's shoulder.

"And I have carried plans. Nine plans in four years, off the middle gate's survey shelf, where they keep what came up with the drainage work. Every one of them is drawn like a house. Rooms. A stair. A thing marked with a line that means a level and not a treasure. Doors drawn on hinges. **I have never once seen a plan of these places with a vault on it, and I have never met anybody who could tell me why the plans are drawn like somebody walked through them and looked at the walls.**"

She put her hand flat on the bench.

"So either somebody has been lying to me and to every house I have slept in for four years, or these were homes, and the plans are homes, and the plans are the oldest thing anybody has and the richest thing anybody has, and the Cartwrights have had them the whole time under a shelf in a drainage store and the story they sell upstairs is *there is gold*." She looked at Nera. "I would like somebody in this room to say which of those two it is, and I would like it said before I am thirty-one days on the road again, because I have got two brothers and a sister on a list and I would like to know what I am carrying them water through."

And Nera Oduya, who had the whole of the air in this boundary and had refused to certify a stair and had lost a man inside her own refusal, looked at the slate board for a moment and said:

"I do not know. I have a valve and a room and eleven weeks of readings, and one of the two things on my board that I cannot account for is the same shape as a reading that came up under a school floor, and I have never told anybody what that shape is because I do not know what it is.

"And I am going to say the honest half, which is that I have not opened that plan either, and I have not asked for it, and if Ilsa Norr unrolls it in three weeks then I will be in that room and I will be the only person in it who cannot be trusted with what is on it, because I will want it to be a house, and wanting a thing to be a thing is the worst possible condition for reading a thing that is old."

And the anteroom went on being a room with a pot in it and a wheel under the floor, and Dalla Sen wrote the question down in the ledger in her own hand, and did not put an answer under it, and then read out her own reasoning to the room the way she always does, because that is the practice. And when she had finished, Ilsa Norr took the key out of her apron and put it flat on the end of the bench, in front of everybody, and left it there while she said the rest of it, and nobody in that room touched it.

"Written, and no answer, and here is what a person reading it in a year is going to need," she said. "They are going to need the week it was asked in, and who asked it, and who was in the room, and they are going to need the sentence about the dowel and the three weeks, and they are going to need the fact that the air engineer said she would not be trusted with it. And they are going to need to know that a clerk from an office forty days away was in the room, because in a year there will be a clerk from an office and this time he will know what to ask for.

"So: asked in the tenth week, in the anteroom, by Ilsa Norr, and not answered. And I am not putting *pending* on it, because pending is a word that makes a thing look like it is being worked on."
