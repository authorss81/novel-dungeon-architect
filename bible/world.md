# World Bible

## 1. The setting in one sentence

The Morrow Basin is a dry, crowded surface civilization whose roads, food, and political security depend on entrances into the Lattice, a buried ancient refuge that is trying to repair itself after its original control system failed.

## 2. Two scales of society

### The surface

The surface settlements sit over porous limestone, old riverbeds, and the buried Lattice. Marrowgate is the first city Marek knows: a river-cut manufacturing city with public works departments, surface tramlines, dry wells, and a political class that would rather call the Lattice a sealed hazard than admit how much of its water and air comes from below.

The wider surface is the **Rim**, a loose network of towns, caravan roads, farms, and city-states. The **Stonelight Compact** is the largest surface federation. It wants controlled access, taxes, and a reliable way to keep a dungeon disaster from opening under Marrowgate. Some Compact engineers and local officials sincerely prefer cooperation; others use safety regulations to turn access into leverage.

Surface technology is recognizable industrial craft: hand pumps, timber shoring, masonry, steam-driven pumps, lamps, cable lines, surveying instruments, and early electrical lighting in wealthy districts. The Lattice adds unfamiliar materials and a deeper engineering logic, not a replacement for every tool.

### The underground

The Lattice is a vast, buried refuge built by the **Cairn Commons** roughly twelve centuries ago. Its rooms sit at several depths, but “depth” is not a simple ladder. A room can become a market, a reservoir, a workshop, a school, or a fault chamber depending on what people have repeatedly done inside it and how well its forces are managed.

People who live there are not visitors. They make repairs, grow food, pump air, teach children, maintain pumps, run kitchens, salvage materials, record births, bury the dead, and argue over water. The network of independent neighborhoods is collectively called the **Hearthholds**. A Hearthhold may be one stable room or a chain of rooms connected by a maintained route.

## 3. How the Lattice behaves

The Lattice has no single mind. It is a self-repairing structure built around old civic rules. It responds to four inputs:

1. **Load:** the force of stone, water, heat, crowds, machinery, and magical stress.
2. **Flow:** the movement of air, water, heat, waste, and people.
3. **Use:** repeated human routines, such as sleeping, cooking, teaching, healing, or storing water.
4. **Memory:** patterns retained in old materials and repaired rooms; it is an imperfect record, not a truthful archive.

### The five checks for a room

Every occupied room must pass five practical checks. Wardens call them the **Room Checks**; they are a field discipline, not a magical status panel.

- **Bearing:** the weight has at least two independent paths into stable rock or anchors.
- **Breath:** air enters and leaves without using the only safe exit shaft.
- **Drainage:** water, heat, waste, and combustion products have somewhere to go.
- **Escape:** at least one route remains usable when the room is full of people.
- **Change:** ordinary expansion, crowding, a cart, a fight, or a fire will not exceed the room's original load budget.

A room can pass all five checks and still be dangerous because of politics, disease, monsters, or a bad decision. Passing the checks is the minimum condition for calling a space a refuge, not a promise of safety.

### Rooms grow from repeated work

A room keeps the shape of a function that has been maintained. A room used for grain storage develops broad floors, low shelves, and dry vents. A room used for an infirmary develops washable surfaces and multiple exits, even if no one consciously designed it. When maintenance stops, the function decays into a **faultform**: a physical hazard that repeats the room's unresolved last action.

Examples:

- A **returning stair** reforms at its first landing and separates the people who need different exits.
- A **doorhand** grows from a door that was repeatedly locked on occupied rooms; it reaches toward anyone who delays a closure.
- A **sump ox** is a heavy, slow form in flooded lower rooms; its body is carried by water pressure and a stalled drainage path, so breaking it does not repair the flood.
- A **glass choir** is a cluster of reflective panes that repeats the last crowd's movements and can lead an evacuating group into a dead wall.

Faultforms are dangerous because their rule is specific. The correct response is to identify the function they repeat, redirect the force, and give the room a workable alternative. Violence alone may scatter a form and leave the underlying failure intact.

### The Lattice does not grant shortcuts

Opening a route requires a counter-support. A narrow shaft can become a shortcut only if the pressure it removes is carried somewhere else. Opening a door can improve air flow while worsening a load on the wall above it. Every useful change has a footprint in a neighboring room.

The Lattice can shift exposed routes over time, especially near entrances and unstable sectors, but it does not teleport people, create food, answer questions, or obey a spoken command. It is a damaged machine with inherited habits, not an oracle.

## 4. Materials and practical resources

- **Anchorstone:** dense gray-green stone that carries stress and holds a mark. It is heavy, scarce, and cannot be quarried safely without understanding its local load path.
- **Joinwood:** a pale fungal timber grown in seams between stones. It is light and useful for temporary braces, but it burns quickly and fails if humidity changes without warning.
- **Bellows moss:** a gray-green lichen that traps dust and keeps narrow air passages from becoming poison pockets. It needs light, moisture, and occasional cleaning.
- **Remembering glass:** translucent mineral panes that hold a limited pattern of pressure, footsteps, heat, and repeated gestures. They are not mind-reading and can show an old pattern rather than the present one.
- **Siltglass:** clear, brittle material formed where pressure and heat meet. It makes excellent windows and terrible load-bearing walls.
- **Spore salt:** a preserve and antiseptic crust harvested from dry galleries. It is valuable, edible in small amounts, and expensive to transport.
- **Black cord:** a tough cave fiber used for temporary bracing, warning lines, and climbing supports. It is inexpensive but cannot hold a load beyond its tested capacity.

The Lattice's most valuable resource is not treasure. It is breathable air, stable water, safe routes, and maintained anchors.

## 5. Depth geography

The series moves through these named regions without treating them as a simple video-game level list.

### The Cinder Steps

The first sector is a sloping set of terraces beneath Marrowgate. It has old transit cuts, unstable stairways, sealed service rooms, and a shallow water problem. New guild routes pass through it because it is close to the surface. It is the first place where the Lattice's room functions become visible.

### The Hearthhold Middle

A chain of older, inhabited rooms with pumps, kitchens, workshops, schools, and market spaces. It is the closest thing the underground has to a public neighborhood. Its stability depends on residents doing maintenance that no external guild can afford to perform.

### The Wet Galleries

A broad network of reservoirs, flooded rooms, vertical drains, and air shafts. The water is not a single underground sea; it moves through chambers that were once civic and industrial spaces. The galleries are the center of the first real air-and-water crisis.

### The Quiet Wardens' Reach

Older administrative, burial, and record rooms maintained by the Wardens of the Lattice. The order has split into people who believe the deep should be closed and people who want the old civic knowledge returned to the neighborhoods.

### The Loom Deep

The oldest and least stable network of structural chambers around the Civic Loom, a distributed control system. The One-Seat sits in this region as an empty socket surrounded by evidence of a failed attempt to centralize control. The Lattice's deepest paths are not a guaranteed reward dungeon; they are a civic engineering emergency.

### The Unmapped Black

Beyond the known routes are sealed cells and undocumented voids. The final story does not fully map this region. Some questions remain open because the answer would require destroying a habitat or violating a local community's choice.

## 6. Factions and institutions

### The Cartwright Guilds

Guilds of surveyors, route-clearers, lantern-keepers, salvage operators, and rescue workers. They sell route charters, claim salvage rights, and train most people who enter the Lattice. Many members are honest workers trapped in a dangerous economy. The **Ninefold Charter** is the dominant cartel within the guilds and controls the recognized clearance seals.

Their conflict: safer routes require open records and shared maintenance, but open records reduce the guild's ability to claim exclusive access. Some guild leaders want reform; others profit from uncertainty.

### The Hearthholds

Independent underground neighborhoods with elected route keepers, craft circles, water stewards, schools, kitchens, and clinics. They are not one government and do not always agree. At a maintained boundary, an air engineer may control airflow and certify a mechanism while an elected route keeper controls civic admission and can veto a crossing; neither authority automatically speaks for every Hearthhold. Their central demand is that outsiders cannot call a route private if the route crosses a home, a school, or a water system.

### The Stonelight Compact

The surface federation that regulates most major Hollowmarks. It has engineers, inspectors, soldiers, and public-works officials whose jobs depend on keeping the Rim stable. Its hardline faction wants to seal the Lattice and confiscate its materials. Its cooperative faction wants a treaty with the Hearthholds and a public record of safe routes.

### The Wardens of the Lattice

Custodians descended from the Cairn builders and later rescue traditions. The **Open Wardens** believe civic knowledge should be distributed and taught. The **Seatwardens** believe a single trained custodian can prevent another Sundering. Both groups preserve real records, and both have caused deaths through overconfidence.

### The Quiet Hands

A loose, non-state network of people and machines that sabotages large civic projects. They believe the Lattice is repeating the history that created the One-Seat and that a safer outcome is to prevent any city from forming. Their methods endanger ordinary residents and reveal the legitimate fear beneath their position. They are an opposing faction, not the final enemy.

### The Cairn Directorate

An ancient governing body, not a currently visible government. Its surviving protocol is the One-Seat: a system that concentrates decisions in one operator. The Directorate's argument was that one person could coordinate a disaster. The Lattice's Sundering proved the argument false. The protocol remains a structural and ideological threat, and it is present in the world from the first volume.

## 7. History and political background

### The Cairn refuge

About twelve centuries ago, the Cairn Commons predicted the **Long Undertow**, a continent-scale pressure event that would tear apart exposed settlements. They built the Lattice as a refuge for millions. The original design had many local anchors, flexible routes, and maintenance distributed among neighborhoods. Its governing principle was simple: no person, room, or district should be the only bearer of every load.

### The One-Seat and the Sundering

During the Long Undertow, the Cairn Directorate feared that disagreement would slow the evacuation. Its engineers installed the One-Seat, an emergency control core that let one living operator make and enforce decisions across the whole refuge. The system prevented some immediate collapses but made every decision depend on one body's ability to carry the load. The seat was overloaded, the network fractured, and the Lattice suffered the **Sundering**. This event created the unstable sectors, repeated rooms, and faultforms that later cultures call monsters.

### Rediscovery

Four hundred years later, surface communities found the first stable entrances and began treating the Lattice as a dungeon. The guild economy grew around clearance seals, salvage rights, and control of safe routes. The Hearthholds formed later, not from a single rebellion but from people who could not survive the guild system without building homes of their own.

### The modern breach

Fourteen years before the opening, public works investigator **Alden Venn**, Marek's father, disappeared during an expedition through the Sable Cut. His brass survey rule and incomplete notebooks survive. Modern surface construction reaches a deeper seam during the tunnel collapse that sends Marek into the Lattice. The breach is not Alden's resurrection or a deliberate summons. It is the same slow pressure cycle that has been threatening the Lattice for centuries.

## 8. The lived dungeon

Not every chapter is a descent. The series must show what happens between runs:

- a pumpkeeper learns which seal is failing before the room floods;
- a route keeper argues over whether a dangerous shortcut should remain open;
- a child learns the five Room Checks in school;
- a cook invents a better heat baffle and changes the menu;
- a guild surveyor loses a finger and receives a wage that cannot cover the injury;
- an underground clinic runs out of clean cloth;
- a surface inspector signs a closure order that strands a family below;
- a resident maintains a small shrine in a room that may become unstable next year.

The dungeon is frightening because it is inhabited, contested, and useful. The final city is possible only if the story has made ordinary life inside the Lattice worth preserving.
