# Chapter 35 — When the Wedges Run Out

The head went down a finger at the fifth bell on a Tuesday, and nobody was hurt, and it was still the end of something.

Oren was under it, because Oren was under it every working hour and had been for a fortnight, with his shoulder against the stone and the hand line over the back of his neck and a slate wedged in the gap so he could see the gap. He felt it come and he said, "Down," and Tams was out of the way and Jo was out of the way and the wedge that was nearest took it in the face and split into two pieces along the grain.

"That is one wedge dead," Tams said, kneeling. "Not lost. Dead. It was packing the head and the head moved and now it is two sticks, and a stick is not a wedge and does not argue about it."

"Seven sound," Jo got down and counted them herself. "Seven sound and two sticks, and the two sticks are the one."

"Six of the seven are spoken for," Tams said. "Three in the waist, three at the head, and one in the basket, because the lamp stand ate the other one down a stair throat a fortnight ago. And the three in the waist are in fill that is gone, and I am telling you as a man who sets them that the fill under the waist is washed out to about a hand, and a wedge in a hand of washed-out fill is a wedge holding nothing. They want doing again. Not because they have moved. Because what they are holding is not there any more."

He put the tally board on the anteroom bench and turned it round, because he had started doing that, and because people in this room would argue with a person and would not argue with a number facing them.

"Seven sound and two sticks, and here is where the seven are:" Three at the school-door head, and a fourth slot at the head holding nothing but splinters. Three at the corridor's waist, in fill that is gone. One in the basket, and one in the basket is what you reach for when something happens, and I want that in the record because I have been carrying one in my head for a fortnight and saying two. Nothing else. No sound timber, no lath, no bronze, no wedges spare. And a list of things that want wedges, which is not a list of things that will get them: the school-door head, four, and not negotiable, because the head carries a cracked lintel over a two-foot-eight gap and the only reason it has not come down onto the door is that pieces of hardwood are in the way. The corridor's waist, three, and not optional either, because the boards on the kitchen side are lying on fill that has washed out from under them twice now. The clinic sill, two, because the clinic return door opens inward and the sill is where a bed goes over. The kitchen brace, two, which has been deferred since the twenty-fourth day.

"Seven sound and two sticks and six spoken for," Marek said, "leaves one in the basket and nothing for anything that has not already broken. And the clinic sill is not a comfort. The clinic return is where Isel slept and where there is a bed and where Mikka takes a basin, and the sill is the lip the basin goes over. It is not broken."

"It is low," Mikka said. "It is low and it is not broken and I have been carrying over it since the twenty-sixth day, and I am telling you that it is the reason I cannot stop the kitchen brace, and I am about to tell you why I am going to stop the kitchen brace."

Sefu took the chair at the wheel's edge, which he did when something was going to be decided rather than measured.

"Go on," he said. "And say it in the order of the people it belongs to, and do not let me merge them, because the moment I merge them it becomes one decision and it is five."

Mikka stood at the kitchen line with her hands in her sleeves.

"The kitchen brace," she said. "I am stopping it. Not deferring it. I am stopping it and I want it written as *stopped*, and I want my name on the stopping, and I want the reason to be the reason and not a number."

She waited, and Dalla put the chalk on the table in front of her, and Mikka kept her hands in her sleeves.

"Six weeks ago the wall behind my flue was straight and I could lean a pot against it. In the fourth week of this it began to come out of true — not much, a finger over the height of a man, but I noticed it because I lean things against that wall four times a day." She came back from the kitchen line and put her thumb on the anteroom's own wall, high up, where there was a hairline crack with a smear of old soot above it. "That is the same crack and it has grown a hand's width since the twenty-fourth day. The brace was the stock's first job. The stock went under a school floor. And now here is the reason I am stopping it, and the reason is not a number." She looked up. "If I brace that wall I am holding a flue up with hardwood that is not there, and if the wall goes, the flue goes, and the flue is the only heat in this building that is not a person carrying a pot. So I am choosing the wall over the brace, and I am choosing it deliberately, and I am going to watch it, and it is mine to watch because it is my kitchen."

"Then the kitchen brace is not on the list," Dalla said.

"The kitchen brace is not on the list," Mikka said. "It is off. If I ever put it on the list, I have taken leave of my senses."

"And I am stopping mine," Ilsa said, and everybody turned round, because Ilsa Norr had volunteered for the hardest cost in this building six days ago and had been proud of it in front of witnesses.

"School," she said. "On the three days in seven when the corridor is not open, the school corner is not a waiting room. It is a school corner with a school in it. My school runs five days. It has run four since the twenty-seventh day and it has not run at all since the twenty-eighth, and I have been getting the morning done by taking the first hour and moving nine children through a kitchen line with a pot boiling, and I said in this room that I would take the waiting room and that it was the school paying and my decision. I meant it when I said it." She put both hands on the edge of the table. "I am taking it back, and I am not taking back the principle, and I am writing down the difference. The corner had a wall and a store and a floor with a drain in it I have just had cut, and it had a week. It does not have a week. It has a wedge count, and the wedge count is the corridor's, and the corridor is where a household goes when the clinic return is full, and if I take the three wedges for my waiting room then a family with a fever stands in a doorway. So: no waiting room. The school corner is a school on the days the school runs, and on the days the corridor is shut the school does not run, and I have told the nine this morning and I am telling you now and I would like it written that I decided it and did not have it decided for me."

"That is three days a week," Sefu said.

"That is three days a week," Ilsa said. "There are no children in this building who do not already lose days. They lose them to the stair. Now they lose them to a wedge."

Nobody softened it. It took the better part of an hour to get Ilsa's line into the ledger in her own words with her name on it, and Jo wrote part of it in her own hand because it was about a room people use.

And Renn would not stop hers.

"I am not stopping the door and the landing," she said, before she was asked. "You are all very sensibly stopping the things that are cracked. The door and the landing are not cracked. The door and the landing are the boundary, and I will tell you what happens at a boundary: the day a boundary lets go of a door, it does not stop being a boundary. It stops being *this* boundary, and it becomes four people with a rope and an opinion, and the people on the other side of it find out later. So the door and the landing stand, and they are watched, and it is not a repair and it is not on anyone's list, because a watched thing is not a repair. It is a person."

"Renn," Marek said. "If the corridor closes three days a week and the school does not run three days a week, then on those days the anteroom's ten is the whole of the sheltered space in this boundary, and it is a room you have already told us is a workshop with a roof. And Leena is in it. And the anteroom is a room you are not permitted to call yours."

"Then put the crew in it," Renn said.

And that was when Marek did the thing he had been building toward since the twenty-seventh day, and it was the same thing, and this time it was worse.

"The crew stands the sixth watch," he said. "On Renn's three days. At the waist, in the wet, on the ledger, in the rota, with a name and an hour, the same as everybody else. Then the corridor opens five days in seven instead of four, and the school corner's waiting room is a school corner, and the clinic sill and the kitchen brace are not on the list, and nothing is stopped except my six hands and my six bodies and whatever it costs us, and I have been told that the crew's labor is the one cost in this building that is not paid to anybody."

It was the right instinct. He could feel that it was the right instinct, the way you can feel a bearing is taking load before you have any idea what it is taking it of, and he had four hours of it before anybody answered, and he spent the first hour hopeful.

Sefu answered first and he answered the way he answered.

"No," he said. "A watch is not a labour. A watch is a person who is answerable for a condition while it is happening, and it is answerable to the people who live beside the condition, and to nobody else. I have said in this room that there is nobody in this neighbourhood with the standing to bind a stranger to a maintenance obligation, and I said it six weeks ago, and I am not going to be relieved of that position by an offer."

"You are not binding us," Marek said. "We are volunteering."

"You are offering, and I am refusing, and those are different words, and the difference is the whole subject of this room." Sefu did not raise his voice. "If a watch can be stood by a person who is not of the boundary, then the watch is not a watch. It is a rota with strangers in it. And in four months there will be a stair to work on, and a stair wants hands at a throat, and somebody will say the crew are already on the rota, and the crew will already be on the rota."

"Then the rota is the problem," Marek said. "I am not arguing the rota. I am arguing the three days."

"No, you are arguing the three days," Renn said. "I am the one who has the three days. Listen to what you have done. You have found the thing I would have to stand in, and you have offered to stand in it, and every part of that offer is about you. I do not need a stranger in the wet. I need a floor that does not fill, and a door that shuts, and a pump with a seat, and a stair I am not asked to think about. You cannot take my three days off me by being braver than me. It is not mine to give away and I would rather it stayed mine."

Jo had not said anything. When she did, it was to write rather than to argue, which was her habit, and she held the board.

"Put it on the board," she said. "In the open. So the six of us can read what Marek proposed and why it was refused." She wrote it and turned it round. "Proposal: the crew stands the sixth wet-seam watch on the days Renn does not, to keep the corridor open five days in seven. Refused: Sefu on standing; Renn on owning; and refused by me because a rota with six strangers in it is the same instrument as a charter, and we have spent six weeks refusing a charter."

"I did not want a charter," Marek said. "I wanted the corridor open."

"I know exactly what you wanted," Jo said, "and you have been wrong about it in the same way three times now, and every time you have wanted to be the person who pays so that nobody else has to be the person who decides. That is not humility. It is the same control with a coat on."

The anteroom was very quiet. Marek felt the sentence go into him and he did not argue it, because she was right, and because the three times were the fund, and the four-foot remnant, and this.

"Second thing," he said. "Then close the corridor. For good. Stop packing the waist. The boards on the kitchen side are lying on fill that is gone; take them up, take the corridor out of use entirely, and put the three wedges somewhere they can be found. We go back to the anteroom's ten and the school door as a relief and no more. That costs nothing and it costs one decision, and the decision is that a room that is safe only while a machine runs is not a room."

"No," Mikka said.

"You said the corridor is the only place a household goes—"

"I said the anteroom is a workshop with a roof, and I said it before the corridor was built, and I have been in it six weeks and I have watched nine people and a litter use a floor for two intervals a day, and a woman with a fever has nowhere else in this boundary to stand." She put her hand on the kitchen line. "Three wedges. I will tell you what three wedges buy. It buys a floor for a family with a child who is burning up, while the clinic return is doing what the clinic return does. That is the whole of it and it is not a comfort, and it is the only thing in this building that is not a person being brave. Take it out and you have got a workshop with a roof, eleven days, and no room at all."

Pell had the tally board propped on his knee from the bench, because a number facing him is a number he can read out loud to a room he cannot walk into, and he read the head line and the waist line and the basket line back to the room in order, and nobody corrected him, and the last line he read was the clinic sill, and he said, "That is the one nobody is going to fund," and Dalla said, "That is the one nobody is going to fund," and wrote it down.

So they did the arithmetic that Tams had already done, twice, out loud, because the room wanted it done out loud, and it took the better part of an hour with a lamp and the tally board and the slate, and it came out where it had come out at the start.

Three wedges at the school-door head, and the two sticks out of it and into the bin, and the head wanted four. Three at the waist. One in the basket. Seven sound wedges accounted for and nothing spare, and a head one wedge short of what it needs and no fourth to be found, and no sound timber, and no lath, and no bronze, and nothing spare for the clinic sill, and nothing at all for a kitchen wall with a crack in it that a cook has chosen to watch.

"The head wanted four," Tams said, "and it wanted them in the morning, and it is going to want them on Thursday. I want that on the page in the same line as the school, because in three weeks somebody is going to come in here and say we chose the school over the door, and we did not, because the door was not offered a fourth."

Dalla wrote the decision in the order of the people who owned each part of it, and read it back before she wrote the next, and Jo corrected two lines and Leena corrected a third, and the page that came out of it said, in five hands, that the anteroom and the service bypass continued conditionally; that the lower corridor might be used for the interval of an attended pump with six watches filled, the lever announced and the kitchen line the way out; that on the three days in seven when the sixth watch was not held, the corridor was not used, and the school did not run, and both of those were costs at the school; that the school-door head was held by two shoring lengths, three wedges and a hand line, was one wedge short of what it wanted, and could not be rebuilt; that the kitchen brace was stopped and not deferred, and the clinic sill was unfunded, and the corridor's waist was packed in fill that had washed out, and all four of those facts were the price of seven sound wedges and two dead sticks in a building with no sound timber over six feet.

And then Dalla read the last line, which nobody had drafted and which she had written in her own hand, which was the true part of every page she made.

"Add this," she said. "In the crew's column. Six hours of Marek Venn's time on the twenty-eighth day, spent on a proposal to stand the crew's own bodies in a watch post the six of them are not entitled to stand in, offered on the grounds that their labor is free, and refused. Recorded because a refused proposal that cost somebody an afternoon is a cost, and the cost does not stop being a cost because the proposal was decent."

"Write it," Marek said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect, and Dalla wrote it, and Ilsa ruled three columns through on the rota with a single stroke of chalk that she did not apologise for, and the school corner went back to being a school corner and a school, and the school ran on Thursday and not on Friday, and Mikka's flue wall sat behind her pot with a crack a hand's width wide that she would look at every morning for the rest of however long it was going to stand there, and in the anteroom the anteroom's ten came back at the fourth bell, and the corridor closed for the nineteenth day running, and eight wedges went into three places and a basket, and there was nothing left over for anything.
