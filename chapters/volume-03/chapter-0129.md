# Chapter 129 — The Low End Of The Corridor

**Shut for the corridor on the fourth day of the twenty-fifth week, and the water-carrying went at the second bell in Renn Vale's seventh term, six pails a day every day in seven in two crossings of four and then two, and the second crossing of that morning failed before anybody had put a hand on the first pail, and a man of twenty-two was not at the fourth step, and he was found at the low end of the corridor at the fourth bell by the woman who has been going into rooms before anybody else for twenty-five weeks, and nothing about it was a rescue and everything about it was a building.**

Jo Verrick had the room at the kitchen-line end in her hand and was in the corridor with a lamp and her rule, at the school door, and she said the sentence in the order the room does things in and she said it flat.

"**Found. At the low end, in the channel, under where the grate is. Located, tested, recorded, and not before, and I am going to say where I am standing which is on this side of him and not on that one, and Dalla is going to say the time, and neither of us is going to go and get anybody yet because Mero Senn is in this building and he is coming.**"

Mero Senn came down the nine steps at a walk, which is faster than anybody expects, and he got down onto the flags and stayed there on one knee for a long time, and he did not ask anybody anything for about two minutes, which Dalla Sen entered.

"**Say the time,**" he said, at last.

"**Between the last of the fifth bell yesterday and the second this morning,**" said Dalla Sen. "**That is what I have and I am not going to put an hour on it, because the pump was not run and the clock in this building is the pump, and I am not going to invent a clock for the one night in twenty-five weeks that a person was in it.**

"**And nobody sent him.**"

She said it out loud, standing in a corridor, and then she said it again in her own words, which is what she does, and the second one is the one that is in the book.

"**Nobody in this building sent Wen Pask to the low end of this corridor. There is no rota. He is not a watch. He is not owed a watch. He has a wage and a bed and hours entered as work at the cook's rate, and he is paid, and being paid is not a post, and I have entered that three times this month for other people.

"**And I did not send him and I did not ask him and I did not know he had gone, and I am the clerk of this building and it is my job to know what every person in it is doing, and I did not know, because there is no page in this book that says where he is at any hour of any day, and I have been clerk here for twenty-five weeks and I did not think of that as a hole until this morning.**"

---

**The grate was off and it had been levered, and the lever was the width of a dead stick, and this building has had two dead sticks for twenty-five weeks and now has one.**

Jo Verrick had it on the flags by the sixth bell, and it was a dressed stone slab about the size of two hands put together, and it had been lifted out of a frame at the low point of the dip and set against the wall.

"**Two of us could not have done it in a hurry and one of us could have done it in a quiet hour,**" she said. "**There are marks on the frame on two sides and they are about the width of a felled bough and they are not pry marks, they are a lever and a hand. And it is a wet place and the edge is worn, and a thing that is lifted out once in nine hundred years has loose dirt in the frame, and there is loose dirt in the frame, and I would not swear to you how long it has been out but I would swear it was not out yesterday at the second bell, because I stood on it yesterday.**

"**And under it the channel goes down about a foot and a half and then it goes away toward the shaft, and the shaft is the one with the slab over it that is packed and not held, and nobody in this building has ever been at the bottom of it and the far wall has never been seen.**

"**He went into a foot and a half of channel on the edge of eleven feet, in the dark, without a lamp, to look at something. I want that said and I want it said by me before anybody else says it, because I am the one who has said *nobody is ever going to stand at the low end of it* out loud in this building, and I said it, and I was right about the six minutes and I was wrong about everything else.**"

"**What was he looking at,**" said Mero Senn, and Dalla Sen said, "**I am going to answer that, and I am going to read it off a page, and I would like the room to hear the page first and me afterwards.**"

She read the two sentences off the review page in the clerk's hand, which has been on that page for twenty-five weeks with no name against it and no day and no watch.

"**Somebody stands at the low end of the corridor and watches the water in the six minutes after the lever comes off, and there is nobody standing there.**

"**And the woman who wrote those sentences has three conditions and the third of them is that nobody is to be sent anywhere to make it true, and the strikings-out of her covers out of every instrument in this building are in nine seconds in a clerk's hand and in an engineer's and nobody has ever asked her why, and I am not going to ask her, and she is not in this room, and I am not going to use her to explain a dead man.**"

---

**Mero Senn said the thing that he is, in a room, and it was not comfort, and he would not be moved off it, and he was wrong about one part of it and said so before anybody found out.**

"**I will not tell you how long he was in it. There is a difference and I am going to say it: I will not tell you how long because nobody in this room knows, and a man who guesses in front of nine people about a dead man of twenty-two is going to be quoted, and I have been quoted myself.

"**I will tell you what is in front of me. He went into a channel about a foot and a half deep at the low end of a corridor two foot eight wide, on the edge of a shaft nobody has been at the bottom of, in the dark, and there is water in that channel in the six minutes after the lever comes off and there was no water in it at all, because the pump had been off since the fifth bell yesterday.

"**So he was not standing in water. There was no water. He went down into a dry hole in the dark to be in the place where the water is, which is the most ordinary thing in the world to do and is the whole of what he did.

"**And the grate was off. And I am going to say the two halves and I am not going to be pressed on which of them is the cause, because a man who is dead is not going to be helped by a man of my trade picking one.

"**The grate was off. That is the reason. That is not the cause. A grate is a lid on a hole and a lid is not a trap and nobody has ever said it was one, and if this building wants to learn a thing out of this morning then the thing to learn is that a grate in a frame is not a lid, it is a habit, and a habit is a thing that can be taken off by a person who is not a person you are looking for.

"**And the other half, and I am the only person in this room who can say it and I am saying it once: he went down. Nobody sent him and nobody told him to and nobody could have, and there was no rota and no watch and no page that says where he is, and the reason he went is that he is twenty-two years old and he has been in this building for more than two months and he is the only person in it who nobody has ever asked for anything.

"**And I have been doing this for twenty-two years and I have seen a man go into a hole for exactly that reason seven times and I have never once written down what I call it because there is no word for it in a surgeon's book. It is not stupidity and it is not bravery. It is a hole in a building where nobody is.**"

"**Then put that in your book,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**I have put it in hers,**" said Mero Senn, "**in six sentences in a clerk's hand, and I am not going to say it in a second book on a second day where it is easier to be generous about it.**"

---

**They found it in the dry part of the channel, an hour later, under a flag, weighted with a dead stick, and neither Jo Verrick nor the cook had any idea what it was until Bel Ordry turned it over.**

It was two sheets of a cheap paper, folded twice, and it had been folded hard and pressed with a thumb, and the weight on it was one of this building's two dead sticks, which is a thing nobody has counted out loud in twenty-five weeks and which Dalla Sen had entered in the material ledger as *seven sound and two dead sticks* on a page nobody has read.

"**Say the number of dead sticks,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and say it in a room, because I have watched a clerk in this building take a figure off a wall in nine seconds and a man in my trade get himself killed by a number somebody had not checked.**

"**Seven sound and two dead sticks,**" said Dalla Sen, and stopped with the chalk up, and then said the other thing. "**It is the second of the two. And the other one is in the basket at the waist and it has been in the basket since the fourteenth week, and I have never once looked in the basket, and I am a clerk in this building for twenty-five weeks and I have never looked in the basket.**

"**Entered: one dead stick out of two, used as a weight on a sheet of paper in the channel at the low end of the corridor, and the clerk did not notice it was gone, and the clerk's ledger says two.**"

"**Is there anything else of ours in it,**" said Iven Tallo, from the doorway, and he had not come further than that.

"**No,**" said Bel Ordry, having looked. "**Paper, and a fold, and a thumb, and a stick.**"

Marek Venn had said nothing from the second bell to the fourth. He had the anteroom step and he had his left hand open on his knee and it was not closing, and he had been the only man in this room for twenty-five weeks who could see a series coming, and he had said so in the twentieth week over a wedge in a school door, and the series had gone on for five more weeks and had arrived at a boy with a tally who had offered himself to a sill on the Monday and had been told to go to bed.

"**I was right,**" he said at last, to the wall and not to the room. "**On the water, and on the sill, and on the corridor, and on the low end. I said it in a room on the Wednesday and I was right about all of it, and none of it has anything to do with a grate.**

"**And I want the room to hear one more thing out of me and then I am going to stop talking for the rest of the week. I have spent five weeks being the man in this boundary who can see what is coming, and it is the only thing I have ever been good at, and I would like it entered that I think it cost that boy his life, and I would like it entered that I have got nothing to offer about it.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**in his words, and I am not going to improve it. And I am going to enter the second half of it in the clerk's hand as well, once, and then I am not going to say it again this week: a man being right about five walls is not the same fact as a man being right about a floor, and this room has known that since the first week of the twentieth week and has not said it out loud until this morning, and it took a man of twenty-two and two flags and a length of spent line to make a clerk say it.**"

**Not one person in the anteroom read it, and not one person in the anteroom knew what to do with a sheet of paper found under a flag weighted with a thing out of this building's own wall. Dalla Sen carried it up nine steps in a clerk's hand and set it on the anteroom table face up, where anybody in this building could read it, and said nothing about why she had done it, and did not have to.**

---

**At the sixth bell the clerk read the day's number and the count in the column with nothing over the top of it, and she read both of them the way she reads everything, which is twice, and the room did not fill in after the last one, and nobody asked her a question about either number.**

"**Three today and a hundred and four since the keeper signed, and the boundary is at two hundred and twenty-one and a quarter, and it adds by the day the work was done, and the two columns reconcile and they have reconciled for twelve weeks.**

"**The crew are at fifty-six and a half and have not moved since Thursday for the reason in my hand.**

"**And a man of twenty-two is sixty-four days and sixty-four nights into a count that is his own.**"

"**Say it again,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**A man of twenty-two is sixty-four days and sixty-four nights into a count that is his own.**

"**Say it without the man,**" said Bel Ordry.

Dalla Sen put the chalk down on the table instead of in her hand, which she has done three times in twenty-five weeks, and said:

"**Wen Pask is sixty-four days and sixty-four nights into a count that is his own. And I am going to say the rest of it out loud in a room, because I am the clerk and I keep it and I have kept it every day since the fifth day of the seventeenth week and it is in a column with no heading and it is in nothing else, and a man who is dead is still a man in a column and I am not going to be the clerk who stops writing a man's number on the day he is found.

"**So I am going to write it tomorrow, in this hand, at the sixth bell, and the day after that, and I am not going to decide tonight what I do when the writing stops, and I am not going to be asked tonight, and I am entering that I have not been asked and that I noticed on Monday that I had not been asked and did not do anything about it.**"

The room let it stand. Mero Senn went down the nine steps and along the corridor and did not cross the waist, and stopped at the low end for a moment with his hand on the wall, and then went back up and put his bag down in the worst room in the building.

**On the anteroom table, face up, in a clerk's hand, in a room where the anteroom was full and the word *door* had been said and repeated at the sixth bell, there were two sheets of a cheap paper folded twice, and eleven people had not read them, and the one that weighed a dead stick was the one somebody would read first.**
