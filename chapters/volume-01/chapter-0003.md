# Chapter 3 — The Cartwright’s Claim

By morning, the water had risen another finger’s width against the base of the lean-to.

The cracked lamp gave off a weak yellow circle. Beyond it, the chamber’s walls faded into gray, and the sealed Cartwright door stood upright in a recess that had probably been a service bay. Its metal wheel was green with age, and a strip of blue sealing cloth had been tied through the wheel’s spokes.

Marek had slept for less than an hour. He woke with Leena’s sling against his shoulder and the taste of dust in his mouth.

“Six liters,” Tams said before Marek asked. “I counted them twice. Leena had a mouthful, Pell had a mouthful, and the rest is still sealed.”

“Write the time beside it.”

“We did. Pell wrote the numbers.”

“I can’t read numbers in the dark,” Pell said.

“That is not a reason to stop writing them.”

Iven crouched beside the sealed door. He had scraped away a little dust with the edge of his badge, exposing a second line beneath the blue cloth. The line was a date, followed by a number that might have been an occupancy and a mark that might have been a weather code.

Marek knelt beside him, keeping his injured right hand tucked against his chest. The room had settled during the night, but not evenly. The floor seam near the door had widened by a hair. The air above it smelled faintly of old oil.

“What does the seal mean?” Leena asked.

“That a qualified Cartwright surveyor inspected this route on a particular date, for a particular number of people, under the conditions written on the form.” Iven traced the worn mark without touching it. “It does not mean the route is good forever. It does not mean the room behind it is good forever. It does not even mean the door will open for us.”

“But it is your mark,” Pell said.

“It is the guild’s mark.”

“You’re wearing the guild.”

Iven looked down at his badge. “That used to make a difference.”

The boy’s anger was clean and unhelpful. Marek could see him wanting to put his shoulder into the door, to turn a piece of metal into an answer. It was the same impulse that made men load a bridge until the bridge told them what they wanted to hear.

“Open it,” Pell said. “If there is an incline, we can send word to the surface.”

“There may be an incline,” Iven said. “The door is set into a transit cut. The outer wheel could be on a licensed line, or it could be buried under a fall. I don’t know which.”

Marek looked at the ventilation collar above the lean-to. “The door shares a vent with the room.”

Iven followed his gaze. “The old plan has one air shaft behind it.”

“Then opening it may pull the draft through the shelter.”

“It may also give us a way to send for help.”

“Help arriving through a door that changes the air path is not help. It is another condition.”

Leena shifted on the litter. “You want to leave it closed.”

“I want to know what it does before I put six people beside it.”

“You keep saying that as if it will make the door less owned.”

“Because it is owned,” Iven said. “The mark is a claim, a permission, and a warning. It tells the guild that a licensed line was surveyed here. It does not tell us who pays for the people who use it now.”

He reached beneath the shelf and produced a flat, oil-dark board. The guild had left a route card behind it, fixed to the wall with a wooden peg. Marek took it with his left hand. The card listed an inspection incline, a hand-operated outer wheel, and a condition that the route be kept at no more than one person at a time when the upper catch was engaged. A note in the margin said that the line was for inspection, not public passage.

There was a physical connection. The incline beyond the door might climb toward the surface, and the outer wheel might still be reachable from a licensed gate. No speaking tube or hidden signal ran through the wall. It was a route that someone had built, maintained, and then marked with a date.

Marek took the clipboard from his belt and wrote on its blank back:

DO NOT USE GUILD DOOR. SHARED VENT WITH OCCUPIED SHELTER. OCCUPANCY SIX. NO CURRENT CLEARANCE. AIR AND ESCAPE UNTESTED.

He showed it to Iven before fastening the board beside the door.

Iven read it twice. “You’re putting your refusal where anyone can see it.”

“I want the reason attached to the refusal.”

“In the guild, a reason is usually the first thing someone argues with.”

“Then they can argue with the wall.”

Pell made a disgusted sound. “The wall doesn’t answer.”

“That’s why it’s currently the safest person here.”

Iven took the route card and pointed to the outer-wheel notation. “If the surface gate is still under guild control, a courier could come down. The incline is narrow and the upper catch may be seized, but it reaches the inspection line. The Cartwrights have used it for years.”

“Could they send help?”

“They could send a person with a key, a claim, and instructions.”

“That is not the same as help.”

“No,” Iven said. “It is the same thing in a better coat.”

Marek turned toward the shelter. Iven was right to be wary, but the group could not stay in the first chamber forever. Water was moving uphill, the floor was settling, and the only air they had came through a broken shaft. They needed a route, even a route that came with a locked door.

He looked at the blue seal again. “We can make the door mechanically known without using it. Iven, show me how the catch works. Pell, stay with Leena. Oren, keep the water line in sight. Tams, listen for movement above.”

He had expected an argument. Instead, Iven simply nodded.

They approached the door from the side, away from the shelter’s vent. Iven showed him the lock bar, the upper catch, and the narrow seam where a person could pass if the wheel were turned. When he touched the bar, dust fell from the ceiling in a straight line.

Marek felt the pressure shift beneath his left palm again. This time it came with a brief taste of iron and a sound like a distant cart moving uphill. He pulled his hand away.

“What did you feel?” Leena asked from the litter.

“Movement behind the wall. Relative, not clear.”

“Can you tell if the door is safe?”

“No.”

“Can you tell if the room is safe?”

“No.”

Pell stared at him. “You keep saying no.”

Marek looked at the blue mark. “The alternative is saying yes because everyone wants a door.”

For half a day they watched the door and the water. They drank another liter between them, small enough that each swallow felt too precious to enjoy. Five liters remained from the collapse. Tams marked the level on the case board. Oren checked the lean-to uprights. Iven explained the clearance mark until Pell could repeat the difference between a survey date and a permission.

At last Iven found a narrow shaft of air moving beside the door. It came from below the threshold, not through the shared vent above. He held a loose thread near the gap. The thread drew inward, then flicked outward.

“There,” he said. “The door has a separate lower breathing space. The old route may still be usable if the upper catch is controlled.”

“Then it needs a test,” Marek said.

“And a licensed operator.”

“We have a licensed operator who does not want to sign a false record.”

Iven’s mouth tightened. “That is not a guild credential.”

“It is the only kind I trust this morning.”

The water moved again.

This time the thin line climbing the mineral seam reached the lower edge of the shelter floor. It did not splash or spread like ordinary seepage. It gathered, held, and rose against the slope in a smooth, shining curve.

Marek touched the wall with his knuckles. The pressure behind it was stronger than before.

The old door remained closed, legally marked and physically useless. The water had found another path.
