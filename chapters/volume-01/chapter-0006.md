# Chapter 6 — The Kitchen That Was Never a Vault

The room beyond the breach had been a kitchen before it had been anything else.

A low cooking hood occupied the far wall, its clay face blackened by old smoke. Beneath it, a stone worktop had been drilled with holes for bowls and kettles. A washline ran from one wall to another, its clips still hanging in a row. Shelves held a cracked cooking pot, a wooden scoop, and a stack of shallow plates gone gray with age. Above the sleeping platform, small marks climbed the plaster: not treasure symbols, but measurements made by someone measuring children.

Pell saw the marks before he saw the sleeping platform.

“Were they measuring the room?” he asked.

“Measuring who could use it,” Iven said. “A child’s reach. A shelf’s height. The number of people the kitchen expected to feed.”

Marek looked at the route card in his hands. The card’s copy was smudged, the lines uneven but readable. He had been thinking of the old place as a shelter because it had survived. The room told a different story. It had been maintained by people who needed somewhere to wash, cook, sleep, and leave again.

Under the worktop, a round cistern cover had been fitted with a hand-sized notch. Tams pried it up. A thin film of condensation shone inside, gathered from the stone above. The water was not connected to the flooded sump.

Marek crouched beside it. “No one drinks it yet.”

“Water is water,” Pell said.

“Water from a roof can carry soot, minerals, or the residue of whatever was stored above it. We test what we can.”

They waited until the droplets stopped falling into the cistern. Tams poured a small measure onto a dark piece of slate. Marek watched the film for grit and cloudiness. Leena, leaning over the litter, smelled the cup and touched a drop to the clean edge of her sleeve. The water left no oily ring, no sour smell, and after it evaporated left only a faint mineral trace.

“Clear enough to use carefully,” Marek said. “One liter verified. We add it to the ration and keep the cistern covered.”

The count changed on the case board. Five liters remained from the collapse, and the verified condensation liter was sealed separately as a reserve.

Tams looked pleased that the number had gone up. Leena looked at the cistern.

“Verified by what?” she asked.

“By what we could see, smell, and test here. Not by the room’s reputation.”

“Put that in the record too.”

Pell wrote it with the blunt end of the chalk, which made the letters uneven. Tams took over before he could erase the whole word.

The room held no food that anyone could safely eat. There was a sealed jar of something dark at the back of a shelf, but its lid had swollen and the smell inside was sharp enough to make Pell turn away. Iven found a bundle of old cloth and a wooden case beneath the worktop.

The case was the source of the room’s more interesting problem.

It was a maintenance case, its corners wrapped in split leather. A faded stencil on the lid showed three small circles joined by a line, with a fourth circle below them. The mark was not Cartwright blue. It was old Hearthhold work notation, worn almost smooth.

Iven ran a thumb over the stencil. “There may be a salvage tag under the handle.”

“Or there may not.”

“The guild pays for recovered material.”

“The room does not look abandoned because someone left.”

Pell looked toward the sleeping platform. “Can we sleep there?”

“Not until we know the platform will hold,” Marek said.

“It’s only a bed.”

“It is a floor over a room that has already lied to us once.”

Marek’s answer came out too sharply. Iven raised an eyebrow, but Leena touched the litter rail with her left hand.

“Let the boy have the platform after the bearing check,” she said. “A place to lie down is not the same as a place to trust.”

They tested the platform with the remaining gear and a stone from the fallen wall. It held. The bed was narrow, but it was dry, and its edge had been worn smooth by years of use. Pell sat on it with his scraped ankle propped on a folded piece of cloth. His face changed when the mattress gave beneath him.

“It remembers somebody,” he said.

“Rooms remember use,” Marek replied. “That does not mean they remember people.”

The distinction was not comforting, but it was honest.

Iven pried open the case. Inside were canvas scraps, a roll of waxed cord, a flat file, two spare braces, and a folded maintenance sheet. The sheet showed a route map with several independent paths. They converged not at one door, but at a maintenance shaft beyond the old room. A second line ran toward a service stair, and a third toward a pump gallery.

Marek could not copy the map with his right hand. The swelling had made the pencil slip. Iven held the sheet flat while Marek used his left. The line wandered more than the map deserved, and twice he had to stop because his fingers cramped.

Leena watched from the litter. “You used to make the lines in one pass.”

“My right hand used to.”

“Do not apologize for the hand. Apologize if you are going to pretend the map is better than it is.”

“I am not pretending. I am drawing what I can see.”

“That is better.”

Iven turned the sheet. “The map has a Hearthhold maintenance mark. If we take the case, the guild can call it salvage. If we leave it, we lose the panels.”

Marek looked at the case. The two broad side panels could make a shore and a backing board. The old room had given them a place to rest, a verified reserve of water, and a map. Taking the whole case would turn a borrowed record into a claim. Leaving all of it would turn caution into waste.

“Take two panels,” he said. “Leave the rest. Write down what we borrowed.”

Iven took a piece of paper from the case. “And what we return?”

“The case, the remaining tools, and anything we cannot carry.”

“And the map?”

“The map stays with us for the route. The case stays here until we know whether the room can bear our return.”

They worked the panels free. The old leather cracked, but the wood beneath was sound. Iven cut one panel into a narrow shore while Tams and Oren used the other as a backing board. Two short offcuts remained, sound enough for a lever or a chock. The case lost its shape. A faint stenciled circle remained on the inner lid, one of the old Hearthhold marks.

Marek wrote on a clean board fixed near the platform:

BORROWED: TWO PANELS FOR SHORING. NO CLAIM TO CASE OR CONTENTS. ROUTE MAP USED FOR RESCUE ONLY.

Leena read it from the litter. “Add that the room’s water is a reserve, not a right.”

Marek added the sentence.

The crew drank carefully from the five remaining collapse skins. The verified liter stayed sealed as a reserve. They did not touch the sump. The water in the lower recess was rising, and the temporary flood gate could not make it clean.

Oren and Tams took turns changing Leena’s sling and checking the litter. They did not wait for Marek to tell them. When Pell’s ankle began to throb, Tams found a scrap of old linen, dampened it with a little water, and laid it across the joint. The gesture was simple and practical. It made the room feel less like an abandoned kitchen and more like a place where people might have survived long enough to become careful with one another.

Marek rested his injured hand on the backing board. The fingers would not close fully. He tried to lift the brass rule with his left hand and found the motion steadier. The rule tapped once against the wood.

Not an answer. A warning, perhaps. Or an old habit mistaken for a message.

“The map points to a maintenance shaft,” he said. “And there is something behind the wall.”

Iven looked toward the seam. “Then we go before the water reaches the platform.”

Pell slid off the bed. “I can walk.”

“You can walk short distances,” Leena said. “That is not the same as being able to run.”

“I didn’t say anything about running.”

“You were preparing to.”

Marek looked at the copy of the map. The service shaft lay beyond the wall. A small dark plate was marked behind it, half hidden by a later course of stone. When he spoke the location aloud, dust slid from the seam in a thin vertical line.

The room seemed to lean toward the hidden mark.

Marek folded the map and stood.

“Take the panels, the verified water, and the litter,” he said. “Leave the rest. We follow the route people left behind.”
