# Chapter 59 — The Order Withdrawn

Talla Roke uncapped the ink with her teeth and put the paper on the bench flat, and the paper had been folded and unfolded eleven times in thirty-one days and the creases had gone soft, and it was the fourth of last month and it was the eleventh week and she had decided twice on the road and once in the school store and she was not going to decide again.

She did it at the fifth bell, on the bench end with her back to the wall, after the tally, which she had asked to be there for, and which Tams Quill had read, both columns, because a courier on a bench is a person in a room and this room did not do things by rank. She had listened to it the way she listened to a road report, which is to say entirely, had not understood one figure in it, and had understood all of it.

"Right," she said. "I have had this folded and unfolded eleven times and I have carried it thirty-one days and I have been in this building eight days, and I am going to do the thing I have been not-doing since the first morning, and I want the clerk to write it in my words and not in hers, and I want the time on it, and I want it read back before I have said anything else."

"Write it," said Dalla Sen.

"**I withdraw the order.** Not the middle council. Me. I have not got the council's permission to withdraw it and I have not asked for it, and I want it recorded that I am withdrawing an order that somebody else made, on a clerk's mark, which I believe they made in good faith and for a reason that is better than mine was.

"The reason is this. At the start of the week a carpenter in this room told me that the moment that slab carries a load in a Hearthhold work, it is material in use under a guild salvage lien, and every hand that touches it after that is a hand that can be asked in a hearing who authorised it. And he was not talking about me. He was talking about himself — he built the other shore, and he asked for that slab twice, and your clerk refused him three times, and I can see from where I am sitting that he is not a man who wants a stone. He wants to *pack a lip*. And the lip is the reason a man is dead.

"So: if I take it, then in nine months the second gate is holding water on a sill that came out of the room where a man died, and there is a mark at the bottom of a piece of paper with the words *Hearthhold boundary* on it, and a family in the middle is standing on a boundary's grief, and the boundary will be asked about it, and the boundary's answer will be a number of hours given by strangers.

"And that is not my argument. My argument is simpler and it is about me. I am a courier. A courier's whole trade is that a thing which is claimed by nobody can be carried by anybody. The moment I write my order into a boundary's book, then this boundary holds a claim — *ours, on the middle, for a sill* — and a boundary with a claim in it is a boundary that can be asked to give it up, and refused, and asked again. I would not be stealing their stone. **I would be putting a second lock on the same door**, and I have been on the wrong side of enough doors to know what that does."

She capped the ink.

"I am not going to write that the order was refused," she went on. "I want that on the page, and I want the distinction in your clerk's mouth, because I think it is the whole of what I have learned in eight days in a room that does not own anything.

"The parapet slab has been refused three times by this boundary's clerk and once refused to a man by name with his name under it, and every one of those was a **refusal**, and a refusal is the clerk's, and a refusal is a thing that stands there and can be appealed and is held up by a person who will say the same words next time.

"This is a **withdrawal**, and a withdrawal is mine, and I am the one who has to live in the middle and look at two brothers and a sister, and a withdrawal does not stand there. It can be made again by anybody with a mark. I have had thirty-one days to write a second order and I have not written it and I am not going to, and that is a decision of mine and it is the only thing I have got out of these eight days that is worth taking out of the room."

Dalla Sen wrote it, in Talla Roke's own words, with the time on it, and read it back, and read the distinction back again because the courier asked her to.

"A refusal is mine and a withdrawal is yours," Dalla said, "and I will record both and I will not improve either, and I would like it in the record in the clerk's own hand as well, which is this: I have kept this ledger for nine weeks and it is the first entry in it that somebody has come here and taken something off the table themselves, and I would like that in the book in my hand as well as in hers, because in a year somebody is going to want to know whether this boundary ever got anybody to change their mind, and the answer is yes, once, and it was not about a stone."

---

The middle's dry lock stayed dry.

Talla Roke said so herself, in the room, before anybody could be gentle with her, and she said it in the voice she used for distances.

"Four households in the second gate carrying water up four sets of steps from the day the cold closed, and no sill, and I have no second order and no money for timber and no carpenter, and I am not going to pretend that withdrawing it was a good thing for them. I have cost those four households a winter. I would like that said out loud by somebody who is not me, and I would like it written, and I am not going to be the one who writes it, because I will write it kindly."

"It is written," said Dalla Sen.

"Then I would like it said out loud, and I would like it said by a person of this building, because it is their book and I am only the one who put it in it."

"That is four households in the second gate carrying water up four sets of steps this winter," said Mikka Hale, from the frame, in the flat voice, with a cloth in her hand, "because the courier in this room gave up a piece of stone that was not hers, and the cost of that is four households and not one clerk's conscience. Write that in the same line. And write that a woman in my kitchen is going to be asked about it by somebody from the middle in about six weeks and I do not know what she is going to say."

"Write it," said Dalla Sen.

---

Then Talla Roke asked for something else, and she asked for it in front of everybody, which was not the way couriers usually did anything.

She asked Marek Venn for the crew's hours.

"I know the rule," she said. "I have been in this room eight days and I have heard it six times and your man with the board says it out loud at the fifth bell like a bell. Thirty-eight and a half, given, owed to nobody, and not to be spent on anything without a named work and a person who has asked. So: a named work, and a person asking, and a clerk to write it down.

"The work is the second gate's inlet. I do not have timber. I do have a courier's legs, a drawing, four households, and a man in this room who is a very good carpenter and is not going to be paid, and a maker of holes in a man from the surface who cannot hold a chisel but can hold a line, and a man from the surface who cannot use his right hand and can use a board better than anybody I have ever met.

"And I am not asking for it now. I am asking for it *named*, with a date, in your book, and I am asking for it in the spring, because in the spring you are going anyway, and because a Hearthhold that waits for strangers to help it is a Hearthhold that somebody else decides when to help it."

Nobody in the anteroom said anything, and Marek Venn looked at the slate where the leaving was written, and then at the tally board, and then at the bench where the middle's order had been sitting.

"There is a spring conversation," he said. "With this boundary. With the air engineer by name, and an hour on it, and the five of us do not go north until it has happened. That is the keeper's sentence and I wrote it and he made me write it again."

"Then there are two springs," Talla Roke said.

"There are two springs," Marek said, "and I am not going to promise one of them to a person I met on a Thursday, and I am also not going to pretend to you that I have not thought about it, because I have thought about it every day since the second gate's name came out of your mouth, and I have thought about it in exactly the way I am not supposed to think about anything, which is on my own, in my head, at about two in the morning, with nobody's name on it."

Sefu Aran said, from the wheel: "Say that last part in the room, and say it twice, and put the time on it, because a man who will admit to a two o'clock plan in a room is worth ten men who have not got one."

"Which of the two springs is the first one," said Talla.

"You are," said Sefu Aran, without any hesitation at all. "Not the crew's hours and not the work, and not this year. **You**, and a named hour, and the air engineer in the room, because the hand-over is the only part of the leaving that has to be done by a person of this boundary and the hand-over is air, and the person who has to be in the room is Nera Oduya, and Nera Oduya is not going to be impressed by a man who has been talking to a courier.

"So the first spring is a conversation between the keeper and the air engineer about a hand-over, and it happens whether or not anybody is leaving, and it would be good if it happened in a month when we all know which spring it is."

---

Corva Nye's report went up the next morning, on the middle passage, on foot, with a clerk's mark and a form and a margin.

She asked, on the morning she left, whether she might stay for the next crossing as an observer, and she asked it properly, in the anteroom, in front of Sefu Aran and in front of Renn Vale, and it was a courteous request and it was, underneath, the most dangerous thing anybody had asked in that room since six people came up a shaft with a plate they could not read.

"Understand me," she said. "I am not asking to be on the landing. I am not asking to touch a stone, and I would not let me. I am asking to sit at the top of nine steps with a slate and watch a clerk write a number, and then to write in my own hand that I saw it. That is the whole of what my office has ever wanted from a route, and I have never once been able to give it to them, and you have one."

"No," said Renn Vale.

Corva Nye did not argue. She looked at her for a moment, and then she said: "Say why, so that I write the right thing in the report, because I am going to write that a route keeper of a Hearthhold refused a Cartwright observer, and I would rather write the truth of it than a version of it that makes her sound unkind."

Renn Vale stood on the common landing with her hand on the wall above the stone and did not go any further down than that.

"Because you would be a second opinion standing on nine steps over a crossing where a woman with a mother and a room is doing the string, and because a person who is going to write a report is not the same as a person who is only there," she said. "And because I have watched four of them now — two of mine, one clerk, one of yours — come in here and be *right*, and be right in this room, and it has not once been a thing they wanted. Your route is not a road you come and look at. If you want to know whether we can keep it, then you come in the winter, and you come when it is cold, and you come and you are asked, and you may be asked not, and you carry water, and nobody writes a report."

"That is a condition and not a refusal," Corva Nye said.

"It is both, and it is both on purpose, and I would like it recorded as both, and I am not withdrawing either half."

---

And Iven Tallo put the guild's page on the table in his own two hands, on the Thursday, and nobody had asked him to, and he had the look of a man who has been waiting nine weeks to be the one who says a thing out loud.

"Right," he said. "In the thirty-eighth day I said that putting the lower side back into service is how a route charter arrives, and in the forty-ninth I said the route's page is a position and not a shield, and I said the wall will hold longer than people think and will cost somebody something that is not written down anywhere, and I said it on a day when it was cheap to say it, and I have been waiting to see which of those three sentences was true.

"Well. A Cartwright clerk has put a page about this stair into a guild office, in her own hand, in the margin of a form, and she is right, and it is the best answer she has ever been given, and I meant every word of it, and I am not going to pretend any more that it has not been written down.

"Here is what it is now. Before Monday, if a man from that office asked me who maintains the route, I could have said: nothing is written anywhere, and here is a building with a road in it and a bell on a rope, and you may go and look. That was a good answer. I was part of it.

"**Now there is a page.** A clerk has a page. And a page is a thing a hearing can be *about*, and the wall I told you about was the answer. It still is. The page is the beginning of that wall's life being numbered. And it is going to be a very good page, and it is going to be in a file, and the next clerk is going to be handed that file, and the question in the file will be *what has changed since*, and the answer will be *a woman refused a Cartwright observer, and a keeper put a name in the margin, and two shores and a bell* — and that answer, written in a guild hand, in a guild file, is a claim that this boundary has a route worth having.

"And that is the cost I said would arrive. It has arrived in a woman's own handwriting and she has not done anything wrong."

"Then what do we do with it," said Sefu Aran.

"Nothing," Iven said. "That is the answer and I have thought about it for two days and I have got nothing else. We do not write a second page. We do not put a word on the route's slate this month, because the slate is the route and the route is six terms and a woman's hand, and the day we add a seventh line to answer a clerk is the day the slate stops being a thing the boundary keeps and starts being a document the boundary defends. And the clerk's page is *hers*, and it is in a file, and we cannot get at it and we cannot touch it, and it is the best thing anybody has done for us in nine weeks and it is also the first nail.

"So: nothing. And a keeper who knows he is doing nothing on purpose."

---

And on the Friday, at the frame, with the check hissing thin and steady and the two cleanings on her estimate standing exactly where they had stood for eleven weeks, Jo Verrick said the thing that the whole of the next month is going to be about.

"I am going to say this once and then I am going to stop saying it and let other people say it, because I have said everything else out loud before I started it and I am not going to start now.

"The seat goes. Not this week. Before the cold. That is a fact and not an opinion, and the fact is: a scored bronze delivery seat with two cleanings on it or fewer, in a pump whose delivery I can no longer hear because the check has gone and is only making noise, in a boundary that has four households' worth of nothing spare, with a shallow sump that comes up at a hand's breadth every five minutes. Two cleanings is the estimate. If the estimate is right then the seat has to be cut out and something put in its place, and if I cut it out and put nothing in, then the pump stops delivering, and if the pump stops delivering then the water in that dip comes up, and if the water in that dip comes up then the corridor's low end is under it, and the corridor's low end is two foot eight wide and it stands on top of the shaft.

"There is no bronze in this building. There is no bronze in this Hearthhold that anybody has told me about. And a seat cut out of bronze and put back in hardwood is a seat with a third of the life, and I am telling you that now so that nobody in this room ever gets to say afterwards that Jo Verrick did not say it.

"So the price of a new seat is: a length of hardwood that nobody has, a man who can cut it, and a man who can hold it, and I have got one of those three and I have not got the other two, and the one I have not got is not money. It is hands."

"Somebody said their hand," said Tams Quill.

"Somebody said their hand," Jo agreed, "and I am not going to use it, and here is why, and it is not kindness.

"If a man with two crushed fingers cuts a seat in a pump housing for eleven weeks, then in a year there is a pump with a seat in it that a man with a bad hand made, and the next person who opens that housing will find the work and think it is fine, and it is fine, and in four years it is not, because the man who made it could not feel what he was doing at the moment it mattered most. **A seat you cannot feel is a seat you cannot tell has gone wrong.** And the person standing at that frame on the day it has gone wrong is me, and I will not have a thing in my housing that I cannot read through the hand that made it.

"So: hardwood, and a whole hand, and not Tams Quill's, and not Marek Venn's. And that means somebody in this boundary has to find me hardwood, or somebody has to cut it, and I am not asking for it this month and I am not asking for it in front of a child."

---

Tams Quill put the chalk down and looked at his own right hand for a moment, and then went back to the eleventh item on the list — the one he had written on the Thursday and read out on the Friday — and put two words under it without being asked, and read them out, because that is what the list is for.

"**The bronze delivery seat, to be cut out and something put in its place, and there is no bronze, and the something is hardwood, and hardwood is a third of the life, and the man to cut it is not this man. And a man who cannot feel what he is doing cannot tell that it has gone wrong.** Read back, and put it under the material and not in a column, and put my name against it because it is my hand that is not going to that housing and a person should be able to find out why."

"Written," Dalla Sen said, "with a time, in your own words, and read back before the ledger closes."

And the wheel went round twice under the floor, and the check hissed, and the anteroom was holding ten including one litter, and a courier with a withdrawn order slept on the bench end with her back to the wall, and a clerk from an office forty days away went out up nine steps and down the middle passage with a page in a file and a border on it that she had written herself, and neither of those two things was a crime, and both of them were the beginning of something nobody in that room could yet name.
