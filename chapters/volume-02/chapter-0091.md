# Chapter 91 — The Same Pail

The first day of the eighteenth week was shut for the corridor, which was a Monday, and by the second bell six people were standing round a pail in the kitchen looking at the inside of it.

Twelve had become eleven. Mikka Hale read it at the fifth bell on the Sunday and wrote it on the Sunday and read it out on the Monday, and she read it out first, before anything else, because she had decided on the Sunday evening that a cook's number that has moved in a room with nobody in it belongs to the first room anybody is in.

"It went on the Sunday," she said, "and I was here and the water was not, and I am going to say the whole of it now and then I am going to stop saying it for the rest of the week, because I have said it three times already this month and it does not get less true and it also does not get more bearable."

On the scrubbed board there was a borrowed pail out of the middle gate's store, on its side, with a crust inside it about the thickness of a thumb in one place: white, and it came off in flakes, and it left a grit under your thumb-nail.

Nobody drank from it. That is the first fact and it is the one that matters and the room did it without being told, because a pail on its side in a kitchen with the crust in it is not a thing anybody picks up.

---

Wen Pask had counted pails for nine days and had a method, and the method was the whole of what happened.

"I will say how I count, and then anybody can tell me I am wrong," he said. "I count the ones that go up and the ones that come down, and I check my own totals twice, and I have been doing it since the middle of last week and I have got a thing I did not like.

"**The ones that come down are not the ones that go up.** They look the same. I have held both of them. But the one that stands on the ledge at the bottom of the nine steps has a crust in it under the water line, and the other three come down clean, and the other three are washed every time they come down and that one is not, **because that one belongs to this boundary and not to the office.**

"And it is not a bad pail, and I have been carrying it about for nine days with my thumb on the crust telling myself I had got a bad pail. I have got a bad pail and it is the best thing anybody has found in this Hearthhold in a month, because **you cannot get lime out of a pail unless it came through stone**, and the middle gate is a road and a counter and four days of passage and there is no stone in any of it."

Renn Vale said, from the landing, "Say the rest of it."

"**The water is coming in somewhere inside this building.**"

For about two seconds the room did what it always does, which is nothing at all, and then everybody started at once.

---

Objections came in the order this room makes them, and Dalla Sen wrote down every one of them, because that is now a thing that happens.

Jo Verrick: "He is a boy of twenty-two who has been here twenty-six days and he has found a piece of lime on a pail, and I have run a pump on mineral water for fifteen weeks and I have never once tasted it, and neither has anybody, and the reason I have never tasted it is **it is not for drinking and it never was.** He is not wrong. He is not a fool either. He is a man who has been handed the only work anybody here has given him and he has done it properly and found something with it, and that is not the same as a man who has found water."

Nera Oduya, from three steps: "**I will not certify it and I am not going to let anybody use the word.** No drinking water enters this Hearthhold until it has been found, and tested, and recorded on a page, in that order, and the page has to be read out and somebody has to be able to object to a line on it. That is not me being difficult. That is the only reason anybody in this building is alive."

Mikka Hale, with her hand flat on the table: "Twenty-six days. **He counted pails for nine days and did not tell me for six of them.** I am not angry and I want it entered that I am not angry, because the day I am angry is the day he stops telling me things. He should have told me on the fourth day. He will tell me on the fourth day next time and not on the ninth."

Wen Pask said, "Yes."

"Entered in my own hand and read out," said Mikka Hale, "and he is not to be sent anywhere and he is not to be given a lamp and he is not to be thanked in a room, because the thank you is the thing that makes a boy of twenty-two do it again and think about it for nine days."

---

Then the clerk asked the question that the room had not got to, which is the one a room always gets to last.

"**What is it that is a room?**

"Nobody has said. I have a bucket of pails and a crust in one of them and that is not a room. I need a wall with a joint in it, and I need somebody to be able to say whose wall, and I need somebody to be able to say who opens it, and if the answer is that it is a wall anybody can open then it is not a room, it is a leak, and the boundary has had a leak for fifteen weeks and would have said so."

Jo Verrick said: "The **haunch.** Where the frame of the outer service door sits on the stone, on the stair side of the anteroom. It is the one place in this building where a person has to put a hand every single day to know the door is still bearing, and I have put my hand on it every day for fifteen weeks, and it is warm, and it has been warm since the cold came, and I put it down to the pump.

"And I want it said out loud in this room that **I have had my hand on it every day for fifteen weeks and I called it warm and did not write one word about it in a book.** That is not a reading. That is a habit. And the habit is how a man misses the one thing in a week."

---

They went and stood at it at the fourth bell, and the anteroom was over its number with eleven people in it and Nera Oduya or Sefu Aran able to stop it, and it was the working part of the day, and nothing about it was a discovery. The outer service door stood open on its new packing of stone and clay and Marek Venn put his left palm flat into the haunch where the frame bears on the stone, which is the height Jo Verrick had told him it was when he asked her instead of guessing, and which had been his ninth question since he came down a shaft into this building, where the answers are the only part of it he is trusted with.

**It was warm, and it was exactly as warm as Jo Verrick had said it would be,** which was the fact that mattered most, because it meant the thing had not changed in the eleven minutes since she had touched it, and a habit is a habit and a fault is a fault and the difference between them is time and the only way anybody in this building has ever found out which one they are holding is by going back.

Then the room waited, because the room was extremely good at that and it had been getting better at it for eighteen weeks.

He got nothing. The same nothing, four degrees, a hand's breadth, the loudest thing in the anteroom, for the tenth time in his life and the first time in a fortnight.

"Ten," said Tams Quill, before Marek said anything, because Tams was already holding the chalk and had been holding it for two minutes. "Ordinary column, and your own hand, and the temperature, and the place, and the reason. **And I am not going to move it, and I want that in the book in the same breath, because I have moved three of yours into my column and every single time I was wrong to and I have said so three times, and this one is a different one.**"

"Why is it different."

"Because this one you did not go and find," said Tams Quill. "**The nine you went and found. This one a woman told you where to put your hand, and you put it there, and it was nothing, and you stayed for forty seconds after you knew.** The column with no heading is for things that came and got nothing. This did not come. This was asked for by a person, in a room, in front of a book."

The arithmetic went into the book, because it has gone into the book since the eleventh week and nobody has ever once asked her to stop: **seven in the ordinary column and three in the count's, which is ten in all, and she has done it twice.**

Marek Venn wrote it in the ordinary column, in his own left hand, because the right one cannot hold a pencil flat and never has since a jack in a dry service tunnel a long way above his head, and he was aware of four people watching him do it and he let them, and that is a different thing from four weeks ago.

---

"What is the joint," said Dalla Sen, with the chalk up.

Nobody could answer her. That is the fact about this boundary and it is the reason the plan on butcher's paper has six empty lines in it: eleven people knew where the water they drank came from and not one of them knew where it *entered*.

**Wen Pask had been thinking about nothing else for nine days and it took him one sentence, and the sentence is in the book, and it is the reason the whole of the eighteenth week happened.**

"The water that is on the pump is cold and it is pumped and it has been in and out of this dip every day for fifteen weeks and it is not the water in that pail, because the water in that pail is warm and it has come up out of stone that the pump is not touching," said Wen Pask. "**The pump is in the dip. The pail is on the nine steps. So the pail is not fed by the dip. So the pail is fed by something that is under this building and is not in the dip, and the only place in this building where a stranger can put his hand on a joint every day and it is warm under his hand is the one Jo Verrick has been putting her hand on for fifteen weeks.**"

"**So there is water in the wall of the anteroom,**" said Mikka Hale, "**and the anteroom is rated ten including one litter for no more than two hours, and the outer service door is the second way out of that room, and that door is the only thing standing between a full anteroom and eleven feet of shaft.**"

"Yes," said Jo Verrick.

"Then nobody touches it this week," said Mikka Hale, "and I want that said in a room, and I want it read out, and I want somebody to be told off to make sure I am obeyed."

"You will not need to be obeyed," said Renn Vale, from the landing, "because the boundary has spent fifteen weeks learning that the only orders anybody in it obeys are the ones that cost something. **And I am the one who will have to hold the terms of a room while it is over its number, and the outer service door is in the anteroom's rating and not in my terms, and I cannot hold both, and I am telling you that in a room instead of finding it out at the fourth bell in front of nine children.**

"So somebody has to ask me to stop that door being used, and it has to be a person who can be refused, and it has to be in a room, and it has to be today."

Nobody answered for some time, and Bel Ordry put his hand flat on the anteroom table where the plan lives inside his coat, and did not take it out.

---

**At the fifth bell the clerk read out the six things, and the two she had entered in her own hand, in that order, and she did not have to explain any of them:** the corridor shut, the school shut, the pot at **eleven days**, and the lip's range, and Renn Vale's seventh term, and the ninth line, and the blank on the review page with the two sentences on it instead of a name, and the air valve, **asked, and not answered, and the spring**, and Bel Ordry's bar at the seventh morning, and the counter-draft not yet arrived, and the four households at the second gate that this boundary had halved the water of and had said nothing to in a room.

And nobody in the anteroom suggested that a wall with warm stone in it was a reason for anything, and the pot was **eleven**, and it had never once gone up.
