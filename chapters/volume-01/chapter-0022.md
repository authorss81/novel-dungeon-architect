# Chapter 22 — A Room the Hearthholds Want

Ilsa Norr counted the children before she counted the room.

She came through the deeper gate with a slate under one arm and a look that took the anteroom apart in about four seconds: the low bearing beam, the grated well, the ten pegs at different heights, the bench, the wrapped bundle of cloth at the far end that she did not ask about because Jo had told her not to.

"Nine this morning," she said. "Ten on market days, and we are not always able to find the tenth. I am not here to be counted. I am here because your engineer is going to draw something and I would rather he drew it on the right floor."

Marek liked her immediately for the wrong reason and then for the right one.

"Then I will draw it where you can stop me," he said.

They crossed in a named work period with the count kept honestly. The anteroom kept nine of its ten: the six of them, Nera at the air valve, Sefu at the wheel, and Dalla at the threshold with the ledger. Renn stayed on the landing and was not counted. Jo, Mikka, and Ilsa went through the deeper gate ahead of the crew, and Sefu stood at the wheel with it open the whole period, because he had decided in advance that a shut gate was a thing everybody had to be told about before it happened and not after.

The lower corridor was not a hallway. It was a working neck.

Marek had been thinking of it as a throat, in the way engineers think of throats, meaning the narrowest section between two open spaces. He had not walked it in months. Now he walked it in the dark of the enclosed lantern and understood that the Hearthholds thought of it as something else: a place where a great many ordinary things had to happen in an order that nobody had ever written down.

The common kitchen ended at a painted line. Beyond it the floor turned, and the turn was not square; the corner had been built around a hearth whose back had to be cleared to let a person past. Then came the clinic door, which opened inward and had to be held. Then a stretch about nine paces long where the floor dipped, and which everyone walked on the same side of without discussion. Then the school door, and beyond it a common landing where the boundary stair began.

"Say the uses," Marek said. "I will not draw until I can say them."

Oren and Tams got the litter round the hearth corner, which took longer than getting it down the corridor had, because the corner is a curve and a litter is a straight thing and somebody has to tell the truth about that. Tams went backward on the front rail with one hand on the frame, and Oren could not see the far corner and had to be told about it twice, and neither of them pretended the second telling was unnecessary.

Pell went ahead with a bit of chalk and marked the low place in the dip, and marked it again from the other side, because a dip that measures two inches at one end can measure four at the other, and this one did.

Iven put his hand on the hearth's back stone and stopped everyone.

"Before we talk about rooms," he said, "understand that this corner is not a corridor that got bent. Somebody built a hearth into a wall and then built the passage around the hearth, and everything past this point is nine inches narrower than the building it came from. That is not a fault. It is a hundred years of a cook's decision, and it is the reason this is a throat."

"Then it cannot be widened," Marek said.

"It cannot be widened without moving the hearth, which is a kitchen's whole winter. Remember that, because it is the first thing that will be proposed."

Mikka spoke first, because she was standing in the dip with a hand on the clinic door and the corridor's worst habit in front of her.

"When a fever comes, the clinic corner fills. The low cot goes, the second cot goes, and then the people carrying the basin are standing in this dip blocking the return, and the kitchen cannot get a hot pot past them." She pointed at the hearth corner. "So either the clinic sends its people into the school corner, where they sit among nine children, or the kitchen stops cooking, or the corridor stops. Every time, it is the corridor that stops. And the corridor is also the way out."

"How often?"

"Twice a month. In a bad month, four."

Ilsa said, "The school corner empties twice a day and fills for three hours. If the room above the east stair is used for anything during lessons, the children have nowhere to be that is not the corridor. They cannot be in the kitchen. They cannot be in the clinic. They cannot be on the landing, because the landing is an exit."

"Who keeps the register," Marek asked.

"Renn's mother kept it. It is on a shelf in the upper gallery with forty-one years of it in three boxes, and it is not mine to lend and not yours to read."

"I would not read it."

"You would move it. You would move it, and the shelf would be empty, and in four years nobody could say who had been in these rooms." She set the slate down on the low stone step. "That is the refusal I was sent to give. Not the room. The boxes."

Jo shifted the basket on her arm. "And mine. My shutter route takes me to four households. When one of them is being cut open for a new hinge, the household, the neighbours, and the person watching all end up in that dip, because the other three places are in use. Half a day of four households standing in a puddle saying nothing, and then I go back the next day because the work is not finished."

Dalla had been writing. She read back what she had written, which was her way of correcting everyone. "Stated uses: a waiting place for families during ordinary repair; a place to put a class that has nowhere to sit; a place to receive a person who cannot walk without shutting the kitchen or the clinic return."

"And the part everyone leaves out," Sefu said. "A person who cannot walk goes down this corridor on a litter. That means the corridor is the stretcher route. Any plan that fills this corridor with people has not left itself a stretcher route."

Ruk Sella came in from the kitchen with a water pail and stopped when she saw the group. She had a boy of about six on her hip and a second child holding her thumb, and she had the expression of someone who had been told the crew was coming and had decided to be present for it.

"Which room are you taking?" she asked.

"That is what we are asking you," Marek said.

"You are not asking me. You are asking whether it will fit. Those are different questions and you will find out they are different." She shifted the pail to her other hand. "Put my two in it during the day and I will not ask anything. Do not put them in it at night. The small one wakes when the stone is cold and then nobody sleeps."

"Then that is a limit on the use," Dalla said, writing.

"And I will not have it called a night room," Ilsa said, before anyone offered. "Whatever else it is, it is not a night room. A gallery with a floor in it is not a bedroom. It is a room with benches, a shelf, four hooks, a grate in the high wall, and a stair that has been worn in the middle for longer than anyone living remembers. It was a school. It is a school twice a day and a store once a week. I will not hand that over for sleeping."

Marek took out the grease pencil stub and then put it away again, which was the first honest thing he did all morning.

"I am not asking for the gallery tonight," he said. "I am asking what it could be, and I am not asking for the boxes."

"Then draw on something else," Ilsa said, and gave him the blank back of Dalla's spare sheet.

Marek stood in the dip of the lower corridor and tried to do the thing he was good at, which was to turn a list of needs into a structure. Bearing first. The corridor's floor was a stone slab on fill, and the wall on the kitchen side was an old infill laid later than the wall on the school side, and that meant the two walls had different ages and therefore different habits. Breath second: the corridor had one high grille at the kitchen end, one low vent at the school end, and the whole run shared its air with the clinic return channel, which meant anything that moved the air for the corridor moved the air for a person lying on a cot. Drainage third: a covered channel ran the length of the dip and emptied toward the pump gallery, so a flooded corridor was a flooded pump gallery. Escape fourth: the school door, the boundary stair, and the anteroom behind it.

Change fifth, and this was where the list became a problem.

He had the diagram in his head. It was clean, it had numbers in it, and it was a diagram of a building rather than of an afternoon.

Leena said, "Say it in words and not as a plan."

"I was going to—"

"I know. Write it on the floor with your finger if you must, but say it in words." She was on the litter against the school-side wall, in the dip, where a passing person had to step around her feet. "You have drawn a corridor that a person on a litter can travel and a crowd cannot share. Say that to me before you say it to anyone else."

"It can carry a litter and it cannot carry a litter and eight people at once."

"So who chooses?" Leena asked. "Because in this corridor, somebody chooses every time, and so far it has been the person on the litter who is the one who waits."

Marek had no answer, and Nera gave him one she was going to take back.

"I want to see the upper gallery before I say anything about the corridor's air," she said.

The air engineer had been quiet since the kitchen line. She had walked the corridor with the dyed cloth held under the high grille, then under the low vent, then at the clinic return, and she had said nothing, which from Nera meant she was still measuring.

"Grille at the kitchen end, vent at the school end, and the return channel in common with the clinic," Marek said. "That reads as a workable breath path."

"That reads as what I can see in an empty corridor." Nera folded the cloth into her coat. "I know what a room does with no people in it. It does nothing. I have stood in four hundred empty rooms and every one of them was breathing perfectly. A room breathes when it has something to take out of it, and you cannot learn that from air that has not been used."

"Then what would you learn from it here?"

"Whether the high grille has an independent relief that is not the corridor. Whether the low vent is a return or a leak. Whether the two of them fight when the pump interval pulls and the stair pressure pushes." She looked toward the east end, where a fixed stone stair climbed out of sight. "And whether that gallery has anything under the deck that can move air, or whether the whole of its ventilation is a hole in a wall with a room behind it. A hole in a wall with a room behind it is not ventilation. It is a promise about a room you have not entered."

"You have not been up there," Marek said.

"No. So I will not draw an air plan for it."

Sefu put both hands on the wheel. "The east stair is a fixed flight. Fourteen treads and a landing. It is not the stair that repeats and it has never once moved under anybody's weight, and I will not have the two confused in a record again." He looked at Ilsa. "Your gallery, your terms. When, and with whom, and what they may not touch."

Ilsa took the slate back up. "Not the boxes. Not the register. And not during the second hour of lessons, which is when the reading goes wrong and the children are worst."

"And what do they get in exchange for a survey?"

"The truth about whether it is safe, which is a thing I have wanted since I was a child and have never been given once."

Marek looked down the corridor. The boy on Ruk Sella's hip had put a finger into the dip of the floor and was watching it come away grey. Nine paces, a turn that was not square, a door that opened inward, and a stair at the far end with fourteen treads. Somewhere above it there was a room with forty-one years of a school register in it and a floor nobody had stood on in longer than anyone could say.

"Two work periods," he said. "One to walk the corridor and measure what is there, one for the gallery. Sefu opens the gate for the work and counts whoever is inside. Nobody sleeps in anything, nobody is admitted, and nothing we learn turns into a room that is open."

Ilsa looked at her slate, then at the stair.

"Take Illev," she said. "Not Renn, not the door. I want the person who can read a stone and say what it is doing." She looked at Marek's open right hand, resting against the wall. "And I want the other one too. The one who keeps saying what is missing."

Leena raised her good hand an inch off the litter rail.

Marek wrote the two work periods into Dalla's ledger with his left hand, and under them the four words that mattered: *no room opened.* Then he asked Nera to write the air question on the same page, so that the survey would start by trying to break the plan rather than by admiring it.

She wrote: *the gallery's air is unknown until it has been made to fail.*
