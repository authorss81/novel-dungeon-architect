# Chapter 202 — A Flow And Not A Count

The sixth day of the thirty-ninth week is a Saturday, the corridor is open, and the school ran for the forty-third morning in the room at the kitchen-line end, and the nine went up the nine steps at the first bell and came down them at the fourth and went out at the far door. The pump did not run. It has not run since nine minutes past the second bell yesterday and there is a line of chalk on the head of its own frame and a hand's breadth under that line a second mark which is not chalk, and between the two of them there is three inches and a bit of a decision nobody in this room has made yet.

No market came into this building on this day and nobody here went looking for one. The one thing anybody knew about a sixth day arrived in a woman's mouth at the first bell, and what she had brought was a fact about a counter, and it was put into the room the way a fact is put into a room in this building, which is by somebody saying it out loud and then standing there while it is written down.

It was a warm morning for the depth of it, which happens twice. The flags by the kitchen-line door had gone from cold to cool by the fourth bell. Along the common landing, through the corridor, a girl of nine was learning a thing about nine stones and kept stopping to look at the ninth one, and nobody in the anteroom could hear what she was being told and all of them knew it was about the ninth one.

Jo Verrick had the frame at the first bell, a finger and two-thirds, and the party wall was a quarter of an inch, the twenty-second reading, and the same as twenty-first, and the string was where the string was. She put her thumb under the string and the thumb came out of it right. She gave the four refusals about the line along the join in her own words before anybody asked her for them, and Dalla Sen entered all four as four refusals and not as one, for the twenty-second morning running.

Renn Vale put her ear to the party wall at the first bell for about as long as a sentence takes to say, four times over, and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times. The tenth morning out of ten. A figure of nothing went into the book against it, and into the space where a reason goes the fact that this was a morning on which she had something else to do and had done that instead, and that she entered the something else without being asked.

The anteroom's terms were said at the first bell by a person and repeated by the clerk, all six. The count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change. The outer service door was the one a body went out of. The fifth of those six terms was in a room with both of the people it names in it. The third of the six was the water-carrying, and it was said, and it was six pails a day, every day in seven, in two crossings, four and then two, and it was said by a person who had said it every morning for a month in a room where nobody had been able to keep it for four of those mornings.

Then Mikka Hale came in with the cloth over her arm and did not go to the kitchen-line doorway first, which is what she does.

"**I am going to say the arithmetic,**" she said, "**and I am going to say it once, and I am going to say it the way I would say it to a person in a kitchen and not the way I have been saying it in this room for nineteen weeks, and I would like a clerk to have her hand near the book and I would like nobody to stop me.**"

Dalla Sen had her hand near the book.

"**Six pails a day is not a number of people,**" Mikka Hale said. "**It is a number of water. It is how much of it comes up off that seam and out of that pump and in nine times over a week, and the number of pails is set by how many of us there are, and I have known how many of us there are every morning for two years because I feed them, and I have never written it down and I am not going to write it down this morning, and I would like it entered that I said that out loud in a room and that I said it before anybody asked me for it.**"

"**Entered, in the cook's words,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that she was not asked, and entered that the number of people in this building has not been given by anybody and has not been written down by anybody, and entered that the cook has just said that the two of those are the same fact and that they are not.**"

"**They are not the same fact, and that is the whole of what I have come in here to say,**" said Mikka Hale, "**and if this room cannot hear the difference between the two then this room is going to do something extremely stupid this week and I am asking the clerk to write down that I asked her to write it down.**"

Then she waited, which she does not often do, and Renn Vale came down the four steps and stood in the middle of the floor.

"**Say the other half,**" she said.

"**The other half is four pails a day,**" Mikka Hale said, "**and four pails a day is what this boundary has been drinking and cooking with and washing in since the twenty-second week, and it is not a round number and it is not a careful number and it is the number I have been pouring for two years. And when the pump ran I had the seam as well, and the seam has given me nothing for a fortnight and the pot is one day, and yesterday a pump stopped, and today I want to say what a day costs me in pails and I have never said it in this room and I am saying it now.**"

"**Then say what it is,**" said Renn Vale.

"**No,**" said Mikka Hale.

The room did what it does about four times in a week, which is nothing, for about two seconds.

"**I will tell you why, and it is a good reason and it is not a moral one,**" she said. "**A number of pails a day is a flow. It tells you how much water this building uses in a day. It does not tell you how many people are in it, and it is not made of people, and it cannot be turned into people by anybody in this room with a pencil. If I say four, and if some person in this building works out that four is one pail for four, and if that number gets into a clerk's book, and if it gets up a passage in a coat, then in a year there is a figure of what is in this anteroom on a school morning and it came from a kitchen and it is about eleven years old.**"

"**And the number of people in this building is nobody's,**" said Dalla Sen, from the book, and she was not writing.

"**The number of people in this building is nobody's,**" said Mikka Hale, "**and it has been nobody's since the twenty-ninth week on purpose and in writing and I have read the reason and it is a good reason and I am not arguing with it. I am telling you that I have a different instrument and that my instrument is not that one, and that you can have mine without having yours, and that is the whole of the reason I came in here and did not go to my door.**"

Marek Venn had been at the end of the trestle since the first bell with his shut book under his forearm, and what he had been doing for the last four minutes was arithmetic, and what the arithmetic was told him was that a flow was a cheaper instrument than a count and a cook was holding it and a cook could be asked a question about it by anybody in the room at any time, and that a flow is not a column and cannot become one, and that a flow can be put on a slate and does not date anything and cannot be joined to the ten on a stranger's sheet lying six feet away from his elbow on that table.

"**Then it is a flow,**" he said, "**and the room should take it, and the room should take it in the cook's words and not in mine, and it should be entered that it is a quantity of water this boundary uses in a day and not a quantity of people in it, and it should be entered that the two are not to be converted into one another by anybody in this room, ever, including on a bad morning.**"

"**Entered, in his words and with the cook's words in front of it,**" said Dalla Sen, and wrote for about as long as it takes to say a sentence, "**and entered that a flow is a third instrument in this building and the third instrument is held by the person who feeds us and there is no heading over it and it is not a column and it is not a count and it dates nothing.**"

"**And the half against, which is mine and which I am obliged to put in the room,**" said Renn Vale.

"**Say it,**" said Mikka Hale.

"**A flow is a number and this room has a rule about numbers, and the rule is a good one and the rule is that a number said out loud in this room becomes a number this room has agreed to. So a flow said out loud in this room is a number this room has agreed to, and there is now a number about how much water this building takes in a day, and a number about water is a number about how many people are in it with one division in front of it, and I have watched this room find a division before and put the answer in a book, and I am not doing that again on a Saturday morning on the word of a cook who has said she is not going to be asked.**"

Nobody answered her, and the pump did not run, and the pot was one day.

Then Renn Vale took the slate out of her apron, which she does four mornings in seven, and put it on the trestle, face up, and did not read anything off it.

"**Then here is the thing I have to say and I would rather say it now than on Wednesday,**" she said. "**The third of my six terms is six pails a day, every day in seven, in two crossings, four and then two. It was a good term. I set it in the twenty-second week when this building had a pump and a seam that gave and a pot at nine days and I set it because a boundary that does not say how much water it is going to ask for is a boundary that gets told. It has been asked for every morning for seventeen weeks and it has been kept for seventeen weeks and it has not been kept for the last four and nobody in this room noticed until I said it on Friday, and I noticed on Friday, and I have been carrying a term I knew was false for two days and did not say so, and I would like all of that entered in that order and not tidied.**"

"**Entered, in the keeper's words, and in that order,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**And I am not going to break it in this room,**" Renn Vale said.

"**You are going to have to,**" said Bel Ordry, from the top of the nine steps.

"**I am going to have to,**" she agreed, "**and I am not going to do it here. A term is a thing I say to a gate, and a gate is where a term is kept, and if I stand in a room with seventeen people in it and say that I cannot carry six pails a day, then from Wednesday morning this is a room that cannot carry six pails a day, and it is a room, and a room is a thing that can be counted, and a room that can be counted is a room with a figure in it. So I am going to go down to the second gate this afternoon and I am going to say it at a gate, in front of the four households and a clerk who does not live here, and I am going to say it as the keeper of a term and not as a woman telling a room, and it will be a fact about a gate and it will not be a fact about this room.**"

"**That is not a small distinction and I want it entered that a person in this room thinks it is,**" said Nella Bray, from the anvil end, with her slate under her arm.

"**It is not a small distinction,**" Renn Vale said, "**and it is not mine and it is not a trick, and I will tell you what it is. It is the only place I have got where a sentence I say goes out of this building and does not come back with my name on it.**"

Nobody improved on that, and Dalla Sen entered it in the space where a reason goes, and did not enter the second half of it twice.

At the fourth bell the nine came down the nine steps and went out at the far door, and the terms were said again before the room filled and not after, and the third of the six was six pails a day and was said by a person who knew it was false and said it anyway, and that went into the book in the space where a finding goes, and not in the space where a reason goes.

Then the room got to the sixth bell and the numbers, and the clerk read the day's figure and the total out loud, and then read the count, and gave the reason herself, and the reason was that a room which has just been given a new instrument has to be told two things it already knew so that it does not make the new one the only one.

"**Two today and three hundred and six since the keeper signed. The boundary is at four hundred and twenty-three and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.**"

"**Wen Pask is one hundred and forty-seven days and one hundred and forty-seven nights, forty-four weeks of a clerk saying out loud in a room that she is going to keep writing it down.**" She turned the slate face down over it. "**It is not going to be one hundred and forty-eight. It is a number of days and not a number of anything, and I read it out on Friday and I read it out today and I would like it entered that a cook said a number out loud this morning and that I have read this one out twice in two days, and that those are two different numbers and nobody in this room has joined them and I have noticed myself not joining them.**"

The board was not walked, and the reason was entered, and the reason was that a room which has been handed a third kind of instrument by a person who is not a keeper and not a clerk does not walk a column in the same hour.

The crew are at seventy-five and a half and did not move, and the reason for the day is in the book, and the reason is that five men who have not been asked in nineteen weeks cannot be asked on a morning when the thing they would be asked about is a term their own foreman has just said out loud is false, and a man does not ask a crew to carry a thing that is already being carried by somebody else badly.

The pot is one day. The seam gave nothing on Friday and nothing on Saturday and is at four threads, and the fire in the room at the kitchen-line end has now not been lit for fifteen weeks, and the four households at a second gate have nothing and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody.

And at the seventh bell the anteroom was a room with a hole in the corner of its floor, and a trestle with a book on it and a slate face down on the trestle next to the book, and a pump in the middle of the floor that had not run for a day, and a stranger's sheet with a wax head at each end of it lying where it has lain for a fortnight. A woman had said a number in that room at the ninth bell that was about water and not about people, and a keeper had said that her own term of seventeen weeks standing was false and had four days of it unkept, and she was going to tell a gate about it this afternoon, and nobody knew yet what was at the bottom of that gate or what it wanted.
