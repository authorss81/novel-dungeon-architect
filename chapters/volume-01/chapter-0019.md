# Chapter 19 — The Work Sheet Other Hands Could Read

By the time the crew returned from the restricted stair, the maintenance draft had been folded and unfolded so many times that its creases had become part of the record.

Dalla laid it on the bench beside the old route charter. Dalla’s local record of the guild papers and Iven’s suspended-bond receipt remained on a separate board. The wrapped empty-seat plate stayed on its spread of work cloth at the far end, its symbol hidden, its evidence undisturbed. Between the plate and the route papers, Dalla placed a clean board wide enough for everyone to see.

“An agreement people can inherit cannot live in one person’s memory,” she said. “It has to say what the next person should do.”

The blank board was the only surface in the room that was not already written on. Marek had spent the day mapping a stair and returned to a room where the pump still knocked. The old route charter had one clear purpose: it identified the licensed line. The new work sheet had several. It needed to tell a resident maintainer what to do with a chattering pump, a crew member how to stop a task, an air engineer when to close a baffle, and a boundary keeper when the room should empty.

He began with a clean sentence: *The route is maintained safely until—*

Leena touched the board with two fingers of her left hand. “Until when?”

“The next review.”

“Name the review.”

Marek said it the way a man reads a clause aloud. “At the next water change after two pressure turns. If a condition changes sooner, the review happens sooner.”

Nera tapped the air valve. “The pressure turns are not yet a schedule. Write ‘after a pressure turn’ and let the people at the cloth decide whether the route can resume.”

Iven took the chalk. His hand was steady, but Marek noticed that he kept looking at the suspended-bond receipt folded beside the ledger.

“The pump work needs its own sequence,” Iven said. “Otherwise the next tired person will think the delivery check can be opened while the frame is loaded.”

He did not write it down first. He crouched at the grate instead and talked Jo through it in order, pointing with the chalk: the gland checked and dry first, the delivery shut before any pressure was relieved, the seat cleaned without scoring the bronze, the frame foot packed rather than raised, the pump primed before use, the strokes counted aloud by whoever is not on the lever. Stop for a wet gland, a reversed cloth, a new movement at the housing, or water at the sill.

Dalla wrote it down as he said it, in her own hand, which was the point. If the sequence lived only in Iven’s memory it would leave with him.

He paused before the next line.

“Add that I fitted the gland and key,” he said. “Not that I own the pump.”

Jo took the chalk from him. “Add that the person who cleans the check records the time and the cost.”

He asked for it, and Dalla wrote it, because the point of a sequence is that it survives the person who knows it.

Marek watched the craft become a sequence another person could follow without hearing Iven explain it. The work was still his until he gave it away. That was the difference between a craft lesson and a claim.

Nera took the board next. She wrote the air conditions in short lines and read each one back before she started the next: the outer service door open; the enclosed lantern instead of any open flame; the pump chamber isolated during housing work; the anteroom air checked before anyone is counted in; the clinic return watched during a pressure turn; the service bypass hatch latched. Then a last line for the three conditions that emptied the room — a reversed cloth, a wet mineral seam at the pump, or water at the sill: clear to the upper landing.

Jo stopped her before the last line. “Water at the sill. That happens when the pump stops, not only when the air turns. Are you writing it as an air rule or a pump rule?”

“Both,” Nera said. “The sill does not care which of them put the water there.”

Jo thought about it. “Then write that nobody argues about whose fault it is. Nobody should have to prove they were right before they move.”

Nera added those words, and put her name beside the air section.

Sefu read the whole section twice before he added anything of his own, and he added one condition at a time instead of a paragraph: no deeper passage without the consent of the people responsible for the rooms entered; he may refuse or stop boundary admission; Nera may stop the air mechanism; a resident may refuse a work task or a room without a crew member being put in their place. Then, in the order a person leaving would find it, the one Mikka had asked for by name — withdrawal stops new admission and new work, and people already inside leave by the outer landing.

He signed beneath it and left the pen on the bench.

Dalla took it up again. “Payment and cost are not the same as permission.”

Jo’s name went beside the pump-cleaning line. “The first cleaning took half a day from my tool route. That cost is not paid by the fact that I learned the work.”

From the threshold, Mikka set a bowl of cooked grain near the edge of the board. “The kitchen can provide recovery water and a meal during a named work period. That is support for the shift, not a promise of permanent room and not a claim on anyone’s labor.”

“And Iven’s guild work?” Marek asked.

Iven looked at the frozen-bond receipt. “The guild has suspended the witness privilege. If the guild refuses payment, the Hearthholds are not required to pay it for them.”

Dalla wrote: *Iven Tallo’s work is recorded as a local maintenance contribution; guild reimbursement and personal compensation remain unresolved. No route ownership is created by the debt.*

Marek read the sentence twice. It was not elegant, but it prevented the work from being used as a reason to transfer the pump.

“Add a line for the crew,” he said. “Temporary maintainers receive food and water for scheduled work according to the local count. Their own drinking reserve remains separate. They may withdraw from a future shift. They receive no ownership and no permanent room.”

Mikka looked up from the threshold. “They receive no permanent room, but Leena may use the clinic cot while her shoulder is treated. That is care, not residence.”

“Write that separately,” Leena said.

Dalla did.

The board was becoming crowded. Marek tried to add a heading for *route* and found that the term still carried too much assumption. He crossed it out and wrote *physical connection* instead.

Then he wrote *pump* and *air gate* and *maintenance shift* and *boundary record*. Each line had a limit beside it. The guild papers stayed apart. The returning stair restriction had its own sheet, attached by a cord to the route record so nobody could lift the pump plan and leave the stair behind.

A knock came from the pump.

Jo looked at the delivery mark. “It is time.”

Marek began to assign the work from memory.

“Jo operates. Iven explains. Oren and Tams take the load. Pell calls the mark. Nera watches the air. Renn watches the door. Sefu can stop the room.”

“Who is writing the result?” Dalla asked.

“I am.”

“You are not holding the board flat.”

Marek put his left hand flat on the board, then turned to the two smooth stones holding the old sheet. His right fingers could not keep the chalk in a line, and his left wrist ached after the stair trip. He could dictate, but a dictated record was not the same as a record made by the people who would use it.

“Dalla,” he said. “You write the result. I will call the structural changes.”

Then the clinic bell went, and the board had to wait.

Mikka appeared at the gate with a request in the same tone Jo used when a route had gone long. Dalla had a name for it: a watch change. Isel’s fever had broken in the night, and Mikka wanted the lower clinic cot cleared and made up, and the clinic corner wanted a person standing in the return channel while it was done.

Sefu looked at Dalla. Dalla looked at Mikka. Neither of them looked at Marek, which was itself an answer.

“Named care period,” Sefu said. “Clinic corner only. Dalla goes as the witness, Jo as the hand. Iven, you stay on this side of the wheel. Oren, you and Tams carry her when it is done, and no sooner.”

Leena chose the moment herself. “Put me on the litter for the walk,” she said. “I want the shoulder dressed while I am in the room, not because the room needs me.”

“Care is not residence,” Mikka said, and went to get the cot clear.

Oren and Tams settled her onto the litter and carried her through. Sefu closed the gate behind her and put his weight against the wheel, and the anteroom kept its ten without a room-count argument. When the dressing was finished and the clinic corner was quiet again, they brought her back, and she came to the board with the board across her knees and a fresh wrap on her shoulder.

Jo looked at the water mark. “And if I stop?”

“Stop the pump,” Nera said. “Do not wait for permission.”

“If the air cloth changes?” Renn called.

“Clear the room,” Nera replied. “The upper landing is the destination.”

“And if the pump fails while the crew is asleep?” Leena asked.

The question stopped the board. Everyone looked toward the anteroom’s dry floor and the pump gallery below it. The old working rating required the pump to be attended. A crew member could sleep through a change if the water stayed down, but no one had yet said who would listen to the delivery check.

Jo answered first. “The pump stops when the shift ends. If the water needs lowering, a resident or a named crew operator stays with it. No one sleeps while a pump runs without a person assigned to hear it.”

“And if the next shift does not come?” Sefu asked.

Jo looked toward the lower route. “Then the route closes for use until someone can take the watch. We do not let a temporary promise become an unattended machine.”

Nera added a line to the air section: *Pump may run only during a named attended shift. No overnight running without a resident or accepted crew watch.*

Marek had expected the agreement to require more from him. Instead, it required him to stop being the person who automatically carried the next shift. The distinction was practical, but it touched the old part of him that believed responsibility meant being the last person willing to say no.

He placed the chalk beside the board. “Then I will not promise that the crew can cover every future hour.”

Renn looked at him. “You can promise that you will name the hour you can cover.”

“That I can do.”

Then the pump began its shift, and for the first time the room ran from the board instead of from Marek’s memory.

Jo took the lever. Iven stayed beside the grate and talked the sequence aloud exactly as Dalla had written it, so the sheet could be checked against the work rather than trusted. On the third count, Leena called out that the pump-parts box had been set inside the escape line. Jo stopped, moved it, and began the count again from one.

Marek watched the floor. He felt a brief pressure change beneath the bearing strip, then the ordinary vibration of the pump. He did not turn the feeling into a command. He stated what he saw: “The frame is answering the load without a new movement at the plinth.”

Dalla wrote it down, and nobody asked him to read it back.

The work sheet did not make the room safe forever. It made the next action less private.

When the shift ended, the maintainers read their own sections back instead of marking them. Nera read the air limits aloud and said they were hers to stop, not hers to grant. Sefu read the boundary and stop conditions and asked that the withdrawal line stay in the same order as the air lines, so a person leaving would find it without reading to the bottom. Jo read the pump sequence and read the cost under it a second time. Renn read the door watch. Dalla read the record line and added that the record was hers to write and not hers to keep. Iven read the craft section, stopped at the line saying he had not been given ownership of the pump, and observed that the sequence would live in Dalla’s hand and not his. Oren and Tams read the lifting and relief positions. Pell read his level call and the sentence saying he could not run or take unsafe loads. Leena read the corrections and put a finger under the words *may be corrected by any person present*. Marek read his own structural observations back last, and it took longer than any of them, because he had to stop twice and begin again.

Nobody marked the sheet as agreed. Sefu’s name sat under his own conditions and Nera’s under hers, because each of them would answer for the lines they had written. The rest of the board was still blank space.

Jo gave the reason before anyone else thought to ask for one. “The first line I would want to write is the condition of that seat, and the seat has not been opened yet. I am not signing a room I have not looked at.”

Dalla wrote that reason at the head of the page and drew a box around it. A sheet that people had only read was still a draft, and a draft that named its own missing part was worth more than a signature taken before the part was known.

At the bottom, Dalla drafted the review line and left it unmarked:

*Next review: the second work-bell after the next complete pressure turn, or sooner if the air, the water, the pump, or the boundary changes.*

The drafted line was the last unmarked thing on the board. It was not a promise that the route would continue. It was a promise that someone would look again.

Sefu hung the board where the pump crew could see it from the grate. Dalla’s copies of the guild papers and Iven’s suspended-bond receipt stayed on their separate board, and the returning stair stayed restricted beyond the service bend.

The route was still conditional, and half the board was still empty.

The next shift now had a name, a limit, a cost, and a way to stop.
