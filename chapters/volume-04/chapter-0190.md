# Chapter 190 — The Check Crosses And The Count Does Not

The fifth day of the thirty-sixth week is a Friday, the corridor is open, and the school ran for the thirty-seventh morning in the room at the kitchen-line end. Nine children went up the nine steps at the first bell with a woman ahead of them, and nine children crossed the anteroom twice, and one of them did not go round the corner of the floor because on this morning the corner of the floor was a hole.

It had been a corner with a chalk line round it since the fourth bell yesterday, and a foreman and a clerk and a woman who keeps a corridor had stood looking at that line and agreed to take the flag out of it. It became an actual hole at the second bell, when Emrys Callow's crew got a bar under it and lifted, and a fourth man took the bar and the other end, and a flag stone that had been in the floor of this anteroom since before anybody in this building was born came up out of it in one piece and stood on edge on the flags with clay coming off it in grey flakes.

The bed under it was what the bulk of this building looks like underneath everything. Nine hundred years of stone, a course of lime about the height of a hand, and then two hundred years of somebody's repair with a hand's width of nothing under a sill, and the flag had been bedded on the lime and the lime had gone, and the flag had been rocking a little in the corner for about a year and everybody had stepped round it.

Renn Vale watched the line in the party wall at the first bell, which is what she does on an open morning, and came back and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, and the clerk entered it as a figure of nothing.

Dalla Sen looked at the line she had written. "**A figure of nothing,**" "**I have entered that twice this week and I am going to say in a room that I do not know what a clerk enters for a nothing, and I would rather say that than invent a word for it.**"

"**A nothing is not a number,**" said Tams Quill from the bench, without getting up. "**It is the absence of one and it is the only honest thing anybody has put in a book in this room for a fortnight.**"

"**Then it stays as what it is,**" said Dalla Sen, and wrote it, and did not improve it.

Then Mero Senn said the thing he has said in this room three times in six weeks and had never once had to say on a morning with a hole in it.

"**A plan with a named injured person in it is not a plan,**" he said, "**and I have named her, and her name is Orla Kern, and she is nine, and she is the fastest of the nine and she came round that corner at the first bell without slowing down at all, and I watched her do it.**"

Ilsa Norr did not turn round. "**She went round it,**"

"**She went round it by four inches and at speed,**" said Mero Senn, "**and I am not going to be the man who tells a nine-year-old to be careful by a corner. So the answer is the nine go round, and you said that yesterday and I am not taking it back.**"

"**I am not taking it back either,**" said Ilsa Norr, "**and I will walk it with them the first four mornings and then I will stop, and Dalla writes that in the space where a reason goes and not in a column, and the reason is that a keeper who walks a room for four mornings is a woman who has decided nine children go round a hole, and not a woman who has noticed that they go round it.**"

Bel Ordry took it from the step, as he takes things. "**And the rating,**" "**Ten including one litter for no more than two hours, and it is a permission and not a count, and a permission that says ten is still ten with a hole in the floor of the room the permission is about. The hole does not change it. Nobody is to pretend it does.**"

Then Callow put his boot on the bed where the flag had been and gave it the two hard knocks of a man who is not a woman with a slate, and the bed sounded the way a bed of lime and stone sounds, which is to say it sounded fine, and that is not what a bed is for.

"**Right,**" said Callow. "**Before we move it. The terms are on the trestle and I have read them back to a clerk twice and they are the same both times, and I am not going to read them a third time, because a third time is a ritual and this is not a ritual, it is a Friday.**"

"**You have not told this room what you came down for,**" said Dalla Sen, with the slate face down.

"**I did not come down for this,**" Callow told her. "**I came down for a man with two names. I have been in this room for six days and I have not been converted and I am not going to be, and my five have not been converted either, and if anybody in this room has understood anything this week it is that we are two crews standing in the same anteroom and we still do not trust each other for the two reasons we gave on the second day, and both of those reasons are still true.**"

"**So: what happens to that wall,**" said Renn Vale, and had not moved off the step.

"**This.**" He put his thumb on the stone standing on edge on the flags. "**Two crews. One wall. One morning. And we are not one crew, and nobody in this room is going to say that we are, and I would like that in the book in the clerk's words and not in mine.**"

"**Nobody is going to hear me say you are one crew,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and here is the shape, and I am going to say it slowly, because a shape said fast is a policy.**"

She turned the slate over and did not put anything on it.

"**One. The six terms stand for both crews. The same six. The kitchen-line side is not worked and not entered by either of you. Nobody is sent to the low end. Nothing goes into the line of nothing in that wall. The person who watches that line is still me, and I am not the man who built anything on that wall and I am not the man who found it, and either of you can say so out loud if you want it said again.**"

Callow nodded. "**It is said,**"

"**Two. Every person whose hands go on that wall says out loud, in a room, what they are holding and what stops it. That is the second of the six and it does not care whose hands they are.**"

"**Three,**" he began. "**And here is the one I have been waiting for somebody to say since Wednesday. I have not said it and I am not going to, because a foreman who proposes the thing he wants is a foreman selling. Somebody in this room who is not me is going to say it and I would like it said before we lift a stone.**"

Nobody said it, and the pump got four strokes in.

"**Then I will say it,**" said Renn Vale, "**because he has asked me to and not the room, and because I have been not saying a thing for two days and I am tired of it.**"

She came down the four steps and stood in the middle of the floor with the hole at her feet.

"**Three. The check crosses and the hours do not.**"

"**Put that in words a foreman could work to,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**The person who checks the work is a person from the other crew,**" said Renn Vale. "**Not from this room. Not from this building. From the crew that did not do it. Your five set the stone, one of ours reads the bed it is bedded on, and one of yours reads the head of the shore it is going under. That is the whole of the arrangement and it is not a small thing, and I want the room to hear why it is not a small thing. A crew that checks its own work is a crew that has stopped being able to find out. I have watched nine years of that. It is not dishonesty. It is that a man holding a bar cannot see the end of his own timber.**"

"**And the hours,**" said Callow.

"**The hours do not cross, and they do not join, and the two of us are not going to end up with one number.**" Renn Vale put her hands flat on her hips. "**Your foreman says his hours in your room. My clerk says mine in hers. They go on two lines. There is no heading over the two lines, because a heading over two lines is a column and a column in this building has caused every trouble I have had for thirty-five weeks. And nobody adds them up. Not this week, not in a month, not ever, and I want that written down as a thing we are choosing and not as a thing we have run out of ways to do.**"

"**The half against that,**" said Bel Ordry, and he came down one step for it, which nobody in the room expected. "**A boundary that cannot state a figure of its own hours on a wall is a boundary that will be told one. And the person who tells it will have a column, and the column will be headed for the person responsible for the work, and the person responsible for the work will be a foreman four days up a passage whose name is in both lines of a form.**"

"**And the first half against that,**" said Renn Vale. "**A room's first column has been a number we are not allowed to state for thirty-five weeks and I have said out loud in this room more than once that the empty column is the whole of what I have. This morning we have made one. Not on a page, in a book, under no heading, and I would like a person to notice that the thing I have been defending for thirty-five weeks is a figure I could not give.**"

"**Four,**" said Renn Vale, "**and I am going to say it, because a foreman with a book four and a half days up this passage is in this room this morning whether anybody likes it or not. There is a piece of paper with a route-holder's name in one line and a second line with nothing in it. Nothing in this arrangement touches that paper. Nobody is going to ask about it, and I am not going to ask about it, and if a name goes in a second line anywhere in this building this week then it will have been put there by somebody who was not in this room and I will know it by Friday.**"

Iven Tallo had been at the anvil end with the chisel in his right hand and his left open and flat on the anvil, and he put the chisel down, which is what he does before he says a thing he means, and he said nothing for about two strokes of the pump.

"**Thank you,**" he said.

Renn Vale did not thank him for it. "**Iven Tallo,**" and nothing else, which in that room is a way of asking.

"**The thing is that nobody has asked me for a signature for six weeks,**" said Iven Tallo, "**and a woman has just put a whole morning's arrangement in a room and protected the one piece of paper in it that has my name on it without being asked to. I noticed. I am not going to do anything about it, and that is going to cost me the thing I want, and I have not worked out what that is yet.**"

"**Four,**" said Renn Vale, and let it alone.

"**Five,**" said Callow, who had it before she did, and she let him have it. "**Neither crew's work goes on a line with a name on it. Mine and yours. The stone is yours, the shores are the district's, and this morning is two crews standing on a landing doing a job that is not a load on a road.**"

Nella Bray had been at the kitchen-line doorway since the first bell with a slate under her arm, and she came into the room for the first time all week, which is not a thing she has done.

"**That is correct and it is narrow,**" said Nella Bray, in the middle of the floor. "**I am a woman who fills in two lines on a form for a living, and I am telling you that it is correct. And I am telling you it is narrow, because the day one piece of timber goes out of this building on a cart, that is a load on a road, and the form has a line for the person responsible for it, and my hand is the hand that fills it in, and I would like that written down today so that nobody finds out in a month.**"

Dalla Sen entered it in her own words and entered that the day a piece of timber goes out of this building on a cart, the objection that has held for six days stops holding, and that nobody in this room had said that until a woman at a counter said it.

"**Six,**" said Callow. "**The order. It is six words and I am going to say them out loud once and nobody is writing them down and if either crew gets it wrong I will hear it at the check. Lift. Bed. Carry. Set. Seat. And then the check.**"

"**That is not trust,**" said Renn Vale. "**I want it entered that a foreman has just said the word.**"

"**It is not trust, and I am not handing you any,**" said Callow, and picked up his end of the line. "**Nobody in this room gets trust from me on a Friday. That is a rule, and I have worked nine years under rules that were true, and this one is true, and true is what I have been asking for since I came up your steps.**"

Then the stone went.

Four of Callow's five and two of Iven Tallo's took the flag off the flags, set it on a length of the crew's own spent line, and walked it to the head of the nine steps. The asking of four and a half hours covers four of his five and one of ours and half an hour of the woman who reads walls. Everything else in that work was a man being paid nothing by anybody and a man being paid nothing by this boundary, and Dalla Sen wrote both of those down in the space where a reason goes and put nothing between them.

They carried it down nine steps and along the corridor and up onto the common landing, and the corridor is open, and the nine were in the room at the kitchen-line end, and the anteroom was emptied for the length of the carry the way it is emptied for any work, and the terms were said before it was emptied and not after. The count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change, and the outer service door was the one a body went out of, and the fifth of those six terms was in a room with both the people it names in it. A clerk entered that a stone coming out of a floor does not alter a permitted occupancy, that she entered it on purpose, and that she is the ninth person in this building to be careful about that phrase.

At the second bell Renn Vale put her ear against the party wall and was there for the length of a sentence, four times over, and came back and said what she could hear.

"**The same four times in the time it takes to say a sentence,**" she said, "**and I am going to say the second half of it out loud in a room, because I said in this room on Saturday that I would say what I can hear and would not know what it is about and would not pretend to, and this morning the second half is where the two people are.**"

She did not stop.

"**Ilsa Norr is in the room at the kitchen-line end with nine children. The physician is in the kitchen-line doorway and has not moved in an hour and a half. Those are the two of us who can stop me and I am not counting the room, because a room is not standing anywhere at the moment that wall does the thing it is doing. I am telling this room where they are, because that is what I said I would do, and because the man who is not in this room said it to me on a Saturday and nobody has answered her since.**"

Nobody answered it now either.

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, and her voice was not steady and she did not make it steady, "**in the watcher's words, and entered that the room knew, and entered that two people who can stop the woman watching that line were on the other side of nine children at the second bell on the fifth day of the thirty-sixth week while eleven men carried a stone through a corridor, and that nobody in the anteroom did anything about it, because there was nothing anybody in the anteroom could do.**"

Then Jo Verrick spent her half hour, and she spent it the way she spends everything, which is by reading.

"**Before,**" she said, and put her thumb where the thumb goes, on the kitchen-line side of the join. "**A finger. And the string is where the string was on the anteroom side. And the frame is a finger and two-thirds and it was a finger and two-thirds at the first bell.**" She took the thumb away. "**After. A finger. The same. The same on both faces, and I want it entered that I did not put a rule in it and that I did not need to.**"

"**And what it means,**" said Callow, "**in a foreman's words and not a surveyor's. I have to know, because I am going to be asked it on Monday and I would like to be able to answer a person instead of a book.**"

"**It means the stone is taking the load off the head and the head is not being crushed any more today,**" said Jo Verrick, "**and it means I have read a figure on this wall every morning for thirty-five weeks and this morning is the first morning I have read one that was about a thing somebody did.**"

Then Iven Tallo read the joint on the kitchen-line side of nothing — read it with his eyes and with his right hand held an inch off it, the way a man reads a thing he has built and cannot hold.

"**The stone is bedded on the lime, and the lime was the reason the flag rocked,**" said Iven Tallo, from two inches off it. "**I have bedded it on the two stones either side of it and packed it with the clay out of that corner, and in two years that packing will be dust and this stone will have a little movement in it again, and the person who will know is a term in a clerk's book.**"

"**Then write the term,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**No,**" said Iven Tallo, and picked the chisel up off the anvil. "**I said no to that in the thirty-second week and I have not changed and I am not going to be asked a second time in a fortnight by two different people on the same morning.**"

Then Marek Venn said the thing he had been holding since the second bell, out loud, to nobody, in the way a man says a thing in a room when he has decided that saying it to nobody is still saying it.

"**I am not in either book,**" he said. "**My hours are four and a half and they are on one of those two lines and neither line has a heading, and my name is not on the board, and I am not one of the five who checked the other crew's work, because I am not on a crew. Six men and a foreman went down nine steps and along that corridor past nine children this morning, and I stood at the top of the steps and watched every one of them, and I can tell you within two of how many of us that was. I am staying out of it, and I want it entered that the reason I am staying out of it is that an arrangement which let me put my number in would be a better arrangement for this building and a worse one for me.**"

Renn Vale looked at him for a while.

"**It would be a worse one for the arrangement,**" she said, "**and I would like a clerk to hear me say that, because a foreman has spent two days teaching this room that the person who holds a bar cannot see the end of his own timber, and a man who keeps a shut book has been telling this room the same thing for thirty-five weeks and neither of us has ever said so out loud.**"

"**Both of them, in a room,**" said Dalla Sen, "**because I am putting it in the book either way and I would rather have the two sentences said than one of them in a space where a reason goes.**"

"**It is the same sentence,**" said Renn Vale, "**and I have known it since I came back to this building, and I have watched two people not say it, and one of them is me and one of them is standing in this room with a shut book under his arm.**"

Then a woman who reads a wall said nothing about any of that, and went back to a stone, and that was correct of her.

Then the check, and the check was the arrangement working or it was not, and it worked.

"**Your stone,**" Callow said to Iven Tallo. "**My five read it.**"

One of Callow's five put both hands flat on the flag and rocked it once and got a sound out of it that everybody in the anteroom could hear, and said it was bedded, and named the two stones it was bedded on and the head of the sill it was under, out loud, in the room, in his own mouth.

"**My timber,**" said Iven Tallo, to Callow. "**Your foreman reads it.**"

Callow put his thumb where a thumb goes and rocked the head of the shore he had seated a hand's width further along. The timber was seated. The head was bearing. And he could not tell, from where he was standing, whether the sill under it was going to hold, and the only person who could tell him that was a woman who reads walls, and she had already said what she had to say and was not going to be asked twice.

That is the whole of the arrangement and it is in a clerk's book in both crews' words, and it is not on the board, and Tams Quill said so before anybody else said it.

"**The terms between two crews,**" he said, "**and a rule about who checks whose work, and neither of them is a thing in this building, and the third time I have been asked to put one of them on that board is the time somebody puts a person on it.**"

Dalla Sen did not look up. "**You have never been asked,**"

"**I have been asked twice and refused twice,**" said Tams Quill, with the chalk in his left hand, "**and the third one is the one I wanted.**"

The board was walked at the sixth bell and it is at eighteen entries, eleven nulls and a claim, a mark, a row, a plan, a condition, a store and a brace, nine in the ordinary column and nine in the column with nothing over the top of it, and he gave no reason for the column and nobody asked.

At the sixth bell the clerk read the day's figure and the total out loud, both of them, and read them again afterwards because two lines under no heading had been made in a room in the morning and a boundary that reads a total once on a morning like that is a boundary that thinks the day is over. Six today and two hundred and seventy-four since the keeper signed. The boundary is at three hundred and ninety-one and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.

The count was read at one hundred and twenty-five and it is not going to be one hundred and twenty-six. Thirty-four weeks running she has said out loud that it is a number of days and not a number of anything, and she said it again at the sixth bell today, and nobody thanked her, and a clerk who is not thanked goes on saying it. The crew are at seventy-five and a half, up four and a half on a named asking in a room with the terms out loud, Renn Vale named as the person who can be told no and not used, and the three who could not be asked named with the reason for each before the asking, and the reasons are not the reasons of the last five askings.

The pot is four days and the seam gave one on this morning and not two, and the four households at a second gate have nothing, and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody.

The anteroom had a hole in the corner of its floor for the rest of that week and for a long time after, and the nine went round it. The wall on the other side of the room had five and a half shores on one side of it, a brace of a school's own beech on the other, and a line of nothing about two fingers wide on that other side. Six men and a foreman had carried a stone past nine children in a corridor, and nobody in the building could say afterwards how many hours it had taken, and that had been agreed to at the fourth bell by everybody in the room.
