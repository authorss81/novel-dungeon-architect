# Chapter 66 — What the Thaw Took

The frost came in on a Tuesday night and did not go off until Thursday, and on Thursday afternoon the boundary got the second day of the thaw at once, which is the way it does it here: a wet wind off the shaft that came up the nine steps and under the school door and along the corridor in one long push, and the corridor's floor went from white to black in about an hour, and the water that came up with the air came off the walls and off the ceiling and out of the waist.

Renn Vale was on the waist at the fourth bell on Thursday. She holds it four days in seven and Thursday was one of them, and she said the whole of it out loud before she took it, as she has said it every time since the ninth week, and the sentence about the stair came out with the rest of it, and nobody remarked on it because remarking on it had stopped about a month ago.

Renn was there for about two hours with Isel Ferrow and the two of them had a school corridor's worth of people past them at a walk, nine children and their teacher at the head, a household from the clinic return behind, the woman from the wash rooms last with her face in her collar.

And at about twenty past the fifth, with the corridor empty and the water coming off the walls, Renn Vale put two fingers on the third wedge.

She did not shout for anybody. She said one word, into the waist, at nobody, in a voice Isel Ferrow has described eleven times since and always in the same words: "Up."

---

By the time the room was full, there were fourteen people in the anteroom and the anteroom holds ten including one litter for no more than two hours, and Sefu Aran said so, and nobody argued, and six of them went down the nine steps to the school store where they stood in the cold and listened, which is a thing a Hearthhold learns to do.

Renn Vale came up the three steps and did not come into the anteroom, and said her piece from the common landing with the slate under her arm, and Dalla Sen came down the nine steps and wrote it on the bench at the bottom of them in her own hand, which is the only room in that building with a bench at the bottom of the steps and it is not a room anybody sits in.

"Two things," Renn said, "and I am going to say them before I say what I want, because what I want is the easy part and I have had a fortnight of watching this room let the easy part go first.

"**One.** The ground moved again. Along the left side, at the shallowest point, about the width of my hand, over a length of about four foot, and it did not crack and it did not drop and it did not make a noise. It settled. And while I was standing on it I felt it come, and I want to be exact about how I knew, because everybody in this room is going to want a number and there is not a number: **it came up through the sole of my boot.** That is all. That is the whole of the instrument. Eleven weeks in this building and the finest instrument anybody has built for that waist is a person standing on it in a bad boot.

"**Two.** The third wedge has come up."

She put the wedge on the bench. It was a hardwood wedge, one of three, public-works, and it had been driven into the fill at the waist for nine weeks, and it came out of the fill with about a quarter of an inch of daylight under its head.

"A wedge in a fill does not come up because it is tired," she said. "It comes up because the ground under it moved and the wedge is doing the one thing a wedge is for, which is telling you. That is its whole office. It is not a post. It is not a watch. It is a thing that says *this moved*, and I have watched the other two go into that fill in the ninth week and I have watched people look at them like a decoration ever since, and this is the morning somebody in this building found out what they were for, and it is the worst possible morning to find out, and I am not going to be the one who is wrong about it.

"And I am going to say the other thing, and nobody else in this room can say it, and it is not modesty. **I have been standing on moved ground for two hours and I did not put my hand down, and that is a decision I made at the ninth week and I would make it again, and the reason is that a person who puts a hand on that fill cannot feel a slump, and a person with a hand on a fill has made a decision that the ground is fine.**"

---

Ilsa Norr said the thing that the whole of the next week turned on, and she said it standing up, and she had been up since Isel came up the nine steps.

"Then the corridor shuts," she said. "Today. Now. Not Friday. Now, because it has moved twice and I am not going to stand a school corridor over a piece of ground that has moved twice in three days on the argument that it is unlikely to move a third."

"Ilsa —"

"And I want it said, out loud, in this room, by me, before anybody can be kind to me about it, and I want it in the book in the same line as the closure and not in a margin somewhere: **I have lost a day of school and I do not get it back, and there are not eleven more Mondays in this year, and nine children will not have a school this afternoon and will have had one fewer school in their lives.** I said that in the seventh week and I said it in the ninth week and I have said it every time this room has cost me a day, and I am saying it now, and I am not saying it to stop the closure. I am saying it because a day is a day and it should be counted in the room that took it, and not in the room that spent it.

"And now the other half, and it is worse, and I have thought about it for two days and I have not got a better version of it.

"**The corridor shuts and the water stops.** And I know that, because Mikka told the kitchen line on Tuesday and Mikka does not tell the kitchen line things she has not checked. And I want the room to hear it from the woman who cooks and not from a schoolteacher, so: Mikka. Say it."

---

Mikka Hale did not put the cloth down. She held it, and she spoke from the frame, and she had the number ready, which is the thing about Mikka Hale.

"Nineteen," she said. "That has been nineteen since before any of you came up a shaft. It is nineteen days of meals, given and not waived, not down by one, and it is in Tams Quill's arithmetic as well as in my words now because he is right that a thing which is not hours can still be a debt, and I have never once argued with him about it and I am not going to start.

"Here is how the water comes in, and I am going to say it plainly because I have been asked once and I answered badly.

"It does not come from a pipe, because there is no pipe, and it does not come out of the ground, because we have tried and there is nothing there but sump. **It comes up the middle passage in pails, on the shoulders of whoever is coming up the middle passage that week, and it is paid for out of what they are carrying, and it has been done that way for longer than anybody in this room has been alive.** It comes in at the top, it goes down the nine steps, and then it goes through the school door and along the corridor to this kitchen, and it passes two places on the way that are two foot eight wide, and one of them is that waist.

"There. That is the whole of the water in this Hearthhold. It is a set of shoulders and a corridor, and nobody has ever written it down anywhere, and I have just said it out loud in a room for the first time, and I would like Tams Quill to put it in the book in my words with the time on it, because from Saturday it is going to be the most important sentence anybody in this building has ever said."

"Written," said Dalla Sen.

"The corridor shut, and the water does not come in, and the pot does not get filled, and the meals do not get cooked, and the number goes down. It went down on Tuesday afternoon when I saw the fill go and started carrying what was in the trough by hand, and it is **sixteen**, and it will be **fifteen tomorrow**, and I have written both of those figures in my own hand on my own board and I am not going to read a number to this room that is not on that board, and Mikka Hale does not read out a number she has not written down first. That is the whole of my trade and there is not much of it."

"So the waist goes and the water stops," said Marek Venn, from the back, before he had decided to say anything, and the room let him get four words out and then a woman at a frame took the rest of them off him.

"That is two things going," said Jo Verrick, "and you have found the connection in eleven seconds, and I am going to make you earn it before you are allowed to say it in that order, because the connection is not the waist and it is not the water, and if the room writes down *the waist went so the water stopped* then in a year a person will read that as a cause, and it is not a cause, it is two separate things that happen to be standing in the same corridor, and one of them is my fault and one of them is the fill's fault and neither of them is caused by the other.

"**My seat is going.** The check is thin and has been since the Thursday of the tenth week and I cannot hear it in this cold, and when the seat goes the pump stops delivering and the water in that dip comes up. And the dip fills the low end of this corridor, and the low end of this corridor is two foot eight wide and it stands on top of the shaft. And the fill at the waist has gone because water came out of that shaft and turned it to nothing nine weeks ago and I packed it myself with washed-out stuff out of the dip because it was what was there.

"So the water that stops the corridor is the same water that took the fill out of the waist, and I put the fill in, and the pump that holds the dip down has a seat in it with two cleanings on it or fewer, and there is no wood in this Hearthhold to cut another one, and I have been telling this room that since the tenth week and I have not moved the estimate and I am not going to move it now that it has turned out to be the biggest thing I have ever said in a room."

She put her hand on the frame.

"So no. Not *the waist went so the water stopped*. The waist went, and the water is stopping, and a seat is going, and they are three things standing next to each other in a two-foot-eight corridor and I am the only person in the room who has told you twice that they are related."

---

The closure went on the clerk's page in the clerk's hand with a name and a date, because the corridor's closure has a name on it, and the name was Renn Vale's, and the reason was in Renn's own hand and was thirty-one words long, and Dalla Sen read it back twice.

*Slump of washed-out fill at the waist, twice in three days, and the third wedge risen a quarter inch, and I will not have a school corridor over it and the corridor is shut until somebody has packed it and nobody in this building can pack it.*

Sefu Aran said the sentence that turned a bad afternoon into the shape of the week, and he said it from the wheel, and he said it slowly, because he had been turning it over since Thursday morning and it had got worse as it went.

"Right," he said. "Then here is the week, and I am going to put it in the air in one breath and I am not going to be asked about it afterwards, because if I say it in three parts somebody will stop me in the middle and we will end up with the first part.

"**The corridor is shut. The water stops. The school is off. And from Monday morning the stair is the only way anything gets into this building, and the stair is four at a time, four minutes, in the pump's window, with a bearer at the near lip, a plinth reader, a string, and a bell, and it is not for a cart.**"

The anteroom was extremely quiet, and the wheel went round twice under the floor, and everybody heard it.

"Say the number out loud," said Iven Tallo, from the wall, in a voice nobody had heard him use. "Somebody say the number out loud, because I have done it in my head and I have got twenty-eight and I do not want to have done it in my head."

"Say the arithmetic," said Tams Quill, and he had the board out and the chalk in his left hand and his right hand in his lap, and he did the thing in that room that had never failed him in eleven weeks, which was to stop being a man with a hurt hand and become the only reliable instrument in it.

"Fourteen pails a day in this cold," he said. "Four to a crossing. That is four crossings a day. That is twenty-eight crossings a week, and the stair's own record of crossings in a week is **four**, out of the ninth week, when the school went over four and four and the ninth child went in the third crossing with three adults because no child in this building goes on a slab on her own.

"So: **twenty-eight against four.** Seven times. And every one of those twenty-eight has a person from this boundary at the near lip for the whole of the crossing, because the bearer at the near lip is a person of this boundary, and it is named on the day's board, and there is nobody else who can be named.

"And I want to say the other half of it, and I want the room to hear it from the count and not from me. That lip went about two inches on the worst day of Jo Verrick's life, and it is the stone that carries the line-block at its base, and a shore went under it a fortnight ago and took a quarter of an inch off it and has no wedge in it, and it is packed with a knee, and the packing is a person.

"Twenty-eight times a week, a person, a knee, and a quarter of an inch."

He put the chalk down.

"That is not a number of crossings. That is a number of **somebody being under that lip**, and I have not got a column for it, and I am not going to invent one at a quarter past five on a Thursday, and I would like somebody in this room who is better at arithmetic than me to tell me what the arithmetic is."

---

The woman from the wash rooms came to the top of the three steps at about the sixth bell, which is the hour she lifts the covers and which is the hour Mikka Hale's own term says she lifts them and nobody else, and she came down two steps and stopped and said the thing that had been waiting at the back of the room all afternoon for somebody to say it.

"Then it does not go over," she said.

"It has to go over," said Sefu Aran. "The water has to come in or the number goes to nothing."

"Then the number goes to nothing." She said it to the wheel and not to anybody's face, which is how she says everything. "And I will tell you why, and it is not a speech, and it is eleven words and then I am going back up to my covers.

"You are asking me to stand on a string twenty-eight times a week so that a pot can be filled. And I have stood on a string twice and the second time was on a Thursday with a man under that lip, and I have had a month to think about it and a month is not long enough. And there is a cover in a room behind me that I lift at the second bell and the fifth and nobody else, and it does not go on a bell, it goes on me, and if I am on a string at the sixth bell with fourteen pails coming up four at a time then the cover stays where it is for an hour, and a cover staying where it is is not a small thing in this building, and every one of you knows whose mother is under the slab.

"So: no. Not over that stair. Not twenty-eight times a week, not for a pot, and I would say the same if it were nine times a week and I am not saying it because I am frightened of a number, I am saying it because the arithmetic is a pot and a cover, and I have got one and the other is mine."

And she went up the three steps, and to the top of the nine steps, and away, and Mikka Hale did not go after her, because Mikka Hale had arranged in the eighth week that nobody would go after her.

---

Renn Vale said, from the common landing, to nobody: "Then we do it four times in seven."

The room did not understand her for a moment and then all of it did, at once, which is a thing that has happened about four times in eleven weeks in that anteroom and each one of them has been terrible.

"No," said Sefu Aran. "No, Renn — that is your rota. Your rota is four days in seven. It is not the boundary's rota. I am not going to let a keeper's wet-seam position become the building's water timetable and the building's school timetable, that is the exact thing — "

"I know exactly what it is," Renn said. "I have been saying it out loud since the ninth week, in this room, in these words, and you are the man who keeps the gate. **A post is only a post while its condition is happening.** The waist is a post while the corridor is open. On the three days in seven the corridor is shut, the waist is not a post, and on those three days there is nobody on moved ground and no school and no water. On the four days in seven the corridor is open, the school comes, and I am on the waist.

"That is not a rota. That is a **calendar**, and it is the worst thing anybody in this building has ever said out loud, and I have said it, and I have said it in front of Dalla Sen, and it goes in the book in my own words with the time on it, and I would like nobody to come and be kind to me about it for about a week."

She came up the three steps for the first time in an hour and stood on the common landing in front of eleven people.

"And I am not going to do it," she said. "I am going to tell you the shape of it and I am going to tell you what I will do inside it, and then somebody else is going to have to have the other three days.

"**The four days are mine, and I will stand on it, and I will stand on it knowing it moved twice, and I will not put my hand down.** And the three days in seven are not a day of no water. The three days in seven are a day of water that comes up the nine steps and through the school door and into a corridor with a shut waist in it, carried by two people, one at a time, over ground I have said is not a post, at a walk, with pails, on a day the school is not coming.

"So the water comes in four days in seven properly, and on three days in seven somebody carries it in by hand past a piece of ground that has moved. And I will not decide who. **I will not decide who, and I want that in the book as well, because in about ten minutes somebody is going to volunteer and I want it to be on the page that I refused to let them.**"

And then the schoolteacher said the thing that nobody had thought to say, and she said it standing on the common landing at the top of three steps, which is where she stands, and Dalla Sen came up the nine steps and wrote it on the bench at the bottom of them in her own hand.

"Then stop," Ilsa Norr said. "All of it. Stop, and listen to me, because I have been in this room for an hour and a half saying *my day* and I have not once said *my week*, and my week is the thing I keep, and you have just taken it off me without asking me, and I am not going to let the week go the way the day went.

"**The school used to run when I said it ran.** Not on a rota. Not on anybody's boots. On me. Nine children and me, and the floor I paid for, and the days I have gone without. And since about ten minutes ago it has run four days in seven, and the four days are Renn Vale's days and not mine, because a woman with a bad boot is standing on a piece of ground.

"And I am not refusing, and I want that in the same breath, because I have refused four days in this building and I am not refusing a fifth. I am going to run it four days in seven on Renn's days, and I am going to lose three days a week, and in a month that is a fortnight of school and in four months that is the whole spring.

"And I want three things in the book in my words, and I want them at the fifth bell and not in a margin.

"One: **the school now runs on a rota that is not mine, and I have agreed to it, and nobody asked me and I am not going to pretend anybody did.** Two: on the three days in seven that it does not run, there is nobody in the school store, and nobody in the school, and nobody standing under that stair who does not have a reason to be there, and I would like somebody in this room to say out loud what those walls are doing on those three days, because I have asked it before and I was told it was a note. Three, and this is the one I actually want: **the school running on Renn Vale's days is the first thing in eleven weeks that this boundary has put on a person's body because a person was the only instrument, and I have just watched six people agree to it in under a minute, and I would like somebody to notice what that was.**"

"Wrote it," said Dalla Sen, from the bench at the bottom of the nine steps, in her own hand, "in her words, and read out, and I am adding in my own hand that the schoolteacher has asked for the three items to be read at the fifth bell and not left in a margin, and that in eleven weeks this is the first entry in that book that a person asked to have her own cost read out on a schedule rather than once."
