# Chapter 28 — The Empty Upper Wing

They went up the fixed stair in the morning, all of them, and the eleventh tread still had its hollow note, and nobody stood on it twice.

The party stopped at the stair head, which was the correct place for it to stop, and which was itself a small argument about the room: Leena's litter came up and set down on the landing with Oren and Tams carrying, because the turn at the top was a quarter-turn and a shoulder could not take one; Pell came up on his crutch as far as the landing and no further, and from there he read the pale floor paths through the doorway and said, without being asked, that the deepest one went to the wall and not to the shelf, and that a person who had gone to that wall a hundred years ago was probably a person who had been sent to fetch something and had taken a long time about it.

Jo went first with a wedge and a mallet and the small file that was still in the basket, because the deck boards over the third post had to come up and the boards were nailed to the joists with square-cut nails driven at an angle by somebody who had had a hundred years to be careless about it. Tams ran the hand line. Oren took the weight at the stair head and did nothing else, because his whole job that morning was to be a wall.

Iven worked opposite her.

"Under the fourth board," he said. "Not the fifth. There is a joist over the fourth."

"There is a joist over the fifth too."

"There is a joist over the fifth that is not over anything you are removing. Leave it." He set his mallet down. "And when the board comes up, put it flat on the joist. Not on the floor. If the floor is stone and the joist is wood, a board laid on stone is a board with a stone under it, and the next person will lift it wrong."

It took the better part of a morning to lift nine boards and lay them flat. The nails came out in pieces. The timber underneath was dark and dry and, in three places, furred with a grey growth that smelled faintly of a cellar. Jo showed it to Iven without a word, and Iven showed it to Marek without a word, and Marek wrote *dry rot at three bays, cause not determined* in the record and did not offer an opinion, because he had no opinion and the growth was not his to explain.

The hole in the deck was what made the room legible.

From the stair head you could see the four posts and the kerb they stood in, and the kerb was open on the corridor side where the post foot had come up, and the stone of the low parapet above it was gone, and through the gap the light came up off the corridor floor eleven feet below in a long pale bar. Two shoring lengths ran under the third post, wedge-driven, packed by a woman with a sore wrist who had taken the work herself and had been paid in nothing yet.

"No one goes over that bar," Jo said.

"No one," Sefu agreed, and he had come up, which he did not often do, and stood at the head of the stair with his hand on the frame.

The barricade went across the stair head at the ninth tread, because a barrier at the head is a thing people step over, and a barrier nine treads down is a thing they have to go round, and the object of the barrier was to make the room absent rather than merely forbidden.

Iven wanted to keep a brace.

"Say the whole thing," Marek said, because he could hear it arriving and wanted it in the open.

"Keep the two shoring lengths," Iven said. "Set them properly. Head them off into the corridor's own wall, cap them with the last of Jo's wedges, and mark them so a person in five years can see they are holding a post and not holding a wall. Not a repair. A known condition, held deliberately, with the reason written on it."

"And the corridor keeps two of its three feet," Oren said, "and if the head ever comes down again, it comes down onto the shoring."

"That is what shoring is for."

"That is what shoring is for," Oren agreed, "and you have never once in your life left a brace standing where somebody could lean on it, and neither have I, and neither will whoever stands here in winter." He tapped the wall. "Take them out. Take them out and put them under the school door, where you will still be able to see them."

Dalla had the opposite argument, and it was not about timber.

"The removal has to be in the record as a removal," she said, "with a date, a reason, and the names of the people who did it. Not as salvage. Not as recovery. There is a guild lien on this building that claims priority in identified material, and I am not going to have the first thing that ever comes up out of that deck described in a hearing as recovered stock."

"It is not recovered stock," Iven said. "It is a floor."

"It is a floor that came out of a hole," Dalla said, "and the difference is the words. Write *removed for safety, not recovered, not claimed, held by nobody, deposited where the people who took it out can point to it* — and then write the reason in the same hand, and then write who signed."

So the boards were laid flat on the joists, and where they went afterward was decided then and there and entered in the ledger as a line with a name on it rather than a line with a pile on it.

And then Marek did the thing he had been building toward since the chalk line broke, and it took him the whole morning to get to it, and he got there in a voice that sounded entirely reasonable.

"There should be something left," he said.

Nobody answered, because nothing followed it, and he had to go on and finish it himself.

"There should be a bench and a length of floor and the four hooks, up here, with the register boxes on the shelf, and a person who comes up on a Thursday to sit and read. Not a room. A place. Three feet of it, held, watched, named. Because a room that ends with nothing in it gets filled by somebody in twenty years, and the somebody will not ask, and it will not be a school floor. It will be a store, and then it will be something else, and I have seen that happen to four buildings on the surface and I am not going to watch it happen to a school."

Jo set down her mallet.

"Marek," she said, "that is the most dangerous thing you have said since we got here, and you said it in a reasonable voice."

"I know what it is—"

"You do not, because you have already worked out the cost and I can see you working it out. Three feet. A watch. A name. And then the watch is a person on a Thursday, and the person on a Thursday is a shift, and the shift is a rota, and the rota is a room, and the room is a floor, and you will have got a public room built out of the wreck of the one you promised us would not be built. That is how a building gets back. It is not by being kept. It is by being given a use and a person and no way to say no."

Ilsa said the rest of it, and she said it from the stair head, holding the slate.

"You are asking me to let a stranger decide what my room is for," she said. "You will not do it by taking it. You will do it by leaving four feet of it, and I will have to say no to the four feet, and then I will have said no twice, and every school that comes after mine will hear that a room can be argued into shape one piece at a time. I have refused you the boxes. I refused you the deck. I will refuse you the four feet, and I would like it written that I refused all three in the same week and that nobody has a different story about it."

Marek stood on a school floor with a hole in it and felt the old thing come up in him, which was the desire to be the person who decided, on the grounds that he was the person who could see the failure coming. He had watched a room fail because thirty people moved the way thirty people move, and he had been right about that, and being right had felt, for a week, like a claim.

"That is fair," he said. "I withdraw it. Take the four feet."

"Recorded," Dalla said.

The register boxes came down in the afternoon, on the hand line, and Renn carried them herself and would not let anyone else touch them, and they went into the school corner's own store under a name and a lock and a line in the ledger, and forty-one years of a school that had been keeping records about itself moved out of a room that was about to stop being anything at all.

The sound decking went to Jo, and that was the only good thing in the week, and it was good by accident.

"Twelve sound boards," she said, going through them on the anteroom floor. "Two with dry rot, four with nothing wrong, six that will do for a shutter. That is my shutter route. That is the route open again, and the hinges on two doors that have been catching since the collapse." She looked up. "The gallery paid for my shutters."

"It bought you back a shutter route," Iven said. "It did not pay for it. Nobody who loses something in one place has paid for something in another."

The last thing was the gallery floor itself, and nobody had a name for what to do with it, so Iven named it for them, in the only way that was available: he wrote *school gallery, upper east stair, unoccupied, no route, no air, no occupancy* on a slate the size of a name slate, and Jo fixed the slate to the barricade plank at the ninth tread with a square-cut nail, and Renn added beneath it, in her own hand, the four dates that mattered: when the school stopped using the room, when the register stopped, when the last class sat there, and the day the crew closed it.

Sefu put his mark beside that, and only that, because his mark on the slate said that the room was shut and not that it was safe and not that the stair was fixed and not that anyone should come up and look.

Downstairs, the maintenance consequence was already on somebody's list, because the standing stock had gone into a hole in a floor.

The kitchen brace that the six-piece stock had been committed to in the first place was deferred again, and now there was nothing to defer it with. The clinic sill, which was the reason two of the six lengths had been spoken for, was on a list that nobody could now shorten. The school-door lintel was still a cracked head over a two-and-a-half-foot gap, and the two shoring lengths came out from under the gallery post and went under it instead, and Jo said that was the correct place for them and that it was also the end of the timber. And Tams, doing the honest arithmetic at the end of the day, put the number on the board: *stores: no sound timber over six feet; no bronze seat; wedges: nine; lath: none.*

Nine wedges. In a building where a bench, a shutter, a brace, and a lintel all wanted to be made at once.

The barrier went across at dusk, and the anteroom got its ten back, and the pump came off the lever and the corridor emptied and the kitchen line carried wash water past a chalk figure on the floor that told every person their own width, and above them, on a stair nobody was going up, a slate said four dates and a name and the word *no*.
