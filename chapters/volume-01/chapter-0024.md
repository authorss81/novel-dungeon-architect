# Chapter 24 — Before the Test

The first thing the test could not have was time, and the reason was a bronze lip.

Jo worked the retaining bolt loose at the sixth stroke of the interval before the door, while Tams held the lever and Oren took the frame. Pell called the water from the anteroom bench with his slate on his knees, and his ankle on the folded blanket, and he called it before anyone had thought to give him the job, which Jo noticed and said nothing about. The sediment came away in a grey fan and the seated bronze showed its scrape, dull and bright at the same time, and Jo wiped the seat and put the wrench down without being asked.

"That is the third one," she said, from the floor with her knees apart and the flat file in her hand.

"Third cleaning," Dalla said, writing.

"Third half-day." Jo sat back on her heels. "The kitchen brace, the wash-room shutter, and the hinges on the two doors I have been putting off since the collapse. Half a day each. That is a day and a half of my route, and I have not done any of it, and I am not going to be able to do it before this test either because the test is on a Thursday and the route is a Thursday."

"Write it as a Thursday," Dalla said, and wrote it as a Thursday.

Marek had come to the preparation with a forty-minute occupancy in his head and lost it in the first ten minutes, in the plainest possible way.

The pump could not run continuously. It had not been able to since the seat was scored, and nobody in the room was still pretending otherwise. Tams Quill worked it out on the case board with a bit of charcoal, because the arithmetic was not really his to decide but the arithmetic was his to do.

"Twenty-six strokes to the first rest, then six minutes off the lever, then nineteen more," he said. "The water comes back up in the six minutes. It comes back up faster at the end than at the beginning, because the seam is wetter than it was." He looked at the wet mineral seam, which was shining along the edge of the service well like something that had been recently wiped. "So call it forty minutes and you have thirty-four of pump in it. Call it an hour and you have a lie."

"Thirty-four minutes of pump inside a forty-minute occupancy," Marek said. "With the water rising in the gap. With the seam feeding the well the whole time."

"And with one of the five watches on the well the whole time," Nera said.

The arithmetic was not the problem. The problem was that the whole shape of the test had changed while the crew was still carrying baskets.

They worked through the preparation for two days, and the two days were mostly logistics.

Iven taught, and the teaching was the part of the preparation that mattered most and that nobody would have thought to plan.

He took Jo, and then Jo took Mikka, and then Mikka took Ilsa, in three steps, and in each step the work got smaller and the words got plainer until it was a woman holding a waxed line against a stone while somebody at the far end called a number, which is not craft at all, and is the only part of craft that survives a person being tired.

"Plumb," Iven said, holding the weighted stone. "It tells you whether a wall is standing straight. It does not tell you whether the wall is all right. Nobody has ever confused those, except people who did not have to live behind the wall."

"Twelve courses up and four across," Jo said, reading her slate. "Run it twice and take the worse answer."

"Run it twice," Iven agreed. "Once is a reading. Twice is a check."

He never once said the sequence was his. When Dalla wrote the whole thing into the ledger she put *taught by Iven Tallo; carried out by Jo Verrick, Mikka Hale, Ilsa Norr* and Iven read it and asked for one word to be changed to *translated by*, which Dalla did, and then he asked for the word translated to be changed to *set down by*, and Dalla looked at him for a moment and changed it again, and Iven said that was better and left the bench.

Dalla recorded labor the entire time, in columns, and the columns were the argument of the week taking shape. She had a column for the task, a column for the person, a column for the time, and a column she headed *owed*, and she was ruthless about it. By the second afternoon the sheet said that Jo Verrick had given four half-days of tool-route work, that Iven Tallo had given nineteen hours of uncompensated craft instruction given while his bond is suspended, that Mikka Hale had given eleven hours of kitchen and recovery support, that Renn Vale had given six hours of door and landing watch, and that Dalla Sen had given her own record-keeping, which she entered as a cost and refused to let anyone call a duty.

Renn saw that line and said, "That is not labor. That is a room with a book in it."

"It is labor," Dalla said. "You would have had to keep a record anyway. The point is that if the room needs a record, the room needs somebody for it, and that somebody has to be paid or acknowledged or refused. You cannot have three and pretend it is free."

Mikka brought the meals and made the argument she had been building toward all afternoon, in the voice she used for a pot that had boiled over.

"Here is what a full room is," she said. "It is a pot that boils over while nine people are on a floor above it. It is a child who has to leave in the middle. It is wash water for a clinic that has to be carried past nine people who are sitting down. It is me, in the kitchen, doing two jobs because the corridor has nine bodies in it who were supposed to be somewhere else."

She set the tally board on the bench.

"Say it in the test. If nobody says it, the test will pass and the corridor will fail on a Thursday, and some Thursday somebody will remember this week and say we never asked."

So the test acquired a term nobody had planned for. Mikka called it an *interruption*, and the rule that went into the plan was this: an interruption is not a failure and it is not a pause. The test clock does not stop. Dalla writes down who left, when, and how many bodies remain, and the room has to hold the changed number rather than the declared one. Because that was the truth nobody had written yet, which was that a room's declared occupancy is a photograph and a room's real occupancy is a Tuesday.

And then there was the last ordinary thing, and it was the one that broke the plan most cleanly.

"Where do they go?" Mikka said. "Thirty people on a floor for forty minutes. Two of them are going to have to leave the room, and they are not going to be able to leave the building, and the clinic wash door is the only door on this side of the corridor that a person can use. I have four people who can go through it, and it is the same door the clinic uses for a sick person on a cot, and those two things are not going to happen in the same minute."

"I'll man the door," Jo said.

"You are on the waist with the load marks," Dalla said.

"Then the test has one fewer body on the corridor than it needs." Marek had the sketch out on a board, drawn with his left hand in a line that wandered slightly and meant exactly what it said. "Say that in the terms."

Ilsa's refusal came later in the same afternoon, and it came without heat, which made it harder to argue with.

"My nine do not go on that floor," she said.

"Understood," Marek said. "It was never going to be—"

"I know it was not. I want it written that I was asked and said no, so that when somebody in a year wants the gallery for a shelter and a class is scheduled, they find out that the teacher refused once and why." She held the slate against her knee. "I will go up. I will stand where I stand and I will call the class pattern, because a class moves together and thirty adults standing still are not a class. But no child goes on a floor that has not passed."

"Then the load is imitation," Nera said. "Say that in the terms too. Thirty adults who do not behave like children are a different room from nine children and twenty-one adults."

"It is what we can honestly test," Marek said, and the sentence was the least impressive he had ever spoken in that room, and it was the true one.

The declared test was written up in lines, and it took a morning and an afternoon, and every line had somebody in the room standing behind it.

Dalla read each line back before she wrote the next one, which was slower, and nobody had asked for it.

"Occupancy: thirty on the upper deck, six in the lower corridor including one litter on a frame, and the five named watches at the boundary. No child on the deck. Duration: forty minutes."

"Add the litter's four minutes each way," Leena said. "Four up the fourteen treads and four down through that door. Anybody who counts this test in minutes is counting half of it."

Dalla wrote it into the line, and then read the line back.

"Gear: one hundred and thirty-two kilograms of load on the deck in six sacks, thirty-eight kilos in the corridor in four baskets. Stretcher route: the fixed flight of fourteen treads with a quarter-turn at the head, then the corridor, then the waist, then the school door. Exits: the fixed flight and the school door."

"Write the anteroom out of it again," Nera said, "and write *may not be used* next to it. The anteroom is the only way out of this building for a person who is not walking, and somebody in a hurry is going to read that board and not this room."

So Dalla wrote *the anteroom route is not part of this test and may not be used by anybody inside it*, and read the whole sentence back twice before she let herself go on.

"Stop authority: Nera for air, Sefu for the boundary and the occupancy, and any person inside may stop the test for the reason they can state in one sentence, with no requirement to prove it first."

"Who overrules it?" Jo said. "Because a stop with a reason on it is a stop, and if anybody can overrule a stop in the moment then it is a suggestion with a good excuse attached."

"Nobody overrules it in the moment," Nera said. "The corridor empties, the room empties, and the argument happens afterwards. That is the only version of a stop I will stand behind."

Dalla wrote that one in her own hand, which meant it could not be argued back out of the room later.

And there was one line for the case nobody had a procedure for, which Dalla had found by sitting with it for an afternoon:

*If anyone from the deck reaches the lower corridor before the litter, the corridor party holds at the waist and does not move past it. The deck party holds at the stair foot. Dalla's bell is the signal for either side. Neither side argues with the other until both are still.*

Mikka added her own condition and made Dalla write it in her own words, which was that the test would stop the moment the kitchen's pot was actually needed, and not at the point where somebody thought it might be.

And the crew, the guild, and the standing instruments sat behind all of it without being argued about, because nobody in the room had anything to say about them. The inspection incline was shut behind a bar. Dael Harrow's route-custody order was on its own board, where it could not be confused with a pump. Iven's suspended-bond receipt was folded beside it. Form 7-B was still unsigned in a Marrowgate office that had not been told any of this, and the six of them were going to sit in a room full of Hearthhold residents on Thursday and let a floor be judged by people who had no legal claim on it and no legal protection from it, which was the particular condition they had not managed to escape in six weeks.

The timber came out of the stores on Tuesday, and it was harder than the test.

The splice needed three lengths and the deck spread needed the lath, and both had to be released by people who had promised them to something else. Jo went with Dalla to the boundary stores and counted the standing stock onto the anteroom floor, six pieces, and stood over it with her hands on her hips while Dalla read out what each one had been spoken for.

"Two for the clinic sill," Dalla said. "Two for the kitchen brace. Two spare for whatever came next, which has been the answer since the winter started."

Then she looked at the two she had not named for a moment longer than the rest of it took.

"And I am going to write down what *spare* means, because in a week one of you is going to read this page and count six. A spare is not a release. Those two are the whole of the reserve, and the reserve is for the next thing that is already broken, not for a thing we have decided to build. If the next thing is already broken this afternoon, then the splice does not happen this week, and we have that conversation instead of this one."

"Release three for a splice we cannot inspect, and we have a floor and no door sill," Jo said. "Release two more and we have a floor, a door sill that is a gap, and no brace. The brace is holding the hearth corner back. Take it out and the corner has to be a curve, and a curve means the pot moves."

Mikka made the call from the kitchen line, and she made it fast, which is how she made most of them. "Take the four. The sill can wait; a sill is a nuisance and a gap is a way in. The brace waits with it, and the corner goes on being a curve, and I will carry the pot round it and stop complaining about it in public."

Sefu, who had not been asked, said the thing that made it a decision rather than a favor. "That releases four pieces of the Hearthhold standing stock to a project the crew proposed, and it leaves two in the reserve that nobody has opened. Write it as the Hearthholds' decision and not as a contribution, because in a year somebody will read a contribution as a claim."

Dalla wrote it as the Hearthholds' decision, in four lines, with the two spares entered as held and not released. Ilsa then asked for two school days to move nine children into the kitchen corner, which Dalla recorded as *school days, given, at the school's cost, not waived*, and which Mikka did not contest, because the school corner was hers and the kitchen corner was Mikka's and the arrangement was between the two of them and not about the crew at all.

The loads went up on Wednesday afternoon and nobody carried a basket they were not named for.

Oren and Tams made the deck loads out of stone, in six sacks of twenty-two kilos, and hauled them up the fixed stair in single file with a hand line because there was no other way to get twenty-two kilos up fourteen treads. Tams counted the sacks and the count went into the ledger. Oren set them where a class would sit, in two rows and an aisle, because the aisle mattered: a room with an aisle is not the same room as a room without one, and Marek had spent a career learning that on drawings and had never once learned it on a floor.

Jo carried the corridor loads herself, thirty-eight kilos in four baskets, and put them along the wall out of the dip, because water in the dip was the one variable nobody could argue about.

Mikka counted the kitchen corner's floor space nine times that afternoon, because nine children on a kitchen floor meant the meal queue could not pass, and the meal queue could not pass on Wednesday either, and the only answer was to move the queue and eat later. That was Mikka's cost and Mikka did not itemize it, which Dalla allowed, because a woman may choose what a thing costs her.

Leena's litter went to the test by her own instruction, and Oren and Tams carried it up the fourteen treads and down again to see whether it could be done before the day it had to be done. It could. It took four minutes, and Leena said the number out loud at both ends so that it would be in the record, and she was the only person in the room who understood that a four-minute litter move is a fact about people and not about stone.

On Wednesday night Dalla read the whole plan aloud in the anteroom, which nobody had asked her to do and which she had decided to do anyway, and the reading took eleven minutes, and at the end of it she asked the room whether anyone present was refusing to be in the test.

The silence went on long enough to be its own answer.

Renn spoke from the landing, where she was not counted and had said so. "I will man the door. I will not stand on a deck and be told it is safe. I have watched that room for forty years and I have never seen it hold anybody. If you want me to say I believe it, you will have to bring me back after and let me look."

Dalla read that back and wrote it down, and Marek did not argue, because the sentence was not a refusal of the test. It was a refusal of a later and larger promise, made in advance, by someone who had earned the right to make it.
