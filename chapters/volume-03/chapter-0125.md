# Chapter 125 — The Word That Is Not Said

**Open for the corridor on the fifth day of the twenty-fourth week, a Friday, and the school ran in the room at the kitchen-line end for the twelfth morning, and the crossing took nine minutes and fifty seconds in two parties and a fifth at a walk, and the ninth of the nine came through last with an adult either side of her because no child in this building goes on a slab on her own, and the sill under that door had been packed in stone and clay on the Thursday by two people and a woman with a rule on her knee, and the whole of it took the best part of a day and is the only thing anybody in this Hearthhold has been able to make in five weeks.**

**The room was warm all morning. That is the fact Jo Verrick put her hand on the floor for at the first bell and said out loud, and it is the fact the whole of the next day turns on.**

"**It is warmer in here than it is in the corridor and it is warmer than it was on Monday, and I have put my hand on the low corner where the four threads were on Monday and there is nothing coming up, and the joint under that sill is packed and the packing has taken the water, and I have got no business being pleased about it and I am going to be pleased about it in a room.**

"**And the floor is drying. At two places. And not the middle. And a hair is not a measurement and I have said that in this room more than once.**"

**Then the day went the way a Friday goes, and at the fourth bell the boundary did the thing it has done on a Friday for five weeks, which is count everybody at both ends, and this Friday the count at both ends came out at fourteen on one side of a low end of corridor and eleven behind a door, and nobody in this room had to guess either of them.**

Dalla Sen had the chalk up at the kitchen-line end, in the corridor, with the schoolroom door open behind her and fourteen people standing in a corridor two foot eight wide, and she read it the way she reads everything, which is twice.

"**Fourteen. At the kitchen line, the hearth corner, the clinic door and the low end, at the fourth bell, on a Friday: me, Jo, the fourth pair of hands at the pump, the cook, the boy who counts the pails, Dalla—**"

"**You are in it,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**I am in it,**" she said, and went on, "**Bel, the air engineer, Renn, Isel, Ruk, and four who carry pails and are not named because nobody has ever asked their names and they are not on anything and I am not going to start. That is fourteen and I have counted bodies and not buckets and I counted twice and the second time was the same.**"

"**And behind that door,**" said Dalla Sen, and she turned round, "**eleven. Nine of them between six and eleven, their teacher, and the cook, who went in at the second bell to clear the hearth corner and has been in there since and is not coming out for me.**"

Mikka Hale came out of the doorway with her sleeves up and her hands black to the wrist and said, over the top of the count, in the middle of it, without waiting to be asked:

"**It is not packed. The hearth is not packed and the fire is not lit and it is not going to be lit, and the reason is that I have decided, and I said on Monday in a room that I would not be asked to make an exception, and nobody has asked me, and so the hearth is cold and it is going to stay cold until somebody with a job in that corner says otherwise, and that is me, and I am not going to be in that room at night with a fire in it.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that she said it in the middle of a count and not before it, and I am not going to move it to before the count, because that is where she said it.**"

---

**The change was written next, in a schoolteacher's hand, in a corridor, and a door was shut on eleven people by the person who keeps it, and it was not wedged, and the reason it was not wedged was given out loud in front of everybody.**

Ilsa Norr wrote it on the back of a day-board because there is no other paper in this Hearthhold, and she wrote it in her own hand, and she read it out, and Dalla Sen read her out against it, and Renn Vale came down one step and said the clause was right and that the word was not *shut* but *closed*, and it was struck in the teacher's hand and *closed* written over it.

"**Say it out loud in a room,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and not off the board, because a thing written on a board in a corridor is a thing said and a thing read back is a thing a clerk has.**"

"**The room at the kitchen-line end is closed with the room in use when it was closed. Eleven were in it. The sill is packed in stone and clay by two people, and it is opened when the clay has stopped shrinking, and the two people who laid it have to be in a room together and both of them have to say it. The condition is on Renn Vale's slate in her hand and there is no number on it and there is no day on it, and I can stop it, and Nera Oduya can stop it, and the two of us can be stopped by each other.**"

"**And you do not want it wedged,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and I am going to make you say that out loud, because I have got a page in my coat that is about doors and there is a line in it that somebody in a year is going to read.**"

"**I do not want it wedged,**" said Ilsa Norr. "**A wedged door is a door a person can open with a shoulder, and there is a line in this building's own book from five weeks ago that says the answer to a man with his shoulder to a door is a man and not a mason. If that door is wedged then somebody in this building will open it with a shoulder, because that is what a wedge is for, and I am asking for a door that is shut and not forced, so that nobody in here can open it by being strong, so that the only way out of that room until the clay stops is somebody deciding to.**

"**And I know what I have just said. I have said that I want the only way out of a room with nine children in it to be a decision. And I have said it on a Friday afternoon in a corridor with the school in the room and I would like the clerk to have the chalk out, because I am not going to be allowed to un-say it and I am not going to want to.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**in the teacher's words, and entered that a room in this building now has a door that cannot be forced and a condition that has to be met by two named people in a room together, and entered that I am the clerk and I have written both of those down and I am not going to say anything about them.**

"**And I am going to say one thing in my own words and I am not going to improve it afterwards. For five weeks I have written down numbers that people say out loud in front of me and I have never once written down a word that is a way out of a room. I have just written one. I would like somebody to notice that and I would like nobody to say anything about it.**"

Somebody noticed. Bel Ordry looked at her for a second and then at the slate and said, "**Then read it out, and read it out with the strike, and I will tell you which week it was.**"

---

**And the second way out of that room is a word, and it is a word said in a room by the person who is going to be in the room, and the clerk repeats it, and nobody says it to a doorframe, and on the fifth day of the twenty-fourth week it was said eleven times in a corridor by eleven people, and the room that was closed behind them had a second way out, and it was that.**

Renn Vale held it. She stood on the corridor side of that door in a corridor two foot eight wide over eleven feet of shaft with a lamp behind her and the pump running, and she said the whole of it, and nobody filled the space after it.

"**A room in this building has a second way out and the second way out is a word. The word is said in a room, by the person who is going to be in the room, and the clerk repeats it, and nobody says it to a door. That is five weeks old and it is the ugliest rule anybody in this Hearthhold has ever made and it is the only thing standing between that room and being a container, and I am not going to pretend it is good, and I am also not going to be the one who quietly does not do it.

"**So: every person who is going to be in that room tonight says the word. In this corridor, now, out loud, and the clerk repeats it. And I am going to ask the nine of them, because the rule says the person who is going to be in the room, and it is not going to be me, and it is not going to be the teacher, and it is not going to be the cook.

"**And I am telling you now what is going to happen, so that it is in a book in two hands before it happens and not in a memory afterwards: nine children are going to say a word in a corridor that they have never been asked for and do not understand, and the clerk is going to repeat it, and it is going to be the second way out of a room with the door shut and the sill packed, and it will not open anything.**

"**And I am going to do it anyway, because the alternative is that I say it, and the rule is the rule, and a rule that is bent the first time it is inconvenient is the guild's counter-draft with better manners.**"

They said it. **Orla Kern, the fourth of the nine, said it first and said it wrong, and Dalla Sen said it again and Orla said it again and got it.** Two of the smaller ones said it together and the clerk said it twice. The two biggest said it separately and one of them said it into the door and the clerk had to ask her to say it out loud. The teacher said it and her voice did what a teacher's voice does at the end of a Friday. Mikka Hale said it last, and said it the way she says things on a board at the fifth bell, flat, and did not look at anybody.

**Ilsa Norr asked the question of the ninth, at the end, on her own account and not asked to, and it is in the book in her words.**

"**Then what is it for,**" said the fourth of the nine. "**In case what.**"

"**In case,**" said Ilsa Norr.

"**Say the rest of that,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**I am going to say *in case*,**" she said, "**and I am not going to say the rest of it, and I am not going to lie to her either, and the two of those are the same thing tonight and I have known that since Wednesday and I did it anyway.**"

---

**And the door was closed at the fifth bell by the woman who had written that it would be, and it was closed with a hand and not a shoulder, and eleven people were behind it, and the number the page says and the number in the room are the same number, and they were said out loud in a corridor in that order and nobody joined them.**

Then the corridor went on being a corridor. The boundary read the six things, and Dalla Sen read the day's number out, which was three, and the total since the keeper signed, which was ninety, and the boundary stood at a hundred and seven and a quarter, and the crew stood at fifty-six and a half, and the pot was ten and had not gone up, and a man of twenty-two was sixty days and sixty nights into a count that was his own and was at the fourth step with a tally in his hand and had been counted twice that afternoon and had not been asked for anything and had offered himself to a sill on Monday and been told to go to bed.

**Not one person in this building slept in the anteroom worrying about it. That is not a thing this building does. The pump ran attended from the second bell to the fifth and would run again at the sixth. The low end of the corridor stood in water for six minutes after the lever came off and then went down. Nine children, a schoolteacher and a cook went to sleep in a room with a packed sill, a shut door, a cold hearth and nine hearth stones, and one of the nine hearth stones had a hole the width of two fingers in the bed of it at its low side, and the ninth of the nine slept on it because it was the warm place and it had been the warm place for five weeks.**

**All of it was correct, and the survey had been right, and the packing was a good repair, and the word had been said eleven times in a corridor by the eleven people it was for, and the boundary's own plan did not have a row for that room on it, and the boundary's own charter had a hole in it one institution wider than it had been a month ago, and a title was in a field book four days up the middle passage, and a school corner stood open at the far end of a corridor with a dry floor in it and nine nobody in it, and a man with a left hand that does not close sat at the foot of nine steps and listened to the anteroom's count come up through the floor in a sound like a thumb run along the edge of a plate.**

**He had been right. He had said on Wednesday that he would be right and he was right, and a woman had told him at the same time in front of twelve people that he was going to lose the next one, and on Friday at the fifth bell the room was shut and the sill was packed and eleven people were behind a door, and the second way out of that room was a word that nine children had said in a corridor and that would open nothing at all.**

Marek Venn wrote one line in his book of his own that night and it is the second line in it in a month, and it says: *a thing can be safe and shut and correct and have eleven people behind it, and the only instrument that says so is a word eleven children said in a corridor, and the word is the second way out, and the second way out is the only way out.*

**He did not write the other thing, and the other thing was this. He had been in this building twenty-five weeks and he had built a great many things that were better than the thing they were, and he had never once before written down the shape of the better thing while he was still holding it. A sill packed in clay in a joint nine inches deep is better than a room that is quietly getting wet, and a room that is quietly getting wet is what this building would have had on the Saturday morning, and eleven people would have been standing in it, and the ninth hearth stone in it has a hole in the bed of it that Jo Verrick put a thumb into on Monday and that four people then packed a sill over instead of looking at.

**He had been right. He was going to be right about the rest of it as well, on the water, on the corridor, on the low end, on the joint, for as long as there was a room in this building with a floor in it, and being right was going to be the shape of his whole life here, and a man who is right about every wall in a building has stopped being a man who can be argued with about a floor.**
