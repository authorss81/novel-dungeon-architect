# Chapter 31 — The Third Work-Bell

The turn came at the second bell, and the review came at the fourth, and the order of those two things was the whole argument.

Marek had wanted them the other way round. He wanted the review in the flat quiet after a turn, when everybody was sitting down and the cloth had hung still for an hour, so that the page could be read in a room that agreed with itself. Instead the turn had taken the morning and the review got the afternoon, and the room was tired in the specific way rooms are tired when they have just emptied and refilled twice.

The turn was the ordinary one now. The wheel turned under the floor, the cloth above the valve lifted and fell, and five people went to five posts without a word being said: Nera to the valve with her hand already on the stop handle, Sefu to the civic wheel, Renn to the landing bracket with the enclosed lantern, Mikka out through the clinic gate, Dalla to the threshold with the bell rope in one hand and her ledger shut under her arm. The anteroom emptied to the outer step, the litter first, Oren and Tams carrying and not touching her right arm. Four minutes. The cloth settled. They came back in.

Nobody wrote it down. That was the point of the fifth week of it.

"Read it," Sefu said.

Dalla had the interim page on the bench, folded to the line they had been arguing about for two days, and she did not open it.

"I am not reading it," she said.

"Since when."

"Since the last time we read it. That was nine days ago and it was a decision, and a decision that gets read again becomes a habit, and a habit is not a decision." She put two fingers on the fold. "Jo holds this."

Jo came in from the grate with her hands black to the wrist and took the page, and did not open it either, which was a thing Marek had never seen her do.

"Right," she said. "Then we start with the parts that are wrong."

She read it to herself, silently, all the way down, and then went back three lines.

"The nine," she said. "The corridor may be used for nine people including one litter, only while the pump lever is moving." She looked up. "Nine was a test number. It came out of a test, with ninety kilos on boards and a fouled litter and a child who wanted to see the grate, and it was never a number anybody was supposed to live by. It is on a page now and it has been on it nine days, and on the ninth day a number stops being a test and starts being a capacity."

"It is a limit," Marek said. "A limit is the safe direction. Nobody is being told they may put eleven in."

"Nine is not a safe direction. Nine is what nine people weigh." She took the chalk off its hook and looked at it, and then put it down again without using it, which was a restraint Marek respected. "One interval is twenty-six strokes, and the whole of it is a test. If the crew is in that corridor on a Tuesday because it is convenient, then on the fortieth Tuesday nobody will remember that the number came from a test, and on the fortieth Tuesday there will be a woman in there with a fever and a man in there with a basket and nine will look like a capacity and not like a coincidence. The number goes. It is replaced with the condition, which is already on the page: nobody stands in that corridor unless the lever is moving and every watch is filled, and the count is whatever the people standing there are on the day."

"Who decides the count on the day?"

"The people who will be standing in it, and the one who will keep it." She put her thumb on the page. "That is my line. I am the one who will keep it, so I wrote that, and I am not renewing a number because somebody liked the shape of it."

"Fine," Marek said, and meant it, and made himself not add anything, which took him longer than the sentence.

It went on for two hours after that, and the page did not get renewed. It got corrected, and one of the corrections came from a man who had not spoken at a review in six weeks and had not been asked to.

Isel Ferrow had come down from the clinic return that morning on his own feet, for the first time since the fever broke, because he had asked Mikka whether the corridor was open and Mikka had said it was not, because the sixth watch was not filled, because Renn was not taking it today.

He stood at the back of the anteroom with his hands behind him and listened to the page go around twice, and then he said, "There is a line with my room in it."

Nobody had been reading the furniture. Dalla found it. It was the fourth line of the clinic-return condition, and it read: *the clinic return watch is held at the return door, which adjoins the room of a resident whose condition made the return necessary.*

"That is not a name," Dalla said.

"It is not a name. It is worse than a name." Isel had not raised his voice and did not now. "It has been there since the seventeenth day and I have read it four times, and every time I have read it I have been the reason somebody stands in a doorway. I have been the fever. I have been the man who was in the bed. And I want it struck, and I want the striking written down, and I want it said that I asked for it, because in about four months somebody who was not here is going to read that line and be told that this watch has a reason, and the reason is a person."

Mikka's face did something Marek could not read at the time and understood a week later, which was that a cook who has spent two months being the person who noticed a fever had just been told, politely, that she had built a sentence with somebody else inside it.

"That was mine," she said. "I wrote it. I want it struck too, and I want my name on the striking, because I put a man's illness in a duty and I did it because the sentence was useful and not because it was true."

"Then what is the reason for the watch?" Renn said, from the landing, where she had come in and stayed, which she did on days she was not holding anything.

"Not the room," Nera said.

"The door." Sefu had not moved from the wheel. "The clinic return door opens inward and it opens onto a room with people in it, and if a person carrying a basin gets to it at the wrong moment it is a wall across a walkway with nine people behind it. The watch is for the door. It has been for the door since the first week and this morning we all found out what it was actually for, which took a man with a fever and a cook with a sentence and me feeling very clever."

Isel nodded once. "That is better," he said. "I would rather be a door reason than a fever reason."

Dalla wrote the strike and the two names under it, and Jo wrote the replacement line herself, in her own hand, at the board, and read it back before Dalla copied it: *the clinic return watch is held at the return door, for the door. The resident in the adjoining room is not a reason, a load, or a term of anybody's plan.*

Renn's correction came next, and it was about her, and it was the one that made the page smaller.

"The sixth watch reads as held four days in seven," she said. "It is not held four days in seven. It is held on the days I can stand it, and there is a list, and the list is mine, and if the list is not on the page then anybody in a year reads four in seven as a rate and not as a woman with a floor."

"Isel," Marek said, before he could stop himself, and then heard himself do it, and put his hand flat on the bench.

Renn gave him a long look. "Isel is not why the list is short," she said. "Isel is why it is longer than it was. Do not put people in my arithmetic. I have four people in my rooms and one of them is not walking, and for four days in seven she is at the clinic return on Isel's arrangement, which is a thing Isel did and which I said yes to, and I said it out loud in this room nine days ago and I am not going to be thanked for it twice. Three days in seven the watch is not held. On those three days the corridor is not used, and the school corner is a waiting room, and that is the school paying for a decision it did not make."

"At the school's cost," Ilsa said, "by my decision, and I would like it to keep saying that, because the alternative is that in a year this reads as a favor and favors turn into obligations in about a season."

"Noted," Dalla said, and wrote it in Ilsa's words, which she had learned to do.

And then it stopped being a page about the corridor and became a page about the building, which is what the review had been for all along and what nobody had been willing to say.

Tams ran the count first, because he always ran the count first and because Dalla had told him in the second week that a number said at the start of a review cannot be argued with later.

"Stores," he said. He had the board turned round so the room could read it upside down, which he had started doing after the first week, when he noticed that people who would not argue with a number would argue with a person. "No sound timber over six feet. No bronze seat. No lath. Wedges, nine." He looked up. "And nothing else, which is the part I keep having to say, because every week somebody comes in here with a use for a piece of wood the way you would come into a room with a use for a nail."

"Nine wedges," Mikka said. "For what?"

"Today?" Tams turned the board over. "Four of them are packing the school-door head where Oren has it on a line, and it wants redoing, not because it is failing but because shoring that was packed by hand on an emergency has set and a set wedge is a wedge that has lost half of itself. Three of them are packing the waist on the corridor's kitchen side, where the fill washed out under the boards. And two are in the basket in Jo's hand."

"So the store is empty and the work is using all of it," Dalla said, and wrote it.

"And the two in Jo's hand are the two we are not supposed to spend," Tams said. "Those are the ones we reach for when something happens. If I am wrong about that I would like to be told now, because everything after this gets cheaper or more expensive depending on it and I would rather know at the count than in an emergency."

Nobody told him he was wrong, and he wrote the two down as a reserve, and Dalla did not use the word *reserve* without a time under it, which is what Jo had made her do on a day with a stone lintel coming down over the only door a litter could go through.

Then the pump wanted its interval, because the wet seam had put a finger of water into the service well overnight and the lower mark had moved, and the interval is the thing the whole afternoon is built out of.

Jo took the lever. Tams counted. Oren took the relief at the frame and put his weight over the plinth rather than against the housing, which was where he had put it every day since the fourth week and which was the reason he was still working. On the ninth stroke the delivery check gave its ragged double knock, and Jo stopped it, wiped the seat with a cloth, and started the count again from one. The bright scrape along the bronze lip had not widened. Dalla wrote that down as small, because it was small, because a small thing written down is a small thing that later cannot be used to tell a lie.

Pell called the water from the stair head with his slate on his knees and his splinted ankle on the folded blanket, and Renn was at the waist with the lamp, and the landing stood empty, which is the whole point of the sixth watch and the reason a person can be in two rooms that are not the same room. Sefu was at the wheel. Dalla was at the threshold. Nera was at the valve with the dyed cloth in her hand, watching a room breathe, and Mikka was at the return door, on a reason that was a door.

The corridor filled to nine. It emptied at the lever-off. It filled again. It emptied again. And Marek stood at the waist, in the middle of it, and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since the twenty-ninth day, because a review that only looks at what is working is not a review.

Nine people could stand on a floor for the twenty-six strokes, twice, with a pump running, in a strip of boards on a washed-out fill, while the air they breathed was the air the pump was making, over a shaft nobody could see into.

The whole of the opportunity was made of bronze. The seat was scored, there was no spare, there was no bronze in the building, and Jo had said in front of witnesses that it was exactly as bad and exactly as bad again in about four cleanings. Every cleaning was a half-day out of Jo's shutter route, and Jo's shutter route was the only route in this building that paid anybody anything, and there had been four cleanings.

And on three days in seven the corridor did not open at all, because a woman would not stand in a wet place, and nobody in the building had the standing to make her.

And there was no sound timber over six feet, so the school-door lintel stayed a cracked head over two feet eight inches, and the kitchen brace could not be made, and the clinic sill could not be reset, and the fire in the flue was held up by a wall nobody was allowed to mend.

And the service bypass behind the anteroom ran continuous stone all the way down to the pump gallery, and the pump gallery was flooding, and below the pump gallery was the chamber they had all come out of, which was failing and was not a place to return to. So the bypass was not an exit. It was a sump with a corridor on top of it and a machine at the bottom of it, and the machine had four cleanings left.

Marek put his left hand flat on the wall at the waist, because it was the thing he did, and the wall gave him nothing. It had given him nothing in the twenty-first day and on the twenty-third and on the twenty-fifth, and it had given him absolutely nothing on the day he had wanted it to most, and it gave him nothing now, and he wrote on the corner of Dalla's slate, in his own uneven hand, the word he had started writing every time: *null.*

"It's the seat," Jo said, watching him. "You know that."

"I know it's the seat. I wrote it down anyway." He put the slate back. "In four cleanings the pump stops, and when the pump stops the corridor stops, and when the corridor stops we are back to a single antechamber that holds ten people and a gate that six of us are standing behind."

"Back to the anteroom and the stair," Dalla said, reading the ledger's own list, because she had been waiting for somebody to say it and had decided she would say it herself if nobody did by the fifth bell.

She read it off the bottom of the page, the way she read everything, and it was the last unresolved line and it had been there since the twenty-seventh day.

"The only physically continuous way down," she said, "is restricted, corded, and not openable by anyone in this building."

Nobody filled the silence, which lasted long enough to become a thing in the room.

"Say the question properly," Mikka said. "Dalla has been carrying it since the twenty-seventh day and she is not going to say it for us. It is not *the way down*. It is: what does this room do on the day the pump stops for good."

And Marek, who had spent six weeks being the man in the room with the plan, found that he did not have one that did not involve the thing they had corded.

"Today," he said, "the honest answer is that the answer is the stair, and the stair is a slab over a throat with a hand repair in its socket that we have looked at for six minutes from a fixed landing, and nobody has stood on it, and nobody is going to stand on it, and it is the only way out of this building that is not full of water." He put both hands on the bench. "So no. I do not have an answer that does not involve it. That is where we are, and I would rather say it at the fourth bell on a Thursday than find out at the fourth bell on the day."

Dalla wrote it. She wrote it as his sentence, in his words, and did not improve it, and when she had finished she read it back and then said the part that made it a record rather than an opinion.

"Recorded as an unanswered question with a named owner and no solution," she said, "and the owner is the six of you, and nobody in this room has agreed to pay for the answer, and the stair stays corded while you look for one."

And the pump came off the lever, and the corridor emptied through the kitchen line, and Dalla rang twice from the threshold, and the anteroom got its ten back, and nobody went to the stair, and above them on a stair nobody was climbing a barricade plank at the ninth tread held a slate with four dates and a word, and on the anteroom bench under a spread of work cloth the wrapped plate lay where it had lain for six weeks and had not said one word about any of it, and nobody in the room had looked at it once all afternoon, which was also a decision.
