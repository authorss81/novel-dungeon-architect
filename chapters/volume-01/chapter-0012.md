# Chapter 12 — The Work That Keeps Water Down

The first named shift began before the route record was dry.

Before Jo took the lever, Dalla confirmed the fresh two-hour interval beside the first review. Renn and Mikka stayed on the landing, the courier remained beyond the incline, and Sefu counted the ten occupants without adding the two on the landing. The pump would run only while the marked time remained on the board.

Jo Verrick stood at the service grate with the pump lever in one hand and Iven’s old flat file in the other. The file had a new wooden grip bound with cord, but the teeth along its edge were worn past the point where anyone would call them serviceable. She had brought it from the boundary work basket after Dalla read the open lines and told her, quite plainly, that choosing one trial shift did not turn her into the pump keeper.

“One shift,” Jo said. “I can learn enough to clean the check. If you want me responsible for it after that, ask me again when I know what the work costs.”

Marek nodded. The distinction felt restrictive until he imagined the opposite: accepting Jo’s first turn as proof that every later turn belonged to her.

Nera checked the air cloth above the pump plinth. “Delivery closed until everyone is clear of the well.”

Iven crouched beside the grate. The pump had been repaired in the gallery below, where the water gave Oren and Tams no room to stand together. Above, the long lever rose through a slot in the grating. Nera had opened the narrow inspection cover, but the pump housing remained below floor level, visible only in the lamp’s angled reflection.

“Here is the work,” Iven said to Jo. “The gland stays installed. We do not strip it because the delivery check chatters. First, stop the lever and keep the delivery shut. Then relieve the housing pressure through the bleed nick. I show you where the nick is; you feel for air before you put the wrench on it.”

Jo leaned over the grate. “I cannot see the nick.”

“Then you learn the location by touch and do not guess.”

Iven put his hand through the opening, careful not to lower his shoulder past the stone lip. “It is a shallow groove beneath the lower boss. When air feathers, cover it with cloth. The pressure lifts grit before it lifts water.”

“And if it sprays?”

“Then you were too late, and someone has to tell you the exact angle before you put your face over it,” Oren said from beside the cover.

Iven, who was the someone, gave him a grim look. “That is also correct.”

Jo closed the cover until only the lever slot remained. The pump was below, but the work belonged to everyone at the grate.

Tams took the first strokes while Iven talked Jo through the next stage. The pump started and began to work hard against a check that was still shut. Only once the lever had settled into its repeated rhythm did Jo ease the check open and the water begin to move. The housing knocked once, then fell into its uneven double beat.

Pell sat on the bench beside Dalla, his splinted left ankle propped on a folded blanket. A case-board record rested across his knees. He called the water mark on the marked stone below by reading it off the lamp’s reflection in the housing, not by guessing from the noise of the strokes.

“Mark one is under the bracket,” he said. “Mark two is clear. The water is at the first notch.”

“Keep calling it,” Tams said. “Do not be right once. Call it when it changes.”

That was better than asking Pell to pretend his ankle allowed him to work. He was a reader at the grate, not a bearer. He looked pleased, and then embarrassed by the pleasure, and put both away before anyone could answer him.

Marek studied the pump housing. The first maintenance plan was clear in his mind: close the outer service door, cover the well, raise the frame with the screw jack, and remove the delivery plate as one unit.

He began assigning the work, then stopped when Jo cut him off.

“You are not lifting the frame,” she said.

“The rear foot is pinned. The jack will make the housing easier to reach.”

“It will also lift the pump away from the line in the plinth.” Jo pointed with the flat file. “I have carried baskets through a room where every weight shifts. You do not raise a machine on one foot because its base is dirty.”

Iven crouched and looked into the opening. “She is right. The foot is packed, not loose. The frame is carrying the stroke sideways.”

Marek’s left hand tightened around the edge of the bench. He had seen the pinned foot as a defect. Jo had seen the place where a jack would make the defect dangerous.

“New plan,” he said. “We do not raise the frame. Oren and Tams take the weight from the side while Iven seats the housing. Jo calls the wrench angle. Pell calls the water. The delivery check stays shut until the retaining bolt is clear.”

“Who holds the lower catch?” Oren asked.

“Leena cannot take it. I can, but I will not use the injured hand for the final pull. Iven calls the release. Oren and Tams take the load. Jo has the wrench. We go on three.”

The correction was public because the error had been public. No one looked triumphant. Oren took his place at the cover, Tams crouched beside him, and Iven moved the wrench into Jo’s hand.

The bolt was crooked.

They got it loose with three careful pulls, each one followed by a pause while Oren confirmed the frame had not moved. When the retaining plate came away, dark water and a smear of gray sediment ran from the housing into the flooded bay. The delivery check itself was not broken; a thin fan of silt had lodged under the bronze seat.

Jo wiped the silt with a strip of old cloth, then held the seat open for Iven to inspect.

“If you scrape the bronze,” Iven said, “you can cut the lip and turn a check that chatters into a check that leaks. Clean the edge, then dry the seat.”

“How do I know it is dry?”

“Touch it with the edge of the cloth. Wet cloth darkens. Dry cloth does not.”

Marek was already reaching for the service well cover. Nera stopped him with a hand.

“What are you doing?”

“Closing the outer service door. The check will stir dust when we open the housing, and I do not want the sediment in the anteroom.”

“The outer service door is the room’s escape path,” Nera said. “It is also the relief draft. Close it while the well is open and the pressure will have nowhere to go. The air will come through the gate instead, and used air can gather under the beam.”

Marek looked at the door, then at the open well. He had treated the draft as contamination to be blocked. Nera treated it as part of the room.

“Leave the door open,” he said. “Cover the well with the loose panel and a damp cloth. Renn watches the landing. If the cloth lifts, we stop.”

Renn moved the enclosed lantern to a landing bracket where the light covered both the outer threshold and the pump grate. “If air reverses, the flame bends toward the pump.”

“The cloth is our warning first,” Nera said. “The enclosed lantern is our confirmation, not an open flame. Do not wait for one if the other changes.”

The well was covered, the outer door stayed open, and the sediment came away. Jo cleaned the seat until the bronze showed an even dull color. Iven checked the bronze face of the gland, then the fiber lining behind it. The gland was dry.

They ran a short draw-down without leaving the well covered. Nera watched the air cloth, Dalla watched the water mark, and Pell called the minute from the bench. The delivery check knocked twice and stopped chattering. The lower step remained clear.

Jo sat back on her heels. “That is all?”

“That is this cleaning,” Iven said. “The housing pinches the wrench because the lower boss is worn. Next time, loosen the retaining bolt before you put the whole tool into the mouth.”

“So the next time is not free.”

“No.”

At the end of the first two-hour interval, the pump was stopped and the room came back to the count it had started with. Nera found the air cloth steady and the water below the sill. Dalla marked the interval closed, and the next cleaning would require a new time before Jo touched the housing.

The first visible result was a pump that could run through one pressure change. The lower cache still held the bulk tools, but the daily work above the grate needed a different set. Tams began a small inventory board beside the lever.

He wrote down the timber lever, the flat file, the canvas cover, the three Hearthhold work wedges, the installed gland seal and drive-dog key record, and the two repair pieces. Then he stopped.

“The pry bar and jack are still below,” Tams said. “If we need them, someone has to go into the flooded gallery.”

The courier called the unmarked gallery equipment guild-frozen pending the hearing. Marek could dispute that description, but the order’s actual language still covered only equipment the guild had financed. The public-works jack, the pry bar, and the remaining timber had to stay recorded in the lower cache until someone with authority decided what they were.

“Leave them,” the courier said from the incline. “Guild-frozen tools are not to be moved without an inventory witness.”

“The jack is Public Works equipment,” Marek said. “The pry bar may be too. Neither is a guild part by the badge in the drawer.”

“The order freezes guild-financed equipment pending the hearing. You are treating unmarked tools as if it said more.”

Iven’s face went cold. “Then it freezes a drawer. It does not make the tools yours.”

Marek looked toward the work sheet, the custody order, and the cache mark Nera had left during the pump repair. The order could make an item difficult to recover. It could not make the item stop being needed. The practical answer was not to carry every tool upstairs, where a later hearing could accuse them of removing guild material from a frozen line, and not to pretend the equipment belonged to nobody. The cache needed an owner, a reason, and a person who could decide when a tool left.

“Nera’s inventory covers the cache,” Iven said. “Dalla records the items needed for the next shift. We leave the jack, pry bar, spare timber, and the Public Works wedges below unless a named work requires one. If the courier wants them moved, he gives us a written order that says where they go and who receives them.”

The courier gave a small, noncommittal nod. The paper on the bench remained separate from the pump.

Dalla bent over the ledger. “The first shift has a cost.”

Jo gave a tired laugh. “It has a cost.”

Jo’s repair-route basket had been standing beside the bench since the anteroom test, and none of the six had touched it. Her ordinary route covered a kitchen brace, a wash-room shutter, and two door frames whose hinges had begun to catch. By taking the pump shift, she had completed none of them. The cost was not a token in a charter; it was half a day’s work deferred and another worker likely to cover it.

Dalla wrote it plainly: *Jo Verrick — one half-day of tool-route work for the first maintenance shift. Further cleaning repeats the cost. Payment or rotation to be decided.*

Jo read the line. “Do not change that to ‘community labor.’ It is my route, and the delay is mine.”

“It will remain exactly as written,” Dalla said.

Renn had watched the door from the landing and the pump through the grate for the full shift. Nera had checked the air cloth at three intervals. Tams and Oren had taken the load and the lever. Jo had cleaned the check under Iven’s instruction. The crew had not done the work alone, and the residents had not done it invisibly.

When Jo’s name was entered on the route sheet, it was with a limit and a stop condition. She could refuse the next cleaning until the cost was resolved. Nera could stop the pump if the air shifted. Sefu could close the boundary if the water or the working room changed. Marek could stop the structural work, but he could not turn his stop into consent.

The pump stayed stopped below the floor, drawing no water until a new interval was authorized. The delivery check was quiet, and the route record showed the cost of the work that had been done.

The first resident-maintained shift was over.

The next one already had a price.
