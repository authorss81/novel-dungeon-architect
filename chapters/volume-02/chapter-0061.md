# Chapter 61 — A Hand and Not a Fist

Two days after the courier went out up nine steps with a satchel and a drawing folded inside it, Tams Quill put his right hand flat on the bench of the anteroom at the first bell and it opened.

He had asked for this and he had been refused twice, once by Dalla Sen, who said that a hand does not get checked on a bench because a person would like it to, and once by himself, in the sixth week, when he decided that a man who looks at his own hand every morning stops being able to tell whether it is better. What changed his mind was a line in the ledger about the twenty-fourth day, which said that a hand is looked at on the day, and if it does not open then it is a month and not nine days, said at the time and not before.

It was a month. It was not nine days. He said so out loud, twice, in a room with nine people in it, and the second time he said it to nobody in particular and had to stop for a moment before he could go on.

The middle and the ring were the two, and the gap between them had been a finger and a half of nothing since the seventh week, and the gap was now the width of two fingers, and the little finger still did not come all the way down, and the hand opened flat and stayed open for as long as he asked it to, which on that morning was twenty-two seconds, and he asked it for twenty-two because somebody in the room would have counted otherwise.

"Twenty-two seconds," Dalla Sen said, writing.

"It is twenty-two seconds because I asked it for twenty-two and it gave me twenty-two, and I am not going to tell this room that it is the twenty-second second, because it is not." He put his left hand flat on the bench beside it so that the two were side by side, and then he took the right one away and looked at it. "It is a hand. It is not a fist. My sister used to say a hand that opens is a hand that is coming, and she was talking about a jar, and she was right about a jar."

For about as long as it takes to boil a pot nobody in that anteroom had anything to say, because the room had done the arithmetic on its own and had got to the same place, and the place it had got to was a woman standing at the frame with a scored bronze seat and no bronze in the building.

"Right," said Tams Quill. "Then somebody had better say it before I do, and I would like it to be the right somebody."

"Get your hand out of the way, Tams," said Jo Verrick, "and go and stand at the other end of this room, because I am going to say the thing and I want to be able to see your face while I say it."

---

She had a board and she put it on the frame's shelf and she did not write on it for a while, which she had never done before, and everybody in the room understood that she was not stuck, she was pricing.

"Right," she said. "The seat goes before the cold. I said that on a Friday at the frame with a room full and I have not moved it. The cold came in on Tuesday and it is here and it is going to be here for five weeks, so I am going to say the rest of it in the same room and the same voice.

"Here is what I want. A piece of hardwood, eleven inches across the face, and two and a quarter deep, and it wants a set face on it — a little proud at the top and a little hollow at the bottom, so that the head sits in the hollow and the proud bit at the top is what stops it riding up. I did not learn that. A man who is under a stone eleven feet down knew it, and I learned it off him, and I have not said his name in a week and I am not going to start.

"Here is what it costs. A day and a half of my time, which is a day and a half of my shutter route, which is the only money that comes into this building from outside it, and I have already lost a day and a half of it in the seventh week and a half-day of it in the ninth week and neither of those is coming back. So: a day and a half, out of a thing that is already a day and a half short.

"And the housing has to be open. Not for a minute. Open. And while the housing is open the pump is not pumping, and while the pump is not pumping the water in that dip comes up, and I have said that sentence out loud in this room before and I am saying it again because in a fortnight it is going to be the reason for something and I want it in the room before the something, not after it."

She wrote on the board then, in her own hand, in short lines, and she read them out as she wrote them, which is how she works and how the room knows it works.

"Seven, the seat is a third of the life of the one it is in. That is not a guess and it is not a saying. That is the ratio between a bronze seat and a hardwood seat in a housing that pumps sump water five minutes after every interval, and I have got it in my head from fifteen years and I have never had to say it in a room before.

"Eight, and this is the one I cannot price and I am writing it anyway. **I have no instrument.** There is no check on this machine any more. There is a hole with a shape in it and it hisses, and if the seat goes, the hiss goes thinner, and if the hiss goes thinner I stop the draw. That is the whole of what I know and it is the only thing I have ever had to know about this pump and I have been running it for eleven weeks.

"Nine, and I am writing it last because it is the cheapest thing on this board and the hardest one to say out loud. **I do not have the wood.**"

---

She put the chalk down and turned round, and the room had the look it gets on a morning when somebody has said the thing they were all waiting for and it has been worse than the version they had in their heads.

"Nine," said Mikka Hale. "Say that again and say it slower, because I have just written a number on a board of my own and I want to know whether the two of us are talking about the same shortage."

"Hardwood," Jo said. "Eleven inches. Sound. And I am not fussy about the rest of it, I have cut seats out of elm and I have cut them out of oak and I do not care which, and I will tell you the one thing I do care about, which is that it has to be dry enough to sit in a wet housing and wet enough to stay in one, and the way you get that is a piece of wood that has been in a damp room for a year or more, and there is not one piece of wood like that in this Hearthhold, and I have been in this building eleven weeks and I know where every stick of it is."

"Say where," said Renn Vale, from the upper landing, where she had come and stood without being asked and without going any further down than that.

But the room went to Tams Quill first, because the room had learned in eleven weeks that when anybody in it said *I do not have the wood*, the answer was on a tally board and the tally board was in Tams Quill's left hand.

Tams did not read the whole thing. He read the material, which is what they were asking for, and he read it in the order he had invented, and he put his right hand down off the bench and kept it in his lap while he did it.

"Material," he said. "No sound timber over six feet. No lath. No bronze. Seven sound wedges and two dead sticks, and every sound one of the seven is spoken for — three at the school-door head that wants four and has been refused its fourth by Renn Vale in her own words and the refusal is not withdrawn, three at the waist in washed-out fill, and one in the basket — and none of the seven is spare and none of the seven can be got. Five public-works hardwood wedges split between the two shores, and the newer shore has none in it at all, and that is on Renn's slate.

"Timber in this building, total, of every kind anybody can point at. A scarfed head of three pieces on the common landing. One length of five foot nine under that head. And a two foot ten and a handful in Jo Verrick's basket, and the basket is Jo's and it was Jo's before I got here, and the two foot ten is what is left of the anteroom's four foot eleven after a shelf of it was cut into packing sizes for somebody else's shore, and I have got that in the *spent* column and I am not going to read it twice tonight.

"So: eleven inches, out of a two foot ten and a handful, which is packing, which is the only hardwood-looking thing left in the building and is not sound and is not seasoned and has been in a basket in a warm room for eleven weeks." He looked up. "I am not going to say the sentence. Somebody will make me say it."

"No," said Jo Verrick. "He is not going to say it, and I am not going to make him, because it is mine. I have got two foot ten in a basket and I would not cut a seat out of it. **A seat cut out of packing is a seat that goes in six weeks and takes the delivery with it.**"

---

Sefu Aran had been at the wheel the whole of it, which is where he is when the room is deciding something, and he said now what a keeper says, which is that a thing does not happen because a woman has said it out loud.

"Name it," he said. "The same way I named the shore, and in my own words, and the naming can be struck out loud by anybody I name it to, and I am naming it to Jo Verrick and to the clerk."

Sefu came up off the wheel's stool, which he does not do, and the room stopped what it was doing.

"A delivery seat in hardwood, cut, dressed and set, in the pump under this floor, by Jo Verrick, on a day to be named, received by the boundary and not by any person above it, and the boundary keeps it and the boundary is responsible for it and nobody outside this building has any claim on it and no claim on the work.

"And I am not going to say *and run until the first thin hiss after it*, because she has told me she has no instrument and I have listened to her tell me, and a seat that cannot be read is a seat that cannot be judged, and I would rather this boundary had no seat than a seat it thinks it has."

"Struck," said Jo Verrick.

"Struck," Sefu agreed, "and in the clerk's hand, with a time on it, because a keeper who strikes half his own sentence and does not say so is a keeper who has learned to write."

Dalla Sen wrote it, and read it out, and read the struck part as well as the kept part, and Tams asked for the word *struck* to be entered under the material as well as under the work, which Dalla did, in her own hand, and made him read the line himself because he had not read a line in eleven weeks.

Then the room came back down to the ground, the way a room does, and somebody said the thing that everybody had been circling since Tuesday.

"Whose wood," said Isel Ferrow.

"Whose wood," Jo agreed. "That is the whole of it. Not *is there any*. **Whose.** Because there is a piece of hardwood in this Hearthhold that would do that seat, and I have known where it is since the seventh week, and so has one other person in this room, and neither of us has said it out loud in ten weeks because saying it out loud would have been a question, and a question in this room has a person attached to it."

---

Marek Venn had been standing at the back of the anteroom with his left hand on the bench end, and he had watched a hand open and a price go on a board and a work get named and struck, and he had felt the thing in him that arrives about four times a week now, which is the itch to have the problem before anybody else finishes asking for it.

It had arrived on the word *seat*, and it had arrived with a shape in it, and he knew the shape. It was not a shape about timber. It was a shape about a sequence: this person holds, that person cuts, this person reads, that person decides, and the sequence is the work, and a work with a sequence can be costed, and a costed work can be asked for by name, and a work asked for by name is a work a person has agreed to. He had been building that shape four times a month for eleven weeks and it had got him nothing but a room full of people who had stopped him.

The honest part of it, the part he could not argue with, was that it was good. It was the only thing he had that was good. Jo Verrick had a price on a board and a work named by a keeper and a list of things she did not have, and in the whole of that there was no place for him to put anything. He was a man with a left hand who could hold a board while somebody else wrote on it, and in a room where a woman was about to be told no four times in one morning, the most useful thing he could possibly be was the person who was not offered anything.

He put his right hand in his pocket, where it had lived since the collapse and had not come out since, and stayed where he was.

Sefu Aran said: "Then let us have the search. Not as a favour. Named, dated, and with the crew asked for by me by name, and the hours on the boundary's column, because the hours that count are the ones that get counted and I have read Tams Quill say so twice."

"Three of us," said Marek.

"Three of you and any of us we want, and I am asking for Renn Vale and not asking for a rota, because a rota is a duty and a question is not, and if a person says no to a question then nobody has been rostered and nobody has been given anything." He looked up at the upper landing. "Renn."

"I will come," said Renn Vale. "And I will say here what I am and what I am not, because it has been said every time for eleven weeks and I am not going to stop saying it now that it is convenient. I do not stand on a stair. I am not going to be on a stair this month, and I am not going to be asked, and if I am asked I will say no and it goes in the book with the time on it. That is wider than the wet seam and it has not been withdrawn and it is not being withdrawn because we are short of wood.

"And I will come because this search is a question about **who owns a piece of wood in this Hearthhold**, and I can tell you before anybody starts that five of the answers are going to be no, and that I know all five, and that I have known all five for some time, and I would rather be standing on this landing when the room finds them out than find them out afterwards in a room I was not in."

Nobody asked her the follow-up. Ilsa Norr, who had been looking at the middle of the room for a moment with her arms crossed, put her hand flat on the bench.

"Then let us do it in the cold," she said, "and let us do it in one day, because a search that takes a week is a way of not doing it, and I know that because I have watched four of them in this building and I have said the words *let us do it in one day* in this room before and I was talking about the school."

---

The anteroom went on being lived in. The wheel went round twice under the floor and went on going under the conversation in the kitchen line the way weather goes on going under a conversation, and there was a frost on the inside of the window in the wash end that had not been there on Sunday, and Nera Oduya put her hand flat on it and took it away and wiped the print off with her sleeve, which is a thing she does not do, and which said more about the week than anything anybody said out loud.

And the check hissed under the floor, flat, continuous, and it had gone thin on the Thursday of the tenth week and it had not come back to a full note since, and the seat was going now, and the cold was here, and there was no wood.

At the top of nine steps, a boy with a splinted ankle and a crutch across his knees had his ear against the stone of the step above the stair head, and he heard the wheel, and he called the interval eight seconds early in a voice that did not carry and did not need to, and he had no opinion about timber.
