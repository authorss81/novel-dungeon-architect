# Chapter 32 — What the Route Costs Without One

The list the crew had made on Sunday was four lines long and none of them could be started.

Marek had written it himself, on the back of a slate, in his left hand, with the four things in the order he wanted them: the lower receiving side of the stair, looked at from the fixed landing; the school corner's channel, opened; a dead-load bench, built; the pump's seat, opened and looked at. Four lines, and the first one needed a lamp and a hand and Renn's account of the receiver, and the second one needed a day, and the third one needed timber, and the fourth one needed Jo.

On Monday morning he learned that the first one needed nothing at all except a woman who had not been asked yet, and that the third one needed a thing that did not exist, and that the whole day belonged to a door.

It began with the ledger, because the ledger came first on Mondays, and because Tams read the ledger out loud whether or not anybody wanted to hear it.

"Five liters from the collapse," he said, "and one sealed liter in the jar, sealed, and not opened. Kitchen provision separate, nineteen days, given and not waived, and the count has not gone down in nine days because the pot is still on. Jo's route: four cleanings, no shutter work done. Iven's instruction, twenty-nine hours at last count, and I am counting it in whole hours because the fractions are not in anybody's favour." He turned the ledger round. "Nothing has been paid. Not one thing. Nine days, and the two columns on this page are the same length they were the day before I got here."

"That is not a failure of the record," Dalla said. "That is a fact about the position."

"I know it is a fact about the position. I am reading the fact about the position." He set the ledger down and wiped his hand on his thigh, which was a thing he did when the arithmetic was going to get worse. "I want it said out loud once, so nobody in the next month thinks there is a pot of money coming. There is not. There is a column, and a pot, and a shutter route that is closed for the season, and a man who cannot be paid without it becoming guild work, and nineteen days of soup that a cook is not going to let anybody call settled."

"I am not going to stop cooking," Mikka said from the kitchen line, where she had been listening with a bowl in each hand, "and I would like the phrase *cannot be paid* to stop being used about me, because the money is the small part."

"Noted," Dalla said.

And then the door.

Ruk Sella came down the kitchen line at the second bell with the child on her hip and stopped in the anteroom and said, "The hinge has gone."

Nobody had asked her for anything. That was the part that mattered. She had a boy of about six on her hip and a second child asleep at the top of the clinic return in Isel's arrangement, and she had come to find a person who could tell her whether the thing that had been letting cold into her rooms all week was the stair or the building, and Oren, who was standing at the school-door head with his shoulder under it because that is where he stood when he had nothing else to do, told her it was the building.

"Sit down, then," she said, before anybody had offered her anything. "I have two children and I cannot have a door that opens on its own when the wheel turns, and I am not going to ask for a watch. I am going to ask for a nail."

It was not a nail. Jo went and looked at it for a quarter of an hour and came back and said so.

"The pin has sheared," she said, "which means the door is hanging on the head, and the head has dropped with the jamb because the jamb is part of the lintel that came down in the school floor. So it is not a door problem. It is a door at the end of a broken frame, and I can rehang it a bit and it will still be crooked, and it will still open on its own in a wind that is not a wind."

"Can it be made to latch?" Ruk said.

"No. Not without timber to the head, and there is none in this building longer than six feet and none at all for this. What I can do is plane the top edge where it is riding the frame, and rehang it on a new pin, and shim it, and it will close. It will not stay shut against the wheel."

"Then close is what I need," Ruk said. "Not shut. Close. I want it to close when somebody comes through it, because the small one is seven and he thinks a door that moves is the building breathing, and he has been sleeping in the clinic return instead of his own bed, and I did not put him in the clinic return because of the door. I put him there because I was frightened, and I would like my own door to be able to do one of the two things it used to do."

The room got very quiet, and Marek understood that he was being shown something, and that the showing was not about a door.

"It is your door," Dalla said. "Say what it costs before anybody starts."

"It costs a board," Jo said, "and I do not have a board that is not already spoken for, and it costs me most of a day out of a route that is already shut, and the shutter I was paid for goes later than I told the household I would finish it." She looked at Mikka. "The six that will do for a shutter are mine and spoken for and the two with the grey growth on them are firewood, and the two under the corridor's kitchen side belong to the corridor and are under nine people's feet this minute. So I want a sound board out of the household store that has never been in anything, and I want it released in writing by whoever owns it, and I want the cost of my day in the ledger in my own words and not softened."

"It is mine," Mikka said, "and I release it, and write it down as released by me and not donated, and I want the third board that I gave you in the twenty-sixth day counted as mine as well, because that is two boards now and nobody has been keeping that count except me."

"Recorded as two, released by Mikka Hale, over her objection and on the record," Dalla said, and wrote it, and the count of Mikka's released timber in this building went from one to two, which was a third of the Hearthholds' entire standing stock, which had been six, and which was every sound board in this boundary that any person in it had the right to give away.

"I will feed whoever does it," Ruk said, to Jo, and Jo shook her head.

"You will feed the room, and the room is us, and the room eats anyway," Jo said. "But I will take the door as a door and not as a favour, and the ledger says *Ruk Sella's door, work done by Jo Verrick, no charge, at the household's request*, and if I ever want paying I will ask for it in front of people and not through a plate of something."

And then, while Jo was planing, the crew found out what the day was actually for.

Renn came in at the third bell with her coat on.

"You have a list," she said to Marek, not unkindly. "Say it out loud, because I have been standing at the end of it for two days waiting for somebody to say it, and the saying-it is not the problem. The problem is that your second line needs the school corner's floor open for a day, and the third line needs timber that is not here, and the first line needs me."

"The first line needs you," Marek said.

"Then you have a day with no work in it, and I have a floor that is going to be a waiting room in three days' time with nine children and a bench on it, and the two of those facts have been sitting next to each other since Sunday." She pulled the slate out from under her arm. It was Renn's slate, not Dalla's, and it had her own list on it in her own hand, and it was longer. "So here is what the day is. The stair is not going to be looked at on Tuesday because you have no lamp-post and no timber and no account of the receiver, and because the two of you who would do the carrying are on my list and on the school's list and both of those lists are longer than yours."

"We could use Oren and Tams—"

"You have Oren under my door-head and Tams on the lever and Jo on a door that is not yours," Renn said. "That is the day. You have thirty-one people in this building and every one of them is already doing the thing that keeps their own room standing, and you have four lines on a slate, and one of them wants the school corner's floor open, and I am telling you that you do not get the school corner's floor on a day when you are also carrying nine children through it on their way to the clinic return for a supper that is late."

"Then what do you want?" Marek said.

Renn looked at him for a while.

"I want to tell you the account," she said, "and I want it to cost me what it costs me, and not one hour of your work."

The room heard the shape of the trade before either of them had said it out loud.

"It is testimony," Marek said. "Sefu records it. Dalla writes it. It is a statement about a stair by a resident of the building the stair is in, made in front of the boundary keeper, and it cannot be taken back and it will be read by whoever comes next."

"Yes."

"And it is not a survey. It does not certify anything. The stair stays corded whatever you say, and nothing opens on the strength of it."

"I know that it does not open anything. That is why I am going to say it." She turned to Ilsa, who had been at the school corner's store with a slate under her arm since the fourth bell, and had not said anything. "I want one box opened."

Ilsa did not move.

"Your mother's box," Renn said. "The one with the household returns in it. Not the register. Not the class lists. The winter returns."

"You refused me the boxes in the second week of this," Ilsa said, "and you carried them to my store yourself and would not let anybody else touch them, and I have not opened one since, and I would like it written that I am opening one now and why."

"Write it," Dalla said, and wrote it, and Ilsa read it and nodded, and went and got the box herself, on the strength of her own arm, because nobody in that room was going to carry a teacher's boxes for her.

The returns were in a child's hand and then in a grown hand, one line a week, for two winters. Iron for the clinic pot, in the third week. Two lamps, given, the fifth. And then, in the ninth week of the long cold year: *the lower landing stone, cut in three, to the wash door, R. Vale senior's account, no survey.*

Ilsa read it twice, and then put the page down flat on the table with her hand on it, which is the way you put a thing down when you are not sure your hand should still be on it.

"They did not remove it," Renn said. "The guild's entry says *removed under a clearance after the stair failed an inspection*, and that is the guild's half, and the guild's half is that it came out in pieces and it went onto a record. Nobody in this building has ever read the entry as *carried away*, because nobody in this building has ever seen what it was used for."

"It is the wash door," Mikka said.

"It is the wash door and the lintel above it and the sill under it, and it has been those for forty years, and it is a stone that does not match the wall around it, and I have known that since I was small." Renn's voice did not change and her hands did. "And after it went out, somebody put a piece of it back."

Nobody said anything.

"Not the guild. The guild did not come back. My mother and two other women, in the last cold month, when the low side had been shut a whole winter and there were litters waiting on the upper flight because there was nowhere else to put them." She put her hand flat on the table beside the page, not on it. "She put a wedge-shaped piece back into the socket under the lower receiving landing, and she packed it with stone chippings, and she told the school staff it was a repair. And it worked for a while. And then it did not. And the guild came, surveyed, found the low side unsafe, wrote *removed under a clearance after the stair failed an inspection*, and corded it. And my mother's name is not in the guild entry and is not on the stair and is not in any of your records, and the piece she put back is still in that socket, because nobody took it out, and the stair has been running on her repair ever since."

She looked at Marek.

"You asked me on the first day to give you the lower receiving side, and I said I would give you the use and not the survey. That is what I have done. I have given you the use. I have now given you the account, and it cost me my mother's name in a room with a keeper in it, and I am not going to be thanked, and I am not going to be softened either." She picked up her coat from the back of the chair. "Do not go and stand in front of that socket and decide something about it in a voice I can hear."

And she went out, and the room let her go, and Jo kept planing the door, and the room was quiet in a way that had a shape.

Marek stood at the bench with the slate in his left hand and the four lines on it and understood that the third line had just become possible, and that the first line had just become a thing he had to look at.

*Dead load bench*: something heavy and dead stood on the near side at the moment the slab came back, so that the difference the stair was asking for arrived from a floor that already existed.

*Receiver*: a stone block, the guild's part and the school's part, cut into three, two thirds of it holding a wash door up in a Hearthhold room, and one third of it in a socket under a corded stair, put there by a woman with chippings and no materials, working alone, in a cold month, for stretchers that had nowhere else to go.

The material for the bench was stone. The stone they had was the fallen parapet slab, which was recorded as *removed for safety, not recovered, not claimed*, and which Dalla had said out loud could not later be described in a hearing as recovered stock.

"Do not," Dalla said, from the other side of the table, to nobody. "I know what you are all looking at. No."

"It is eleven feet of old stone lying in a store with a line through it," Marek said.

"It is eleven feet of old stone lying in a store with a line through it that says it was removed for safety and not recovered, and the moment a person stands it under a load, in a record that a guild can read, it becomes material in use, and material in use under a lien is a thing they can call." She closed the ledger. "You may use it for a door stop. You may not build on it."

"Then the bench has no weight in it," Iven said, from the doorway, where he had been standing for some time, "and a dead-load bench with no dead load in it is a bench, and a bench does not do anything." He came in and set his mallet on the table. "I have been a route carpenter for twenty years and I am telling you that a person who has come back to fix a door is not the person to decide the shape of a dead load, and that I do not know where there is a hundredweight of stone in this building that is not a hearth, a sill, a parapet, or somebody's wall."

"There is the pump gallery," Tams said. "It is full of sump and it has been full of sump for six weeks and there is stone down there that the flood took off the floor, because the sump is not clean and the sump has never been sorted."

"The pump gallery," Nera said, from the valve, without turning round, "is an air space I maintain, and it floods, and you are not going to sort stone in it while a machine with a scored seat is the only thing holding the level down. So the answer to the bench is no, and the answer to the missing timber is no, and the answer to the missing account has just been given, and I would like somebody to notice that this is the most expensive day this building has had in which nothing broke."

"It has not broken because nothing was attempted," Marek said.

"It has not broken," Nera said, "because you did not start. That is not nothing. That is the first honest thing your list has ever done."

By the fifth bell the door was hung and closed and crooked, and the boy of seven came down the kitchen line and put his hand on it twice and said it was a door, and Ruk Sella took him back up without saying anything, and the corridor filled and emptied over the dip twice more, and the anteroom took its ten back at the lever, and the bench had the wrapped plate under its cloth with its empty seat in the middle, and Marek Venn stood in a room where four lines of work had become five facts and a refusal, and wrote on his slate, in his own uneven left hand, the five facts, and did not draw a plan, because he did not have the timber, the weight, the account he had wanted, or the standing to ask for any of it.
