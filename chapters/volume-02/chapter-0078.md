# Chapter 78 — The Column With No Heading

Tams Quill read both columns at the fifth bell on the last day of the fourteenth week, and he read them in the order he always read them, and he got to the crew's figure and then he put the chalk down, which he has done twice in thirteen weeks.

"Forty-four and a half," he said. "Given, owed to nobody, and **it has not gone up.**"

The anteroom waited for the other half of it, because there is always an other half.

"No hours," said Tams Quill. "None. I have had the board for six days and there is not one mark on it in the ordinary column and I have been asked about it four times by people who are not being nosy, and I want to answer all four at once, so here it is and it is the whole of what I have got.

"Nothing in the fortnight was asked of us. I want to say that in the order it happened, and I am going to call the days by their weeks, because there are two Mondays in a fortnight and a man who does not do that is a man who has decided not to look at something. I have got it in a list and the list is short and I do not want anybody in this room to hear the sentence and imagine it as a complaint.

"**The first Monday:** five of us stood on the other side of a wall for forty minutes while five women unrolled a thing in a locked store, and the terms said *the five of them are not in the room*, and that was a term, and it was honoured, and I put nothing in the ordinary column for it, because a man standing on one side of a wall while something happens on the other side is not work and I have always been a man who will put anything in a column if you let him and I am not going to be the man who starts now.

"**The first Tuesday:** three of us in this anteroom for a day and a bit while this room overflowed, and I did the ordinary thing of being quiet in a corner with a slate.

"**The second Monday:** the arithmetic of a corridor and eight pails over a fill, and Iven Tallo did that, and he is not one of the five of us and he is not a Hearthhold and he is not on a rota, and he priced it in his own hand and he said out loud that his day of his own trade is not going in the crew's column because it was his choice and nobody asked us. **And he is right, and I would have got that wrong a fortnight ago, and I have written it down that he taught me that this fortnight.**

"**The second Tuesday:** a bar on a cold wall and a hand on a cold wall, and neither of those was asked of me and the second one was mine and nobody asked me to go down those three steps and I went because I wanted an eighth one.

"So: forty-four and a half, and it stands, and it is three weeks now — the twelfth, the thirteenth and the fourteenth — since the second week of the whole time we have been here that our column has not gone up, and I have never had to say a number bigger than two in this room and I have said it now."

---

"**Say the rest of it,"** said Bel Ordry, from the side, "because you have got that face on and it is not the face of a man who has found a piece of good news."

"Right," said Tams Quill, and picked the chalk up again and put it down again, which was a thing he did.

"Thirteen weeks. Forty-four and a half hours. And I have been proud of that column for eleven weeks and I said in this room, in the eleventh week, that the half that gets counted is the half that is ours, and that is still true and I am not touching it.

"And here is what it is measuring, and I have had it since about the third day and I have not said it, and I am saying it now, and it is the worst thing anybody in this room has said to me, including the woman who reads it out.

"**That number has never been a measure of us.** It is a measure of whether this boundary needs us. Forty-four and a half is not a quantity of engineering. It is a quantity of *asking*. Every single hour in that column is a line with a person's name against it in somebody's own writing saying *I would like your afternoon.* And in this fortnight the number of lines of that kind in this Hearthhold was zero, and the number of hours did not go up, and there is no way in the world to look at that and see anything else.

"I have been a man with a board in a room for thirteen weeks and I have thought the board was about us. **The board is about them.** It is a very good board. It is the only instrument in this Hearthhold that has never once lied. And it has just told me, in the ordinary column, in the plainest arithmetic anybody in this room will ever hear, that three weeks is the longest run since we came up a shaft that we were not needed, and that I have got a hand that opens and a column that does not move and those are the same piece of news."

Nobody in the anteroom said anything, and the covers were lifted at the second bell and at the fifth by a woman in a room nobody had walked into, and the pump hissed its one note.

"There is more," said Tams Quill, "and the more is worse, and I am going to say it because if I do not say it in this room then somebody in a year will do arithmetic on this board and get to it and think they have found something.

"**Our obligation ends on a condition, and the condition is in a book, and the condition is that this boundary can do the work without us and a person named by the boundary has said so out loud, in the room, with the reason.**

"And a column that does not go up is the first piece of evidence this Hearthhold has ever produced toward that condition. Not a person. Not a word. **A number that stayed still.** And I am the man in this room who could stand up and say *we are finished*, and it would be true, and nobody would be able to argue with a figure, and I have had six days with this board to think about it and I am not going to say it.

"And I want that written down as a refusal and not as an observation, in my own hand, in my own words, and read out, because **a man who could end an obligation with arithmetic and does not do it has to say that he knew he could.**"

"Entered," said Dalla Sen, "in the count's words, in his own left hand, with the time on it, and read out again at his request, and I am adding in the clerk's hand that this is the first entry in thirteen weeks in which a person has declined to be relieved of something, and that the declining is now on the record, and that the record is the one object in this building a stranger can hold, and I said that on a page in this room eight days ago and I am having to say it twice in eight days."

---

"Theirs," he said, and turned the board, and read it without rounding, which is how he reads it, and the anteroom had learned eleven weeks ago that the not-rounding was the point.

"**Ninety-five and a quarter.** Sixty-five before the assessor, and eighty-one and three-quarters after, and thirteen and a half in this fortnight, and I am going to read the thirteen and a half because it is the only list that matters and it is short.

"**Ilsa Norr, one and a half.** A whole day emptying her own store and carrying nine boxes down nine steps and a half-day for the description afterwards, and the half is hers and she asked for it to be hers and not the crew's. **Mikka Hale, two.** The pot, the book, and reading out, and she has not let me count the pot as a day for seven weeks running and I have not let her have it either and we have agreed to argue about it in the kitchen, and the fourteen-year-old she put at the top of nine steps for two hours is in that two and not in this book, and I asked her about it on the second Monday and she said she would rather he was in her book than in mine. **Jo Verrick, one and a half.** Closing the hunt for the wood and eight pails over a fill, and she will tell you the hunt cost a day and a half of her shutter route in the twelfth week and I have not entered that either, because it was in the first week of this fortnight and it is in the book. **Renn Vale, one.** The door. And she is still not having her four hours counted and it is the fourth week running I have said that out loud and she has not asked me to stop. **Dalla Sen, one.** Evenings, and I am counting a clerk reading a stranger's paragraph in her own time as work, and in the first week of this fortnight she read a description of a nine-hundred-year-old plan in her own time and that is in the one. **Bel Ordry, four.** Four mornings at the bar in this fortnight and the last of the four was the Sunday, and it is the sixth morning anybody has put that bar against that wall, and the entry he read out on the Thursday that said *measured: nothing*, and I am not entering that as an hour, it is not an hour, it is a man refusing to lie. **Isel Ferrow, one and a half.** A fill instead of a door and a shutter route half given up for it. **Nera Oduya, one.** The unrolling, the geometry, and two lines on a page with nothing between them.

"That is the list, and the list adds, and I have done it twice. **Thirteen and a half on eighty-one and three-quarters is ninety-five and a quarter.**

"And the ten days and the ten nights and the leg and the bundle on a back are **Wen Pask's**, and they are in the column with no heading and in nothing else. Not because they are not real. Because he is not on a rota, and he is not paid, and he is not owed, and he is not a post, and there is no column in this book for a man who is kept."

---

The column with no heading took the rest of the hour and it is the one that filled up.

"It is the fullest column on this board," said Tams Quill, "and I want that said first because it is going to sound like a boast and it is not. It is just true and it is the only thing on here that I cannot make smaller.

"In this fortnight it has: a leg at a service bend. Ten days and ten nights in a school store by a man who is twenty-two and has no bond. A day when a person stood on the wrong side of a wall for forty minutes because a term said so. One hour of the pump's own water going over a fill, put in by the man who put the fill in. Half a hinge off a man's mother's door, not fitted, not this fortnight, not any week. And an entry that is a man's answer to a question nobody asked him, which is that a bone in a stranger's pack is not going in a column, because the man it belonged to is under eleven feet of dressed stone at the near landing and is not coming up, and there is nothing of him in this building to put anywhere.

"And six mornings, in one column, where a bar said nothing three times and something three times and the something has a slide on the end of it.

"That is seven lines, and four of them are six days old, and the crew's ordinary column has not had a line put in it since the eleventh week. **I have been the man who insisted for four weeks that this column would get a name, and I have taken it back, and I am not going to explain why any more than that, because the reason is that the day I head it, it becomes a unit, and a unit is a thing you can total, and a thing a room can be praised in.**"

---

Then he came to the last thing on the board, which was not on the board, and he had to put the chalk down and pick it up twice.

"**The ninth line.**"

The anteroom did what an anteroom does, which is nothing, for about four seconds.

"I can put it in a column in about four seconds," said Tams Quill, "and I have known which column for three days, and I have not done it, and I am going to tell the room which one and why not, because if I do not, somebody will do it in a year and think they were the first.

"It goes in **held.** Held is the column for things that are still doing their job. It has had nothing in it for thirteen weeks but a shut. The ninth line is a room at the bottom of four treads with a one in the count and *none* in the when and nothing in the work, and by every rule this board has run on since the seventh week that is the definition of held, **and it is the only thing I have ever wanted to put in that column that is not a piece of wood and not a cord.**

"And I have not done it, and Renn is going to tell me why, and then I am going to ask for it to be written down in both our hands, because a refusal with two people on it is a different animal from a refusal with one."

"**A room is not kept by crossing it,**" said Renn Vale. "A room is kept by somebody going into it. There has not been a person in that room in the whole time I have been in this Hearthhold, and the day somebody goes down those four treads the keeping of it begins, and it will not stop, because that is what a room is. And I wrote that on a slate at the end of the first week of this fortnight and it has not been withdrawn, and I am not withdrawing it on a Sunday because a woman read a one out of a page in a locked store and everybody in this room heard her read it.

"So the ninth line is not held. **The ninth line is *none*.** It is a condition, and it is a condition somebody wrote down nine hundred years ago about a room, and it is on a page in a locked store, and it is the only piece of evidence anybody in this building has ever had that this place was maintained on purpose, and it says *on no day*, and I have read it four times and it has not changed and I have not changed, and I would like a person forty days away who is sent a copy of this book to notice that a boundary has refused to classify a room that its own oldest document describes."

"Noted in two hands," said Dalla Sen, "and read out twice, and I am adding in the clerk's hand that *none* is a condition in the ninth line of a document in a locked store and is not a word used in this book before the Monday of the thirteenth week, when five women unrolled a dowel in a room the five of us were not in, and that a boundary has now got two ways of saying *not on any day* — one of them nine hundred years old and in a store, and one of them a woman's in her own words on her own landing — **and the two are not in agreement and nobody has asked them to be.**"

---

Mikka Hale read the book out at the fifth bell, which she had said in the twelfth week she would do on the last day of every week, and it took four minutes, and she read it the way Jo Verrick read a price board and Tams Quill read a column: out loud, in her own order, with no summary.

"Six lines. Three shut days of eight pails, names and dates, and those three shut days are the Sunday and the two days after it, and a *water* in the third column forty-one times, and then on the first Wednesday night a line in my own hand that says *I have put a rota in a book and I did not mean to and the word when means boots.*"

"Second reading," said Dalla Sen, and Mikka Hale made her read it twice, and the second time was not better and was not worse and both of them were in the book.

And at the fifth bell, on the same line, as Renn Vale had asked for in the twelfth week and had now got twice: **the lip's range, and the seventh term, and the ninth line, and the pot, and the blank on the review page with a blank against it.** Five things, in five hands, read out at the fifth bell every day of this week and every day of next week, and the clerk's between them, and the anteroom hearing all five, and the pump going round under the floor, and the check on its one note.

And Marek Venn, at the end of the tally board, with a slate under his arm that had nothing on it, understood that the finding he had come down a shaft with had been in this room the whole time, and had been in it for thirteen weeks, and was this:

*A room is kept by somebody going into it, and a book only records that somebody went, and the difference between the two is the whole of what this Hearthhold has been arguing about since the seventh week, and it has just been written down in a clerk's hand on a page in an anteroom, by accident, as a warning, on the same afternoon, by a man with a board who declined to be relieved of an obligation using arithmetic.*

He had six nulls in a column and two in a bucket, which was eight in all, and no usable reading anywhere, and the brass rule was in the pocket of the coat on its hook, where it had been for six weeks, and he had not put his hand on it once in a fortnight, and there was nobody left in that building who would be able to tell him whether that was restraint or luck.
