# Chapter 85 — No Column for a Person

On the fifth day of the sixteenth week the school store held five people, nine register boxes on a dressed-stone shelf, two beech lengths propping the shelf, a locked box under the boxes, a bundle on the floor, a blanket on the floor, a boy of twenty-two with his leg up on a crate, and a carpenter who had not sat down.

Ilsa Norr unlocked it and stood in the doorway with the key in her hand and did not come in, and Dalla Sen wrote down that she had not come in, and the reason she gave took about eleven seconds and it was the best thing anybody in this building said that morning.

"**A store with a man sleeping in it is not a store.** It is a room with a man in it who is not a child and is not owed anything and has been in here sixteen nights. And if I walk in and start asking about the boxes, then I am a schoolteacher in a man's bedroom asking him about a box, and he will answer me, and he will answer me because he has no bond and no wage and no trade, and **that is exactly the shape of a thing he cannot say no to, and I am not doing it on a Friday.** So the key is mine and the door is open and I am standing in it, and if anybody in this room wants to ask me a question about a box they can ask me at the school door like everybody else."

Nobody asked her about a box.

---

"Read it out," said Iven Tallo to Wen Pask, "and read the fourth one twice, and read it in the words on the paper and not in mine, because you have heard me say it twice and it has got my voice into it by now and I want the paper's voice."

Wen Pask got the fold of guild paper out of inside his coat, where it had been for sixteen days, and it was soft at the creases and it was not his and it was not anybody's in this room's, and he read it out standing up with his weight on his good leg.

"*An apprentice under a suspended master may continue in the trade for a period not exceeding one year under the supervision of a named holder entered against a named route, in a register kept by the office, on payment of the annual supervision fee.*"

"Again."

"*Named holder. Named route. Register kept by the office. On payment of the annual supervision fee.*"

"Right." Iven Tallo put his own hands behind his back, which is what he does in a room when he is about to say something he has cost himself. "**The argument holds.** Third time. I am not going to say the other thing and I want the clerk to have it in my own words that I did not say it, and the reason I am not saying it is that the other thing has been in this room since the fifth day of the thirteenth week and every day it has been in here it has got a little cheaper, and it is going to keep getting cheaper, and the day it is cheap is the day I say it."

"Then say why you cannot say it," said Tams Quill, from the doorway, with his right hand open and his left holding nothing, "because I have watched you not say it three times and each time it has looked more like a principle and less like a reason, and I am the man in this building who has to tell the difference."

---

"There is one route in this Hearthhold," said Iven Tallo.

Nobody said anything, and the anteroom's pump was audible through the floor of the school store, which is a thing nine people can hear and a store can hold.

"There is one. Not one that people use — I do not mean the corridor and I do not mean the nine steps. **One that a route is.** One that could be named in a line and meant, and it is the one that goes down three steps and round the service bend to a fixed first landing, and it is the only object of care in this Hearthhold that a person who has never heard of us cannot pick up, and Dalla wrote that down in this building eight days ago in front of eleven people and read it out twice, and she is right, and it is the reason thirteen weeks of work in this place came out the way it did.

"So: the paragraph wants a named holder entered against a named route in a register kept by the office. There is a boy's trade in it. There is a year's worth of trade in it and a boy of twenty-two who has walked four days and cannot be paid and cannot be on a register and cannot be put against anything.

"And the only route I could enter against it is the one we have spent five weeks getting out of every file, and **I could put the stair in a register in a single afternoon with a piece of chalk and my own name under it, and it would be a good afternoon, and the boy would have his trade, and this boundary would have given the one thing it has got that is safe.**"

He said the last four words and then stopped for long enough that the pump came round once under the floor.

"That is the argument against, and it has been in this room since the sixth day of the thirteenth week and I have not said it out loud until now, and I am saying it now because a man who has held an argument in his mouth for a fortnight to look careful in a room is not being careful, he is being slow, and I have been slow.

"And I am not going to do it. **And the reason I am not going to do it is not the argument.** The argument would have been enough in the fifth day of the thirteenth week. It is not enough now, because now I have had a fortnight to think about it and what I have got instead of the argument is worse."

"Which is," said Wen Pask.

"Which is that **I do not know whether I would still say no in the spring.** And a man who cannot be sure of his own no in the spring has no business putting his own name on a form about a boy. I am thirty-six. I have been a man who says no in rooms for thirty-one years, and I have never once been asked whether I would still say no in a month, and this is the first month I have been asked, and I found out on Thursday night that I do not know. **So no. Not on that ground. And I want it written that the ground was not the argument, because in four years somebody is going to find that sentence in a guild file with a boy's name on the top of it and they are going to think I was proud.**"

"Entered," said Dalla Sen, in her own hand, "in the carpenter's words, and read out again, and I am adding in the clerk's hand that a man has just declined to use the argument that would have worked, and said so, and that is not in any column of this book and I am not going to make a column for it."

---

"Then it is me," said Wen Pask, and he said it fast, the way a young man says something he has had ready for two days. "No, hold on. I have got something and I have had it since the Friday and I have not said it because I was waiting to be asked and I have not been asked.

"That paragraph is not a door. **That paragraph is a door with a year's price on it, and the price is the annual supervision fee, and it is paid to the office, and I have read what the office does with money because I have been on the other side of a counter four times in a fortnight and a woman at that counter told me the rule about pails before I asked her once.**

"There is no money in this Hearthhold. There is not one coin in this building and I have looked, and if there was I would not touch it, and there is not. So fill in the paragraph and what I have got is a boy on a register for a year, and the office has my name, and the fee is not paid, and in a year the paragraph runs out and I am back to no bond and this time I have been on a roll and taken off it in front of people.

"And the other half is worse and it is the half I did not know until the Friday. **Iven Tallo would be the holder. He is the only man in this building who can supervise a trade. And if his name goes in that register then the man who is teaching me is the reason my trade exists, and in four years a boy in a guild office reads a register and sees whose name is on it, and he does not read the argument, and he never will.** So no. And I did not know on Friday that I was going to say that, and I have been carrying it about since the Friday and it has got heavier."

Iven Tallo looked at the floor of the school store for about four seconds.

"You have just refused a year's trade to keep me out of a book," he said.

"I have refused a year's trade to keep a fee out of a file," said Wen Pask, "and you out of it as well, and I do not mind which of those you think was the reason, and neither do you, and that is a thing we are both going to have to live with."

---

It was Tams Quill who found the thing, and he found it off the back of a refusal, which is the way he finds everything.

"Right," he said, from the doorway. "You have all been arguing about a paragraph and a page and a boy, and none of you has looked at the air engineer's page, and the air engineer's page is the oldest thing in this building after a wall.

"Nera Oduya's inventory. **Twelve lines.** Pump's bed, the school's two beech lengths, Bel's mother's floor, the wash rooms' rack, the middle gate's store, the others she asked and was told no. Twelve lines, and every one of them says *no*, and every one of them has got an owner against it and a reason against it, and four of them have got a condition as well, and I have read it four times and it is four.

"And under the twelve there is a **thirteenth** line, and it has been sitting under them since the tenth week, and I have read it out in this room twice and never once asked what it is a line *for*, and it says what the seat wanted, and there is no owner to put against it, and Jo has not written zero and will not write zero.

"So the page is: **a column for a thing, a column for an owner, a column for a reason, and in some lines a column for a condition.** Twelve lines answered in a form the form has. And the thirteenth line is not answered, and it has not been answered for six weeks, and it is the only line on that page that anybody in this building reads out loud every day at the fifth bell.

"And I have been assuming it was a material," said Tams Quill, "because it is on a page called an inventory, and it is not. **Read it back, Nera. It is your page. What does the thirteenth line want.**"

From the top of three steps, and she had not come down, and she was not going to:

"It wants a length of hardwood and a man who can cut it and a whole hand to feel it with, and the hardwood is a material and the man and the hand are not, and the column for an owner is a column for a person, and there is nothing wrong with a column for a person. **The problem is that a person is not an *owner*.** Line one is the pump's bed and the owner is the pump, which nobody owns. Line eight is a floor and the owner is a man, and that works, because a man can be an owner of a thing and it says *his*. **Line thirteen's owner would be a man, and then it would read *owned by*, and a man does not get owned, and so it cannot be written in that column, and it has been sitting there unwritten for six weeks, and the only reason I did not see it is that I have been reading that page as a list of materials, and it is not a list of materials. It is a list of what a building needs, and I wrote it as though the two were the same, and they are not, and the difference is a man, and I have no column for him.**"

---

"**Then there ought to be one,**" said Bel Ordry, from the back.

"There ought to be what."

"A column." Bel Ordry came forward and put his hand on the edge of the shelf, not on the boxes, and everybody in the store watched which of the two he did. "For a person. Not *owned by* — for a person. Who does it, not who has it. Because a building is a list of things and a building is kept by people, and the page has got a column that says *whose wood is this* and it has not got a column that says *whose work is this*, and everything difficult that has happened in this Hearthhold in six weeks has happened on the difference."

"That is the sentence of this month," said Iven Tallo.

"It is the sentence of this month and it is not mine," said Bel Ordry, "and I do not want it and I am not going to be thanked for it, so write it in my words or do not write it at all."

Dalla Sen wrote it in Bel Ordry's words and read it out, and Tams Quill said, before she had finished reading it:

"And there is a column on my board with nothing over the top of it, and a man of twenty-two has been in it for sixteen days and sixteen nights with his leg and his bundle, and there is a half hinge in it that belongs to a man two rooms above the clinic return and has been in it since the twelfth week, and it is the only column in this building with nothing over the top of it, and it is exactly the right size for what the air engineer has just said, and **I am not going to head it.**"

"Go on," said Dalla Sen, with the chalk up.

"Because the day I head it, it becomes a unit. And a unit is a thing a room can be praised in, and a room in this Hearthhold can be praised in exactly one way, which is by being low on it. And there is one line I am not going to have in a column in this building with a heading over it, and it is not his, and it is not hers, and it is not mine.

"**A wage is the only line in this book that must never be a thing a room can be praised in.** A wage is not low. A wage is the opposite of low. And the day that column has a heading, somebody in this building is going to be glad about it, and I will not be there when it happens."

---

Marek Venn stood in the doorway of the school store with a plan in his head and a lock he had never been given and understood, for the second time in three days, that the scarcest thing in this Hearthhold was not wood.

It had been a plank in the fifteenth week, and a hand in the twelfth, and a name in the fourteenth, and now it was a column on a form that had been written by a competent woman in the fourth week for the purpose of finding timber, and it could not say *the seat wants a man*, and because it could not say it, Jo Verrick's estimate of one has sat on a board in an anteroom for six weeks with nothing under it, and because nothing is under it, nobody can tell whether a cleaning has been paid for by a man or by a stroke, and that is not a small thing in a building where the whole argument is about whether the people and the beneficiary are the same people.

And what he had learned in the sixteenth week, in a kitchen and on a bench and in a doorway, was that every document in this Hearthhold had a place to put everything except a person. The clerk's book had columns for keepers, for watchers, for prices and refusals. The plan on the dowel had counts and conditions and no names. The inventory had owners and reasons and no column for a man. **And the one instrument a guild office would accept, which would have handed a boy of twenty-two a year's trade, required a name and a route and a register, and the only route in this building was the one thing that could not go in a file.**

So the boy's trade was held by a paragraph that needed a person, and the building's needs were held by a page with no column for a person, and a wage was held by a column with no heading that its keeper refused to head on principle, and the man who could have put his name on the form was standing in a school store with his hands behind his back because he did not know what he would say in the spring.

None of it was a shortage of wood. All of it was a shortage of a place to write a person down.

At the sixth bell Ilsa Norr locked the school store from the doorway and said that nobody had looked at the boxes and that the boxes were registers and that a register is a thing that is written in and not read, and that she had said so in this building nine days ago and would say it again.

Two guild refusals went in that day, in two hands, on one page, and Dalla Sen read them out at the fifth bell on the next day: **a carpenter who would not put his name in an office register to give a boy a trade, on the ground that he did not know what he would say in the spring; and the boy, who would not take the year's trade, on the ground that he could not pay the fee and would not have the man who was teaching him in a guild book.** And under them, because she had learned something in the last three weeks of it, the clerk wrote a third thing, and read it out twice, and it was the only sentence she had ever written in this building that was not a record of anything.

*That this Hearthhold cannot currently write down what it owes a man, and has now said so out loud, in a room, twice, in two hands, and does not know yet whether that is a failure or a description.*
