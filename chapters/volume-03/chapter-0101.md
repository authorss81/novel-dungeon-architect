# Chapter 101 — Under The School Floor

**The first day of the twentieth week was a Monday and the corridor was shut,** and the school did not run, and the only reason the school corner had an empty full morning in it was that nine children were somewhere else for the first time in three days.

Ilsa Norr found the water at the second bell, the way she finds everything, by counting what is not supposed to be there. She was on her knees at the low corner with her sleeve pushed back and her hand flat on a flag, and she counts children and not barrels.

It was not a puddle. That was the first thing three people tried to make it into and the first she would not let it be.

"**It is cold,**" she said. "**And it is wet, and it is not under the sink end, it is at the door end, and the door end is the end that is worn.**"

Then she sat down on the floor with the nine of them, because a woman who counts children does not stand over children while she works out where the water is, and Mikka Hale watched her do it and said nothing, which from the cook is a review. And she said it to a room, not to a man.

"**A schoolroom floor is a floor. I have been standing on the answer since the eleventh week.**"

---

At the low corner, which is the corner nearest the school door, the flagstones lie in a shallow dish about the width of a hand's span, and a dish that deep in a building like this surprises nobody. **Nine children had stood on it for twenty weeks, and a dish in a floor is not a fault. A dish is a use.** What was in it was not standing water. It was **dark**, in a way the stone two feet away was not, and where it crossed the joint between two flags the joint had a line of it in it, and the line was not straight.

"**Found,**" said Dalla Sen, with the chalk up, and she said it before anybody had said the word, because that is the order this room does things in and it has been the order since the eighteenth week. "**Found, located, tested, recorded, and not before. Somebody say where.**"

---

Jo Verrick knelt at the school door with the three-foot rule out of her apron, laid it across the corner twice, and did not have to be asked to say the thing a floor says when it is only doing one thing.

"**A finger and a half of fall to the door, and it did not get that way in a month. Nine children, three days a week, out of the same door for twenty weeks, and the stone under them going down where they go.** And it is not the weather: it has not moved with the weather in three days and I have been in this room in three weathers. The water is in the joint under the flags and not on top of them, and **four people have walked over that corner for twenty weeks and looked at it, and I am one of the four, and none of us looked at it, because a floor in a school is a floor.**"

Renn Vale said it from the landing, in the way she says things that are not about structure.

"**Then that is a wear surface and a wear surface is a use.** A thing in this building gets worn where it is used and not where it is admired, and I have watched that sill go shiny in the middle for twenty weeks and nobody has ever once asked it why."

Dalla Sen wrote it down and then stopped with the chalk up. "**I am going to write *wear* on a public page and I want somebody to object to the word before I write it, because I have learned that word in this building twice this month and I was wrong the first time.**"

There was no objection, which she entered, and then she went on to the next question, which is how this room finds out whether a word is any good.

---

Located took Jo Verrick twenty minutes, a lamp, and the low sound of somebody counting.

"**There is a bed joint under this floor.** Flag on bed joint, bed joint on a course, course on the fill, and it runs out from under the low corner away from the door, along the party wall, six feet, and comes up into the haunch behind the outer service door.

"**And the whole of it is nine inches below this floor at its lowest and it does not have to go up anywhere, because the water does not climb, it spreads, and a spreading thing finds the low side of a floor that nine children have worn dished toward a door.**"

---

Then the air engineer said the wrong thing, in public, in front of a room, and it was a reasonable wrong thing, and nobody consoled her.

"**No,**" said Nera Oduya, from three steps. "**That is not the seam. I will not have the seam put under a school floor in a book without somebody arguing it with me. The seam is a lime joint in a dressed stone haunch and this is a bed joint under a floor, and between them there is a course of stone, and a course of stone is what a course of stone is for. Water does not come through dressed stone because it wants to.**"

She had it in a sentence and the room waited, and Dalla Sen wrote it in her own hand exactly as it was said, and Jo Verrick went and got a lamp.

"**Air engineer,**" she said, from the party wall, four minutes later, "**come and look and do not touch. There is a stone out of that course and a stone back in it, and the one that is back in is in lime and the lime is a finger wide, and the bed joint runs out of the bottom of the patch and your bed joint is not in the haunch. The haunch is where the water comes out. The patch is where it comes in.**"

Nera Oduya came and looked at it and stood up again and said it in her own words, which the clerk wrote beside her own words.

"**Then the course is not continuous, and that is a fact about this building nobody in it knew, and I was wrong, and I want it entered that I was wrong and not softened, and that I said no before I looked, because today it cost a room forty minutes of a morning it did not have.**"

Dalla Sen entered both halves and repeated the strike she had already made, which is a thing she does.

---

Two fingers went onto the stone at the school-door end, and then she went along the wall on her knees, at a crawl, with her hand flat on the plaster, all the way to the anteroom door, in about ninety seconds, saying nothing while she did it.

"**It is the same line and it is not the same joint,**" she said. "**There is stone between them and forty feet of floor and a doorway, and I am telling you it is a line and not a hole, because that is the difference that matters today. The seam has not gone anywhere and has not increased. Same four threads, same leaf, same warm as yesterday.**"

She turned round. "**The seam has found the low side of a floor that nobody has looked at. That is a different sentence from *the seam has found a way out*, and I would like the room to hear the difference, because one of them is a problem and the other one is a nuisance.**"

"**Objection,**" said Mikka Hale, and she said the word she was striking out loud. "*Nuisance.* Take it out, and put in what it is, which is a wet floor in a schoolroom that nine children are going to stand on tomorrow, and put my name on it, because I am the one standing over it at the fifth bell asking whether anybody has dried it."

Struck, written in the cook's hand, and repeated.

---

Then the room found out where the water wanted to go, and it was the worst place in the building.

**The air engineer had said the water had a direction. She had not said it had a destination,** and when Jo Verrick asked her for one, at the fourth bell, with her knee still on the flags, Nera Oduya gave the answer she had worked out in ninety seconds of crawling along a wall, and it was wrong.

"**There is a fall to it,**" she said. "**The joint runs to the haunch and the haunch is dressed stone, and a joint that is wet will find a way down, and if we open the low corner we can lead it into the covered channel in the dip and the dip already takes the pump.**"

"**No,**" said Jo Verrick.

"The dip takes the pump—"

"**The dip takes the pump for six minutes after the lever comes off, and then the water stands in it two foot eight wide on the edge of eleven feet of shaft, and there is a chalk line on the frame of that pump that a woman in a sling drew and it has not moved.**" She folded the rule, four times an inch. "**There is nowhere to lead it that is not the worst place in this building, and if you open that corner and lead it down, you have made a drain into a room that fills.**"

She stood there.

"**Then it does not drain,**" she said. "**I have said a thing in a room that is not true and I am not going to take it back, because the first half is true and the second half is not, and a person who takes half of it back teaches the room to believe the other half. It is not a drain, this room is not a cistern, and I do not know what it is. I would like that written down in a clerk's hand without anybody being kind about it.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**in the air engineer's words, and I am not going to be kind about it either, because the clerk's job is not to be kind, it is to be right, and there are now two things in this building's book that the air engineer does not know and one of them is new.**"

Not one of them consoled her. Bel Ordry, standing at the school door with the two parts of the plan still in the store and not in his coat because there was nowhere in that room to keep a coat, looked at the band in the joint and said nothing at all, which for him was a position.

---

**Recorded** was where the fight was, and the fight was a preposition, and this room has fought about one before.

**On the page the clerk had it already:** *the water under the school corner's floor is from the seam in the anteroom's haunch.*

"**Strike *from*,**" said Nera Oduya. "**Write *in a joint that runs to the haunch*. *From* asserts a cause, and this page has a place and a patch in a course and three people's hands and no cause at all. If somebody in a year comes at this boundary about a wet floor, I want the page to make them go and find the patch themselves.**"

It was struck. It was replaced in her hand. Dalla Sen read the line and then the strike, because a clerk who repeats a correction without the strike has corrected nothing.

**And Mikka Hale then said the thing nobody had got to,** with her hand flat on the flag, and it is the reason the morning went as it went.

"**Now stop. All of you. Look at what we have just found.**

"**That is the water this boundary drinks.** Two jars a day, maybe three, out of a joint in the haunch behind our own service door. It is in my separate numbers and not in the pot and never has been, and it is the only drinking water anybody in this building has found.

"**And there are nine children who sit six feet from it.**"

---

Marek Venn got up off the step, and then he did the stupid thing, and he knew it while he was doing it.

"**Then cap it.**"

He said it to the room and not to anybody, and the room went the way a room goes.

"**Cap the seam. Not the floor. The seam.** Four threads and a leaf the size of a hand, and if we put a hard thing across the mouth of it we hold the water in the wall instead of losing it under a schoolroom floor, and the floor dries, and nobody has to move nine children anywhere."

Mikka Hale put her hand flat on the door frame and left it there.

"**Say the rest of it,**" she said.

"**The rest of it is that there is no mortar of the right kind here and no timber and there are two jars a day, maybe three, and that is all the water anybody in this building drinks.**"

"**Keep going.**"

"**The only thing that will hold against that water is more clay, and clay shrinks as it dries, and a cap that shrinks takes the water with it when it lets go. It does not seal. It closes the mouth for as long as the clay is wet, and then it comes off the joint and the joint is wider than it was.**"

"**Yes,**" said the cook. "**And then where does the water go.**"

"**Into the floor. Into the bed joint. Into the haunch. Back where it was, and now with a mark on the wall that a stranger can see.**"

"**And the pot.**"

Marek Venn stood in the middle of the school corner with his left hand at his side and worked out the last ten seconds of it in front of eleven people, because they were not going to wait.

"**The two jars are your separate numbers,**" he said, "**and you said that out loud in this room for a reason. So if I cap the seam, the pot does not go up. The pot stops being topped. It is ten today and it is not going to be eleven tomorrow, and you would find that out at the fifth bell, on your own board, in your own words, and not from me.**"

She took her hand off the frame.

"**That is the whole of it,**" she said. "**Say the rest of it to the room, because the room did not hear you say it, the room heard a man talk about clay.**"

"I did not say that part out loud."

"**You said it three times in your head and once in the middle, and I have been listening to you in your head for five months, and you must do that less.**"

Renn Vale came down one step, which she does when she is going to hold a term.

"**I will add the other half so the room has both. You have not proposed a bad fix. You have proposed the ordinary fix, and it is right for a wall and wrong for this wall, and the reason is that a joint with water in it is carrying something and this boundary has never once found out what.** You cap a joint you cannot account for and you have changed a load path. **And you did it on a Monday morning in a schoolroom, and the whole of what I have been arguing for since the sixth week is that a change to a room people use is written by the person who will keep it. I keep this room's floor. I have not been asked.**"

The room said nothing, and then Marek Venn said the thing that had been in it since the fourth day of the nineteenth week, and he said it to the four walls of a schoolroom with nine children not in it.

"**Then I have nothing left but the one thing I cannot do twice,**" he said. "**I have said in this building that anybody who comes and asks me to read a boundary is going to be told no, and I meant it, and I have run out of ordinary answers four days earlier than I thought, and the only thing I have left is that, and I am not going to use it on a floor.**"

Dalla Sen wrote nothing for about four seconds, which is the longest she has ever sat still, and then she wrote, and read it out.

"**Entered: that a man said in the school corner on the first day of the twentieth week that he had nothing left but the one thing he cannot do twice, and that the clerk did not understand it and is not going to guess in a book what it means, and that if it turns out to be a thing she can say in a year, somebody must say it for her.**"

Renn Vale said, "**That is the fourth time this building has written down a person saying they do not understand something, and it is the first time it has been the clerk, and she is the only one of us who has never needed it.**"

And then, with nothing left, he did the thing he had been doing for a fortnight, and everybody in that room knew what it was before he said it.

"**Then take the crew's hours.**"

"**No,**" said Renn Vale, without moving. "**You asked me that on Friday and I said no, and you have not asked me since. That column is a quantity of asking and you are not asking, and if you were, you would have to say the terms out loud and name a person who can be told no. What you want is a favour, and I have told you what a favour is. Take your hand down.**"

He took his hand down, and the clerk entered that, and nobody said anything.

---

They did not cap the seam, and they did not put a drop of that floor in anything, and no closure was added to anything that day.

What they did instead was decide, in a room, with the cost named out loud, to do nothing until the fourth bell on the second day. And Marek Venn said the sentence the week is argued from, out loud, in the wrong order, which is his habit.

"**Then the floor is the problem and not the water. We are not going to move the water. We are going to get nine children off a dished floor and we are going to do it before Wednesday, because Wednesday is a corridor-open day and Wednesday is the last day that school runs in this room.**"

Then he sat down again, and eleven people let him, and the clerk wrote the decision and the cost of it in the same line, and the cost is that **the school does not run here tomorrow either**, and nobody found a way to make that not be the sentence.

---

At the fifth bell he went up to the haunch and put his left palm flat into it, and it was warm, exactly as warm as Jo Verrick had said it would be, and that mattered because the thing has not changed in the time since somebody last touched it, and the only way anybody in this building has ever found out which one of two things they are holding is by going back.

He got nothing. Four degrees, which is about what the wall is at that height, a hand's breadth, the loudest thing in the anteroom, for the twelfth time in his life, and it took forty seconds instead of ten.

"**Twelve,**" said Tams Quill, with the chalk in his left hand and his right one open and flat in his lap. "Ordinary column, your own hand, the haunch, the temperature, the reason. **And I am not moving it, and that is the fourth time I have not moved one of yours, and I am not going to change it in a room where a woman has just knelt on a floor.**"

"Why is it the ordinary column," said Bel Ordry, who is the man who is going to be asked.

"**Because he was asked for it,**" said Tams Quill. "**A woman told him where to put his hand, the same as the tenth, and the tenth is in the ordinary column and so is this. The column with no heading is for things that came and got nothing when he went and found them on a cold wall in the dark on his own. Nine in mine, three in his, twelve in all.**"

Marek Venn wrote his own entry in his own left hand in the ordinary column, because the right one cannot hold a pencil flat and has not since a jack in a dry service tunnel a long way above his head, and he was aware of the room watching him do it and he let them, and that is the fourth time.

Then he sat on the anteroom step with that hand open on his knee and did an arithmetic that had nothing to do with the board. Twenty weeks ago he was the only man here who could lay his hand on a wall and be useful. There has been exactly one other such man since, and she found the patch in the course with a lamp in four minutes. **Neither is on the rota, neither can stand a watch, and they have more of this building in their hands than he does.** He had spent twenty weeks building a case for being the man who goes, and the case had got weaker every week, and he had not noticed because he had been the one doing the going.

---

**The costs were named out loud and were not the same twice.**

Renn Vale's was a room. "**The corridor is shut and the school is not running, and that is the only reason this corner has an empty morning. If we spend it looking at a floor, we spend the morning we have got.**"

And Ilsa Norr's was the only one nobody had thought of, and she gave it at the sixth bell, standing in her own floor with her shoes wet through.

"**Then what is the school going to do on Wednesday.**"

The room did not try to answer her.

Then Dalla Sen read the board out at the sixth bell, and the column with no heading read the way it reads: a leg, a bundle, **thirty-six days and thirty-six nights**, a half hinge, a line refused, a page gone out of sight, and a wage, and the count still does not head it and nobody in that room has asked him to for four batches.

**At the sixth bell the school corner stood empty with the lamp in it,** and nine children would have been in it on Wednesday, and the boundary had a week to find them a room. The mouth in the shut wall above them was warm, shut for twenty weeks, going into the throat and not out of it, and nobody opened it.

**There are two other rooms nine children could be taught in, this boundary owns neither of them, and one of them holds the plan.**
