# Chapter 75 — A Supervising Holder

Wen Pask came up the middle passage on the Friday of the thirteenth week with a bundle on his back and a fold of guild paper in his inside coat, and he was twenty-two and had been apprenticed for three years and he had walked four days to say four sentences.

Nobody went down the nine steps to meet him. They met people at the bottom, and he came up them, and he stopped about two thirds of the way because of the leg, which had gone at the service bend on the way up and which he did not explain, and Tams Quill watched from the bench at the top of the steps and entered that in the column with no heading because it was a leg and it was a person and there was no column for it.

"**Wen Pask**, out of the middle gate's store, three years to Iven Tallo, and I have walked four days and I have got four sentences and I would like to say them in order in a room with him in it.**"

"Named and entered," said Dalla Sen, "on his own authority, no watch, no rota, and no date given to him, and say the four sentences, and I will write them as you say them and not tidy them."

---

They were these.

"One. The office has read a master's suspension into the roll. That was nine days ago and there is a date on it and I know the date because it is on the paper.

"Two. An apprentice's bond is his master's bond until he holds his own, and it is not a separate instrument, it is the same instrument with a second name on it, and that is the trade's oldest rule and it is a good rule and I have been on the receiving end of it for three years and I have never once thought about it.

"Three. **My bond went with his on the ninth day, and I have got no bond, and a man with no bond cannot be entered on a register, cannot be paid trade work, and cannot be signed against a route by anybody in this Hearthhold or any other.**"

"Four," said Wen Pask, "is that I have got a piece of paper and I did not know I would need to bring it up nine steps myself, and there is nobody at the middle gate to send with it, and I have thought about that on the road and I have decided it is because the middle gate is a place where a thing like this happens to a person and everybody is busy."

He put the fold of paper on the bench and did not open it, and Dalla Sen did not open it, and it sat on the bench in an anteroom for about a minute while the pump went round under the floor.

Then Iven Tallo said, "Give it to me," and the room did not remark on the fact that a man who had spent a fortnight refusing to touch anything in this building had reached across a bench in under two seconds.

---

He read it standing up, in his own two hands, both of them, and it took him about ninety seconds, and he read the whole thing out loud at the end, and he read it the way he reads a price board, which is as though every word has to be said twice in the mouth before it is allowed to be true.

"I am going to read the whole of it, and then I am going to read the fourth paragraph again, and the room will want to argue with the fourth paragraph and I want it argued with after, not during.

"It is a notice, on guild paper, with a seal on it that I recognise and a number I recognise, and it is addressed to *the master, apprentice, and to the master's surety*. It says that the suspension of Iven Tallo's bond of the nineteenth year has been read into the roll on the ninth day of this month. It says the apprentice is therefore out of the trade until his own bond is issued or his master's is reinstated. It says he may not be entered on a register, may not be paid at trade rates, and may not be put against a route.

"**And then the fourth paragraph.** Which I am reading again.

"*An apprentice under a suspended master may continue in the trade for a period not exceeding one year under the supervision of a named holder entered against a named route, in a register kept by the office, on payment of the annual supervision fee.*"

He put it down.

"The annual supervision fee," said Jo Verrick, from the frame, "is money, Iven."

"It is four pounds and it is money," Iven agreed, "and it is the least of it, and Dalla, read the paragraph back and then somebody say the other half out loud, because I have been staring at it since the Thursday and I did not have the rest of it until this morning.

"**The named holder is entered against a named route.** Not a man who supervises. A man who *holds a route*. So the trade has looked at this situation and decided that the way to keep a boy in a trade is to find somebody who owns a piece of ground and put his name in a book next to it, and I want everybody in this room to sit with that for about four seconds before anybody says anything, because **that is the charter. That is the whole of the charter, that I have been arguing about for thirteen weeks, on one line, in a notice about a boy, and I have never once seen it in a form I could read.**"

"Entered," said Dalla Sen, "in the carpenter's words, in his own hand, with the time on it, and I am reading the notice into the book as a paper that came up nine steps on a man's back and not as a finding, because I want the page in a year to be able to tell the difference between what this boundary decided and what was sent to it."

---

Nobody in that anteroom had heard Wen Pask say anything at all yet, and he had been in the room for about ten minutes, and Dalla Sen went and stood by him, which she does.

"You have not said anything about yourself," she said. "Do not let this room take your four sentences and start having a meeting."

"I have got a fourth-day road and a leg," said Wen Pask, "and I would rather be useful, honestly."

"Then say what you came up nine steps to say to him," said Dalla Sen, "and say it to him."

Wen Pask looked at Iven Tallo for a while, and he was twenty-two and he had a man of thirty-six in front of him who had taught him everything he knew and had spent thirteen weeks in a room a floor above a broken pump teaching a Hearthhold the same things.

"I want to say the thing you said to me in the store in the winter before I came," he said, "when I asked why we did not sign the gate book. You said: *a page in a file is how a Hearthhold ends up owned.* And I wrote it down. I have got it on a bit of paper in my other pocket and I have read it on this road four days' worth of walking, and I want you to know that it is the reason I can say what I am going to say now.

"**I have been suspended by a page in a file.** By a sentence in a paragraph, read into a roll, nine days ago, in an office I have never been in, about a suspension that is about a lip with a woman under it. And I did not know it was about that. I went and found out on the road, from a woman at a counter, who told me the date and the number and did not have to look at a book, and that is the whole of my trade for three years, gone, in a morning, by an entry.

"So I am not angry at you," said Wen Pask. "I want to be careful about that, because everybody in this building is going to assume I am angry at you, and I am not, and if I am not then that is worse, because it means the argument holds. **The argument holds.** I have been suspended by the argument, by the same argument, in the same words, made by the same man, and it did not even have to be him to do it."

"Yes," said Iven Tallo.

"Yes?"

"**Yes. It holds. I said it, you wrote it down, and it has cost a twenty-two-year-old boy his trade in nine days and I am not going to stand in this room and be told I was wrong, because I was not wrong about the page.**"

He turned round and put both hands on the edge of the bench.

"And I am also not going to be forgiven, and I know that nobody in this room is going to try, and I know that Mikka Hale in particular is going to stand on that frame for the next four minutes looking like she wants to do something, and **do not.** If somebody in this building makes this easier for me today then the last thirteen weeks have been about nothing.

"So here is the cost of my sentence, in the order it arrives, and it is not one thing, and I have counted.

"The first is him, and he is standing there, and I have got nothing to give him and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

"The second is that the middle's store will not release a stick of timber on my word, because the timber is released against a master's bond, and it is line eleven on the air engineer's page and it is four days away and it is four hundred paces of seasoned hardwood for a sill, and a courier stood on a bench in the tenth week and withdrew her own order and it cost the middle the timber that would put its water-lock right. **So that store was already spoken for by a person who walked out of here. Now it is spoken for twice, because of a paragraph.** And nobody in this Hearthhold is getting a plank, and the reason has moved from *a shortage of reach* to *a shortage of permission*, and I do not know which of those is worse and I have been in this business for thirty-one years and I know."

"It is worse," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel, "and I will tell you why it is worse, and it is not the money. Reach is a road and a road can be argued about. **Permission is a person, and a person can be found.**"

---

"And the third," said Iven Tallo, "and this is the one I have been holding since the Friday morning, and I put it in the room because it is going to get worse on its own if I do not.

"Wen. I want to ask you what you want, and I want you to say it out loud in front of the clerk, and I am going to make it hard for you, and I am sorry about that in advance, and I am not going to ask you a question I would not ask the keeper."

The boy took a minute. Dalla Sen wrote the minute.

"I want to work here," said Wen Pask. "For nothing. On your page. I know it is not a guild page and I know it is about work and it is kept separate from the route's, and I have heard about that in four days' walking and a woman at a counter, and I would like to be on a page with no name on it in a file forty days away, and I would like it very much."

"**No.**"

The anteroom did not expect it from him and neither did Marek Venn.

"Sit down, and hear the reason, and it is not a small one, and it is the reason you are going to want to hate me for about a year and I would rather you had it now.

"That page is the crew's page. Five names on it. And every one of us is leaving in the spring, and there is a written obligation on it, and the end of that obligation is that the boundary can do the work without us and **a person named by the boundary has said so out loud, in the room, with the reason.** And that has not happened, and nobody has said it, and it is not going to happen this month.

"So if I put you on that page I have got a page with a twenty-two-year-old on it that is going to empty out in the spring, and I would have to tell you that on the day I put you on it, and you would say yes anyway, because you are twenty-two, and in four years a clerk in an office would find a route that was once kept by a man named Tallo and after him by a boy named Pask, and there would be a name on it, and **I would have taught a boy the sentence and then broken it for him inside eleven weeks, in the same building, four doors from where I learned it.**

"And the second reason, and it is worse, and it is the reason a woman at a frame will tell you the same as I am telling you if you ask her: you would be here on a favour. On an unpaid boy's labour for a man he is fond of, in a building that cannot pay anybody. **And I have spent three weeks in this anteroom watching a cook invent an instrument out of the opposite of that, and a belt of a man with a bad hand be offered a floor and refuse to be thanked for it, and a woman on a string stand at five in the morning three days a week for nothing, and every one of them is carrying a debt with no date on it, and I am not adding a thirteenth to this building's list. Not you. Not on my page.**"

Wen Pask stood in the middle of the anteroom floor and did not say anything for a while, and the pump went round, and the check hissed on its one note.

"Right," he said. "Then what do I do with four days of walking."

"Wen," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel, "I am going to say the one thing a keeper can say, and it is small, and I have been in this building thirteen weeks and it is the only sentence I have got.

"**You are entered in this book as a person who is not paid, not on a rota, not on a page of work, and not owed.** Dalla, in your own hand: *a man of this boundary, arrived, refused a trade, refused by a work, and kept.* And that is the whole of what I can offer, and it is not a wage and it is not a bed, and I am not going to pretend it is either, and you can go back down the nine steps and up the middle passage tonight with a full trade and a name at the middle gate's counter, and nobody in this room will say a word against it.

"And if you stay, then in six weeks you will be the only pair of hands in here that is not somebody's," said Mikka Hale, from the frame. "And I am going to say that out loud because it is the most selfish thing I have said all week. There will be a job. It will not pay. **It will be on a book in my kitchen in my own hand, and I will write down what it is and what it costs and I will read it out on Fridays, and it will not be a favour, because I have decided this week that a favour with a date on it is not a favour.**"

Wen Pask looked at the anteroom floor for a long time.

"The middle gate's water-lock is dry," he said, "and there is four hundred paces of seasoned hardwood in that store, and a man with a crutch lives upstairs from a clinic and he has offered a floor and been thanked for it and refused the thanks. And I have got a bundle on my back and a leg and no bond, and you have all just told me there is no work."

"Yes," said Dalla Sen.

"Then I will be a man of this boundary who is not paid, not on a rota, not owed, and not useless," said Wen Pask. "And I will sleep in the school store and I will not put a thing in it, and I will tell Ilsa Norr that in the morning and she will say no, and I will be in the corridor in the morning."

"You will be in the corridor in the morning," said Ilsa Norr, from the school door, and it was the first time anybody had heard her speak to him and she did it in four words and went back up the steps.

---

Marek Venn stood at the end of the tally board through all of it with his right hand in his pocket and his left hand flat on the wood, and the thing he was not going to say was the following, and he said none of it, and the not-saying took him most of the afternoon.

In the eleventh week of the twelfth he had filled one of three boxes on a guild form in his head in four seconds, with no name in it, and had said so out loud in a room, and had asked for the rule to go in the book. In the twelfth week a woman forty days down a road had put a form flat on a bench so that a form on a table would be a form. In the thirteenth week, four days up the middle passage, a boy of twenty-two had been carried off a trade by an **entry in a roll**, and the entry had been made by an office about a suspension, and the suspension was about a document this boundary had declined to fill in.

**There was no name in the box, and the boy is off the roll anyway.** He had assumed, for eleven weeks, that the danger was a name. That a page was dangerous because a person could be written to it. And it turned out that the page had not needed him at all. The page had needed a *date*, and a *sentence*, and somebody forty days away with a stamp and no reason to come and look.

He put his slate down on the shelf, blank side up, for the fifth time in twelve days, and did not write on it, and Dalla Sen did not ask him what he had wanted to write, because she had stopped asking that in the ninth week and it was one of the two worst things she had ever had to stop doing.
