# Chapter 18 — The Stair in Someone’s House

The pressure change left a mark on the Hearthholds’ map before anyone thought to redraw it.

Nera recorded the wet seam in the maintenance ledger, and Sefu added a line to the boundary record: the service bypass was not to be used as an air shortcut during a stair-pressure turn. The returning stair had been a mapped hazard since the crew first reached the anteroom. Now the wheel could be heard through a working route, and the old school-and-infirmary marks had become part of a room people lived beside.

On the next attended maintenance trip, Sefu allowed the six-person crew to return as far as the stair’s fixed first landing.

“No one crosses the slab,” he said. “No one tests the moving part with a body. The gate stays under my control, and Nera keeps the air route separate.”

Renn Vale came as the resident guide. She had not offered a history of the stair, but she had heard Marek describe its upper flight and lower receiver the night before. When the crew reached the service bend, she walked ahead without being asked.

“It is called the school step,” she said.

Iven looked at her. “The guild record calls it a returning stair fault.”

“That is what the surveyors wrote after they stopped using it. Before that, the children crossed the upper flight and the litters took the lower side.”

Marek watched the stair from the fixed landing. The visible slab still ran in its rails across the stone throat. The rope, wheel, and counterweight remained where the earlier map had placed them. The missing receiver was a broken socket below the lower receiving landing, and the pressure-fed seam crossed the wall beside the upper flight.

A faint movement ran through the slab. No person was on it. The pressure seam shifted, the counterweight dropped a finger, and the stone returned toward its first position.

Renn did not look surprised. “It used to do that when the school below was empty.”

“What is below it now?” Leena asked from the litter.

Renn pointed toward a seam in the wall. “A store room, a wash room, and a way to the old clinic corner. The rooms changed. The stair kept its instructions.”

Marek unfolded the copied map. The old school-refuge proportions were there, but the route he had followed through the Cinder Steps did not show the neighboring rooms clearly. He had treated the stair as a mechanism to map. Renn described it as a piece of a neighborhood’s memory.

“The lower receiver was removed,” Iven said. “The guild record says it was taken out under a clearance after the stair failed an inspection.”

“After the inspection,” Renn repeated. “Not before the children used it.”

Leena’s left hand rested on the litter rail. “The guild record is not wrong because the residents remember a different use.”

“No,” Iven said. “But the two records do not tell us why the receiver is missing. A removed part can mean a repair, a theft, a decision to stop carrying a load, or a decision to hide what the load was doing.”

Marek looked at the broken socket. “The lower route was meant to balance the walking route. If the receiver was removed, the slab still asks for a return load that cannot arrive.”

Renn nodded. “And the clinic had nowhere else to send a person who could not walk.”

The statement made the stair larger than a fault in a wall. It had once been a way for children, walking patients, and stretchers to leave the same building without sharing a landing. Now its mechanism repeated the unfinished separation.

Pell shifted on the bench at the side of the fixed landing. His crutch was beside him, and the splinted ankle rested on a folded strap. “The upper side would fit one person,” he said.

“It would fit a person who can cross without changing the balance,” Marek said. “That is not you today.”

“I could cross slowly.”

“You could also be the reason the slab moves before the rest of us are ready.”

Pell’s face tightened. “I am not a pack.”

“No,” Marek said. “That is why the old route matters. The dead pack did not move it. A walking person did.”

The answer was kinder than a refusal and harder to bear. Pell looked toward the lower flight, where the missing receiver waited beneath the stone throat.

Leena said, “If we put a litter on that lower side, the old balance might change.”

“And if we do not, the stair keeps the difference,” Iven said.

Marek’s left hand found the fixed rail. The pressure beneath it was faint, then gone. He had the same brief sense of a load shifting through the wall, but it gave him no instruction beyond the fact that the stair and the service shaft were sharing a condition. The copper taste of certainty he wanted was not there.

Sefu stepped between the group and the movable slab. “We have enough to restrict it. We do not have enough to repair it.”

Marek looked at him. “The map might help someone repair it later.”

“The map helps someone understand what they are dealing with. It does not give them permission to move the slab or put a person on the lower route.”

Renn pointed to the old painted arrows. “My teacher marked the upper flight for anyone who could walk. The lower mark was for a stretcher. The children were taught not to run because the landing could move.”

“Who maintained it?” Marek asked.

“Different people. The school staff, the clinic workers, and whoever was near the wheel when the pressure changed. The guild did not come for every turn.”

Iven looked down. “That is why the guild record makes the receiver look like a missing part. Parts are easier to list than shifts.”

Sefu turned to Renn, who had brought a small repair slate from Dalla. “Record the local history as testimony, not as a survey correction.”

Renn took the slate. “The upper flight carried walking people. The lower receiver carried the stretcher side. The school and infirmary used them as separate exits. The guild says the lower receiver was removed. I remember it being used before the removal. I do not remember who ordered the break or why.”

The admission was incomplete in the right way. It gave Marek a human history without giving him a solution.

Oren moved to the fixed side of the stair and checked the bearing stone. Tams secured a line to the fixed rail without touching the movable slab. The line was not a brace. It marked the area that must remain empty and gave anyone approaching from the service bend a visible boundary.

Nera watched the air cloth at the stair-side grille. The stair pressure cycle was not currently pushing into the clinic return, but the connection remained physical. “Keep the grille closed during a turn,” she said. “If the cloth moves, the stair is sharing the air condition.”

Sefu took a length of black cord from the boundary stores. He tied it across the fixed upper approach, low enough to catch a person but loose enough to remove for a recorded inspection. The cord did not touch the slab, rope, wheel, or counterweight. A Hearthhold mark beside it recorded the restriction in simple strokes: *walking load repeats return. No crossing. No litter.*

Marek watched the cord take the place of a decision he had not yet made. He had wanted to map the stair for a future route. The map existed. The route did not.

Leena looked at the broken receiver. “We are closing it because the lower side cannot be certified.”

“Among other reasons,” Sefu said. “We are closing it because the stair sits in rooms people use, and no one has the right to make those rooms carry a test they did not consent to.”

Nera touched the air cloth. “And because the next pressure turn could pull the stair condition into the clinic return.”

Iven added, “The guild may call this a faultform. The local use is evidence of a function that failed. The two descriptions can exist together.”

Marek wrote the condition in the copied map: *visible movable slab; stone throat; pressure-fed seam; rope, wheel, counterweight; missing lower receiver; walking human load repeats return; dead pack does not; no crossing; no litter; restricted pending repair and public maintenance terms.*

He added one more line: *return after a public maintenance decision, local history review, and a safe way for an injured person to leave.*

The phrase was not elegant. It was necessary. Without it, the restriction would look like fear rather than a recorded condition with a future task.

Pell looked at the black cord. “So we are not using it.”

“Not today,” Sefu said.

“Or until someone fixes the receiver.”

“Or until we know what fixing it would do to the rooms around it.”

The stair gave a low sound beneath the floor. The slab moved a little, returned, and pulled the rope tight. No one crossed the cord.

When the crew withdrew to the service bend, the returning stair remained behind them, visible through the bend but no longer part of their route. Its mechanism was not defeated. It had been named, restricted, and left for a future decision that the present people could not make for the rooms above it.

Marek carried the map with the new restriction written across the stair’s edge.

This time, the line did not point toward an opening.

It pointed toward the work required before anyone could call the stair a route.
