# Chapter 25 — When the Gallery Loads

The empty room passed everything, in front of witnesses, on a Thursday morning before anyone had been hurt.

Marek inspected it first because that was his job and because he wanted it on the record that he had. The splice held under his hand and answered his weight with nothing. The hardwood wedges at the post feet were tight. The four low slots were cut back open and propped, so that the void beneath the new spread could breathe. The chalk lines he had laid at dawn were still crisp at eight o'clock, which meant nothing had moved and no one had walked where he had told them not to walk.

Jo went over it with the plumb line and read twelve courses up and four across. "Twice, and take the worse answer," she said, and got the same answer twice.

Iven looked at the splice for a long time and said nothing at all, which Dalla recorded, because the absence of a carpenter's objection was the only evidence available.

Nera ran the warm stone under the high grille and watched the cloth at the shaft mouth, and then again on the deck, and again with Oren standing on the boards over the third slot, and the result was the same as it had been two days before: the room could be made to breathe in an empty state, and nothing at all could be said about what it would do with thirty people.

"Empty inspection is not a room test," she said, and wrote it on the plan, and signed under it.

Sefu counted the boundary watches in person. Air valve, upper landing, clinic return, civic wheel, and the bell at the threshold. Renn on the landing with the lantern, Mikka at the clinic return with a clean cloth, Dalla at the threshold with the rope and the ledger, himself at the wheel, Nera at the valve. Five, all filled, and the room was declared open.

"Thirty on the deck, six in the corridor including the litter, and the five at the boundary," Sefu said. "Forty minutes. Announced. Go."

They went up single file with a hand line, and thirty adults arranged themselves in two rows and an aisle where a class would sit, and the litter came up last, four minutes, timed by Leena out loud at both ends.

For the first nineteen minutes nothing happened, and this was the part nobody had planned for and the part that made the test expensive.

The pump ran its twenty-six strokes and stopped for its six minutes, and the water came up a notch in the corridor's dip, and Pell called the mark from the stair head with his slate on his knees and his splinted ankle on the blanket, and the mark rose exactly where he said it would. The air cloth held. The test loads sat where Oren had set them and did not slide. Mikka's pot boiled over at minute twenty-one and she stopped the test, because she had said in writing that it would stop, and the count came down by one and Dalla wrote the number and the time and the number went back up when the pot was dealt with.

A man called Beraun — nobody had asked him to be there and nobody had the standing to send him away, which Dalla noted as a separate line — came up to the door of the gallery, looked in at thirty strangers on a school floor, and said that the room had a different smell than it used to, and went back down.

At minute twenty-four, Ilsa called the first class movement.

"Now," she said. "Everyone to the shelf side, the way I would move them to look at the slate. Three steps, together, no talking."

Thirty people took three steps toward the outer wall. The deck answered. Marek watched the chalk line at the second bay stay where it was, and the line at the fourth bay stay where it was, and felt nothing at all in the stone under his left palm, and wrote down in his own notes that at twenty-four minutes the room had shown no movement under a single class movement.

At minute twenty-six, Ilsa called the second one.

"Back to your places, and then forward again to the shelf. Two goes. That is what happens when you have finished a reading."

That was the mistake, and it was the ordinary kind, and it belonged to a teacher and not to an engineer.

Thirty people turned around and walked thirty steps in the same direction at the same time, and the deck took it. Marek heard it in the boards before he saw it: not a crack, but a change in the sound the deck made when a foot came down, from wood on wood to wood on something hollow. He put his hand on the rail and the pull came up through his forearm, brief and relative, and told him nothing, as it never did, and by the time his fingers had closed the chalk line at the third bay was broken.

Not cracked. Broken, from the inside, in one piece, the way a line breaks when the two ends of it go apart.

"Stop the forward movement," he said, and thirty people stopped, which was to their credit and not to the room's, because the damage was already in the post line and not in the walking.

The post at the third bay had moved. Its foot had come up out of the kerb on the corridor side by the width of a fingernail, and the low parapet above it — a course of old stone that had been holding the outer edge of the deck for a hundred years without anybody thinking about it — had dropped into the gap and gone over.

It fell about eleven feet.

It went into the lower corridor, not far along, and it hit the waist.

The sound that came up the stair was not dramatic. It was a single flat crack, and then a second crack underneath it, and then a long grinding slide, and then the specific noise of a heavy stone landing on a floor that people were standing on.

Mikka called the clinic clear. Renn called the door clear. Dalla rang the bell once, hard, for the corridor, and the corridor party stopped where the plan said they would stop, at the waist, and did not move past it.

Marek got to the stair head and looked down and the rest of his life divided at that point.

The stone had come down across the waist and taken the lintel of the school door with it. The door still hung on its lower hinge, and the head above it had dropped a hand's width and stayed hanging there, cracked through and hanging. The clear width of the school door, which had been four feet, was now about two and a half.

In the corridor below, Leena was on the litter against the school-side wall with a slab of stone dust on the sling, and Oren was on his knees beside her with his hands on the frame, and Tams was six feet away holding the hand line with both hands and no plan. Jo was at the waist with her back to the litter and her hands up, which was the correct place to stand and no use at all. Below the stair, on the corridor floor, the fallen parapet lay across the full width of the dip, and the drain channel ran under it, and the water from the pump's rest interval was already finding a way around the end of it.

Above them, thirty people stood on a deck whose outer joist had visibly dropped, so that the floor now ran down toward the wall in a slope that was impossible to have walked on and had been walked on.

Nobody had been hurt. That was the first fact and it did not last, because the second fact was that the school door was the only exit in the test, and the litter had to go through it, and the litter was four feet long, and the door was two and a half.

"Marek," Leena said, from the litter, "I want to be the first thing out of that door and I am not going to be the first thing through a two-and-a-half-foot gap with a stone lintel over my head."

"You are going to be the first thing out of the room," Marek said. "Through the door, in order, one at a time, and I will be under the lintel."

"You cannot hold a lintel with that hand."

"No. Oren can. Tams can. They are standing there."

The wheel turned below the floor.

Nobody had called it and nobody needed to. The wet seam along the edge of the service well opened the way a lip opens, the air cloth above the air valve lifted and fell, and every person in the boundary watches went to the post they had been assigned, because that was the fifth thing they had practiced and the practice was the only reason the anteroom did not also become a casualty.

"Water at the second notch and climbing," Pell called from the stair head. "Third notch in about four minutes at this rate."

Nera, from the valve, with her hand on the stop handle: "The stair turn is with us. The corridor's relief is gone because the door head has dropped. I have no air I can promise in that corridor while the pump rests."

So the room failed three ways in the same four minutes, and none of the three was a monster.

Ilsa's voice came down the stair, and it did not shake, which Marek would remember for a long time afterward. "Nobody moves on my floor. Stand where you are. Do not go to the stair."

Thirty adults stood on a sloping school floor in the dark and did not go to the stair, because a teacher had told them in plain words not to, on a Thursday, hours earlier, in a plan they had all read.

The work took nineteen minutes and it is the worst hour of Marek's life to date.

They unloaded the deck first, because a loaded deck with a kicked post was a deck that could go. Ilsa walked thirty people off in single file at the stair head, one at a time, at the pace of a class leaving a room, and Iven stood at the top with his hand on the rail and his other hand on her arm, and nobody hurried, and it took eleven of the nineteen minutes. Tams handed sacks down to Oren two at a time and Oren set them on the corridor floor and not on the dip, and the deck came up about half an inch as the load left it and then settled, and the third post stayed where it had gone.

Jo took the wedge set to the waist and worked the two shoring lengths in under the kicked post foot, hands, no lever, in the time it took Ilsa to get twenty-two people down a stair. Tams put his shoulder against the school-door head at the same time and held it while the wedges went in, and the head stopped grinding down and hung there, cracked, four inches lower than it had started, which was the whole difference between a repair and a hole.

Leena went through the gap second, after the first two people, on the litter turned endwise with a person at each front corner, and the litter cleared by a finger on each side. She said the number out loud as she went, because she was still timing things, and Dalla wrote it down afterward and did not know what to do with it and put it in the record anyway.

The water reached the sill at minute forty-one. Jo had the pump back on it at minute forty, seated in the housing, Tams on the lever, and the check knocked twice and drew. Nera held the air route and said, once, "This is an attended start under a stopped test, and I am the one who allowed it, and the reason is a body of water at a sill." She wrote her name under it.

When the dust had settled and the water had stopped rising, Marek went back up the fourteen treads alone, and stood on the gallery deck, and put his left hand flat on the third joist.

The deck gave a small, honest, ordinary answer. The chalk line at bay three was broken. The bay four line was intact. The splice had not broken; the splice had held, and the thing that had failed was four feet to one side of it, where the splice's neighbour had been supposed to be.

Iven said what he had said two days earlier, without any satisfaction in it at all.

"The crowd does not stand still," he said. "It walks. You spliced the middle of the floor and put a crowd on it, and the floor did what floors do."

"I know," Marek said.

"Write it in those words."

Marek could not write, so he said it, and Leena wrote it from the litter with her left hand while Oren held the slate: *the upper deck failed under ordinary repeated movement, not under a single extreme load. The splice held. The unspliced joists adjacent to the post line did not.*

Then he came back down and gave the order he had known he would have to give since the chalk line broke, and he gave it before anyone asked him for it, which was the only part of it he was proud of.

"The gallery is closed," he said. "Today. Not reopened for a survey, not opened for a class, not opened for a look. The stair head goes across tonight, and the stair head stays across until the deck over that post line is taken up or rebuilt on something we can see, and until a person who has to live with the answer says so, not me."

Sefu nodded and did not soften it. "Recorded as closed by the proposer. That is a different sentence from closed by me, and it is the better one, because it means you did not need me to tell you."

The costs came out in the next hour, and Dalla wrote all of them because that was her job.

The Hearthhold standing timber was gone. Three lengths were in the splice, two were under the post feet, one was under the school-door head, and the bundle of split lath was under the deck spread with half of it crushed. All of it was under a floor nobody could walk on. The repair brace for the kitchen, which the stock had been committed to before any of this began, could not be made at all now, and neither could the clinic sill the other two lengths had been spoken for.

The clear width of the school door was marked on the floor in chalk, and it was two feet eight inches at the head, and it would stay that way until somebody with timber rebuilt the lintel, and there was no timber. Jo's shutter route was now a Thursday and a half-day and then some, and the smaller of Iven's two files was under the deck and was not coming out. The school day was cancelled for the fourth time in a month, and Ilsa wrote that down as a cost to the school and not to the project. The clinic's wash water had to be carried past the dip in single file for a week.

None of that was the worst of it. The worst of it was that the anteroom's own pump, which had been the crew's first piece of public work, had run through the middle of the failure to keep a corridor from flooding, so that the one record anyone would ever find of the repair in Marrowgate would have a failed school floor attached to it.

Marek stood at the foot of the stair and looked at the wrapped bundle of cloth on the anteroom bench, where it had stayed for six weeks, and felt the old temptation — the stupid, seductive one, that the stone had been showing them something, that the room had made its own argument, that a symbol on a plate in a kitchen a long way below had a view on this.

It was a plate with a hole in the middle and lines going out from it. It had not told him where to put a splice.

He let it go, and that was the second time in two hours he had let something go, and it cost him more than the timber.
