# Chapter 127 — The Man Who Sets A Bone

**Shut for the corridor on the second day of the twenty-fifth week, and a man came down the last of the middle passage with a bag and a strap and came up the nine steps into the anteroom, and he said what he was before anybody asked him, and he said the other thing before he said anything else, and the other thing was the thing that is against him.**

"**Mero Senn. Forty-five. Rescue physician and surgeon, and I have done twenty-two years of it, and I have been at the second gate and I have come down the last of this passage, and there is a bed in this building or there is not.**

"**I am the last person who should be standing in that corridor and I am going to say so in a room before I look at her. I heard about a child at a counter on the way out and I did not come up this passage, because I was at a counter, and there are four people behind a shut lock and one of them is sixty-one and does not walk. I heard about the child and I did not come, and I am telling you that before I look at her, because a man who comes in saying he was asked for is a man who wants to be forgiven, and I am not here to be forgiven. I am here to set a bone.**"

"**Nobody asked you to come,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**Then I am here for a bone and for nothing else, and I will take a room and I will pay for it in work, and if this building has a form with a line for a person responsible I would like to see it, because there ought to be one and I have been in this trade twenty-two years and there is never one.**"

**There is no room, and the room that was shut has been opened since the second bell yesterday, and he was put in the worst room in the building, on a hearth stone, at the end furthest from the door, with a child of nine lying on her back on a hearth stone at the other end of it and her foot in a strip of crate board bound with a piece of an old apron.**

---

**He looked at the foot for about four minutes and did not say anything encouraging.**

"**Who set it.**"

"**I did,**" said Ilsa Norr, from the step.

"**Say how.**"

"**Her leg was under the stone and the stone had gone over and the foot was not turned in at all, and there is no bone in a foot that a child of nine can break without a doctor and no doctor in this building, and I got the foot straight and bound it to a strip of crate board off a register box and I bound it with a piece of my own old apron because that is what I had.**"

"**You did it correctly and it is a bad set,**" said Mero Senn, "**and both of those are true and I am not going to let anybody in this room have only one of them.**

"**It is a bad set because the board is flat and there is nothing in it and it will hold the foot in the position the foot was in when I arrive, and the foot was in the position a stone put it in. It will hold. It will not be right. And in about four months a woman who is a very good cobbler in this Hearthhold — do you have a very good cobbler?**

"**I have a man in this building who can work beech and a shoemaker's last out of a thing,**" said Iven Tallo, from the door, "**and he is paid and he is twenty-two and he is not a carpenter and he is not in this room, and if you want him I will go and get him and I will not be able to tell him why.**"

"**Then get him, and tell him the foot, and do not tell him the rest.**"

"**I cannot do that,**" said Iven Tallo. "**I can tell him what is asked. I cannot tell him what is not asked. I have been in a trade for twenty years and a man who is told what is not asked has been told a thing and it does not go back in.**"

"**That is correct and it is inconvenient and I accept it,**" said Mero Senn, "**and tell him the foot and tell him there is a man in the room who has put it wrong on purpose because there was nobody else, and let him be angry with me, which he will be, and I would rather be angry at than pitied.**"

---

**And then he said the sentence, out loud, in a room, with his hands in a bowl of water, and four people in this building recognised it and had been doing it for twenty-five weeks without knowing there was a name for it.**

"**A plan without a named injured person is not a plan,**" he said. "**And a plan with an acceptable casualty is not automatically acceptable. Those are two rules and I did not write them and I have not improved them in twenty-two years, and I say them to rooms, and I have said them to eleven hundred rooms, and I have never once said them in a room that did not already know them.**

"**And this room knows them. This room said the first one out loud in a corridor yesterday morning and thought it had invented it.**

"**I am not going to make a thing of that and I am not going to be charming about it, because you have been doing my work for twenty-five weeks without a physician in the building and I would be a poor man to walk in and take credit for it, and I would be a poorer one to walk in and be impressed. I am going to say that you did it first and that it did not save anybody, and then I am going to go back to the foot.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, and her voice was not quite what it had been for twenty-five weeks. "**Entered that a physician has said that four people in this building have been doing his work since the first week of the twentieth week without knowing it, and entered that he has said it that way, on purpose, before anybody could have thanked him, and entered that the clerk wanted to thank him and did not and that the wanting is entered because the clerk does not keep that sort of thing to herself.**

"**And entered that the two rules are on a page now, in a clerk's hand, in this corridor, next to a count of eleven and a name, and that they were said out loud in a room by a schoolteacher on the first day of this week and by the man who wrote them on the second, and that the first of those was on the first day of this week and the second of them is today, and that the first cost a child a stone and the second has cost the man who said it nothing at all.**"

---

**The first public failure of this boundary's twenty-five weeks was then read out in a room, in the man's own words, with the number in it, and a doctor was in the room, and nobody forgave him and nobody blamed him.**

Dalla Sen had it on the back of a day-board, and she had copied it out of a book of his own that nobody else in the building has read, in his hand, word for word, and she said so before she read it.

"**I am going to read this out and I am going to say where it is from first, because this boundary has had a great deal of paper read out in rooms in a month and the clerk has stopped pretending that paper and a room are the same thing. It is out of a book of Marek Venn's own, in his own hand, written on the night of the fourth day of the twentieth week, and no other person in this building has read it and I have read two pages of it in twenty-five weeks and this is one of them.**"

She read it out.

"**I recommended that the room at the kitchen-line end be shut and I was right, and the repair was a good repair, and the survey was right about the ground, and eleven people were behind a door for a night and a morning, and one of them is a child of nine with a foot in a strip of a register box bound with an apron by her own teacher, and I am the man who recommended it and nobody in this room is going to forgive me for it and nobody in this room is going to blame me for it and both of those are going to cost me something and I would like the clerk to enter that I said it.**

"**And I am going to add one line in a room and in my own words, and the clerk is going to read it back and if I have said it wrong she is to strike it.**

"**I have been in this building twenty-five weeks and I have watched a boundary find the cheapest answer four times and take it twice. I took the third. And I want it entered that I knew on the Wednesday what I was recommending and I said the words *eleven* and *fourteen* out loud on purpose, twice, so that everybody in that room would be holding them by the end of it, and I have been not understanding since Wednesday that a number said out loud twice in a room is not a check. It is a way of making a room agree with a man.**"

Renn Vale answered it, and she answered it on the landing, and nobody in the room had asked her to and nobody in the room could stop her.

"**The rule says the person who writes a change to a room people use is the person who will keep it, and that person is not to blame for it, and I am that person for a room that is not mine and I have been for a week and a day, and I have taken the protection the rule gives me and I am going to tell this room what it is worth, because this is the week to say it out loud.

"**It is worth this. It is worth me not being the one a doctor looks at. That is the whole of it. It is worth a room not turning on a schoolteacher, and it is worth the room going on being a room, and it is worth nothing else, and a rule that is worth nothing else and is called the reason nothing has gone wrong in this building for twenty-five weeks is a rule that has been holding up a wall.

"**And you are not going to be forgiven, and I am not going to be blamed, and neither is she, and the cook is not going to be blamed for a fire she has not lit, and Jo Verrick is not going to be blamed for a stone she told us about in a room on the Monday before it moved. There is nobody left in this to blame and that is not an acquittal. It is what happens when a good rule is used properly by people who are frightened.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**in the woman who keeps the terms' words, in full, and entered that she is the person the rule protects and that the room asked her nothing and that she answered anyway, and entered that this is the first time in twenty-five weeks that the person the rule exists for has stood up in a room and said what the rule is worth, and entered that the clerk is putting it in the book under the third column of nothing, because it is not a work, and I am the clerk and I am allowed to say that a thing can go in a book and not be a work.**

"**And I am going to say the rest of it, because a doctor is standing in this room and he said he did not come to be forgiven and he is not.**

"**Nobody here has forgiven anybody since the first week of the twentieth week. That is not a virtue. That is this boundary being afraid, and I am the clerk and I have watched it for twenty-five weeks and I have put facts next to each other on purpose eleven times and I have been asked by a doctor to say one thing about a stone and one thing about a child, and I am going to say them, and then this room is going to go on with its week.**"

---

**A man who had been in the room for an hour said one thing about the room and got it wrong, a woman with a rule told him he was wrong, and nobody was kind to him about it.**

"**You can have the room again in a week,**" he said, at the fourth bell, looking at the bed of the ninth hearth stone with his hand on his own jaw. "**The foot is the only thing in here that decides it. A stone on a floor is not an injury. Give me the week and the foot will be the foot and the room will be a room.**

Jo Verrick said, "**You have been in this building an hour.**

"**I have.**

"**Then you are wrong, and I am going to say why you are wrong, and it is not because I do not like you. That stone has a hole under its low side the width of my hand and I can put my arm in up to the elbow, and the nine of them sit on those stones every school day, and a stone that has gone over a hand's width at the low side will go over again, and it will go over on a day when somebody has a lamp in the wrong place.**

"**And the floor in this room is drying and nobody knows where the water went, and a floor that dries and a bed of lime that has gone soft are the same thing for about a month, and I do not know which way either of them goes next.**"

"**Then you are right and I am wrong,**" said Mero Senn. "**And I am wrong because I am thinking about a bone and you are thinking about a floor, and a man who has been in a room an hour has no business correcting anybody about a floor, and I have been doing it for twenty-two years and I am going to do it again and be wrong again and I would like somebody in this building to write that down so that the next one of me who comes up a road is slightly less confident.**

"**I am not going to be argued out of it by a man with a lamp either. I am going to say what I actually think, once, and then it is this room's.**"

He said what he actually thought, and it was six sentences, and Dalla Sen wrote all six.

"**I do not think this room can be taught in. I do not think any room of nine children and a hearth and a door can be taught in by people who have one pair of hands between them and no timber. I think a foot is a week and a stone is a fortnight and a floor is a month, and I think a man who came up a road on the second day can be wrong about a month and I have been, and I do not think it is mine to decide.

"**What I will decide, and I will say it now so that nobody in this room can tell me afterwards that I did not: if that room is taught in while that bed of that stone is a hole, and one of those nine goes under it, then that is not a floor and it is not a plan and it is not a casualty anybody accepted. It is a decision somebody made with a number in their hand. And I have never in twenty-two years been able to forgive that and I have never once been able to stop a room doing it, and both of those are true and I have put both of them in the book.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**in his words, and read back twice, and entered that the plan has a line in it that is about this and that the line is not a number and has never been a number and is a physician's, and that it is the first thing in twenty-five weeks in this building that has been said by a person from outside it who was not carrying a form or a permit, and that I am not going to be moved by that and I am entering that I was moved by it.**

"**And entered that the boundary is at a hundred and fourteen and a quarter — that is three today and ninety-seven since the keeper signed — and that it adds by the day the work was done, and that the two columns reconcile and that they have reconciled for twelve weeks, and that a man of twenty-two is sixty-two days and sixty-two nights into a count that is his own and is at the kitchen line with a tally and a pair of hands that can work a sill and never has.**"
