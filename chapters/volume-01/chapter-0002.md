# Chapter 2 — The Room Below

Marek woke with his face in grit and a weight across his hips.

For several breaths he could not tell whether the weight was stone or his own body. His left hand throbbed. His right hand was buried beneath him, still attached, though every finger felt like it had been struck with a hammer. He could open and close it, but the fingers felt padded and distant. Above him, far above, a shaft of pale light fell through broken ceiling and disappeared into the dust.

He did not remember falling so far.

“Marek.” Leena’s voice came from close by. “If you can hear me, do not move your right arm.”

He lifted his head. The chamber was larger than the tunnel had been, with a low ceiling and walls of dark gray stone veined with dull green. Water whispered somewhere beyond the light. The air was cool, damp, and breathable, but the coolness seemed to come from a depth the room was trying to conceal.

“Who’s here?” he called.

“I am,” said Leena. “Still attached to the shoulder you wanted me to discuss later.”

“Iven,” someone answered from the other side of a fallen slab. “If this is a guild rescue, it is badly dressed.”

“Tams,” said a third voice. “And I have the lamp.”

“Pell?” Marek tried to rise.

A smaller cough answered. “Here. My ankle is attached to the line, not to me. I think.”

The panic in his chest loosened enough for him to count. Marek, Leena, Iven, Pell, Oren, Tams. Six.

He forced himself onto his left elbow. “Name the last place we were all together.”

“East Spur inspection,” Leena said.

“Who was carrying the second chock line?”

“Tams and I had it,” Tams replied. “Oren had the cart. Iven was checking wedges. Pell was pretending to be a lamp boy.”

“I was not pretending,” Pell said. “I was carrying the lamp boy’s job.”

The ordinary words steadied them more than reassurance would have. They took stock before moving. Tams counted the skins: eight liters had survived, distributed across two partly collapsed containers. The twenty-meter rescue line had come back as a frayed nine-meter length. One hand lamp remained, its glass cracked but its wick somehow still alive under a skin of dust; the other was gone. The folding rule, grease-pencil stub, damaged chalk case, surviving screw jack, one pry bar, two short timber uprights, and a damaged but recoverable hand-tool kit had all been pulled from the wreckage. The rest of the shift's gear lay under the fallen stone.

Marek looked for the survey case and found it half buried beneath a slab. The lining boards had split.

He crawled toward Leena. She sat against the wall with her right arm held close to her body. Her face was grey, and the sleeve of her coat was dark with grit. When he reached for the shoulder, she moved away.

“First tell me what you see,” she said.

Marek studied the room again. The floor was not level. A thin seam ran from beneath the fallen slab toward a wall, where a dark line of moisture climbed above the apparent grade. The water at the edge of the light was moving inward.

“Your shoulder is out,” he said. “I can reduce it, but it will hurt. The arm is still in the socket; the head has come forward and down. I will pull from this angle, not from the wrist.”

“I know what pain does. I want to know whether you’re going to call me dead weight.”

“Never.”

“Good. Then tell me what you need.”

He crouched beside Pell’s ankle while Leena watched. The rescue line had left a dirty rope bruise, but the joint was not swollen and the boy could move his toes. “Your ankle is scraped, not twisted,” Leena said. “Keep it raised until we know the floor is steady.”

Marek looked to the broken timber and the frayed rescue line. “A flat surface. Someone to hold your hips. Iven and Oren can anchor the door frame. Tams, keep the lamp low and watch the water. Pell, call out if the floor moves.”

“You are giving orders to everyone.”

“I am giving them jobs. There is a difference.”

“There is usually a difference,” Leena said, “until the floor tries one.”

They made a splint from a narrow piece of the survey case. Marek folded his coat into a sling and used the line to secure it, leaving Leena’s right arm supported but not pressed against her chest. Iven and Oren braced themselves against the fallen slab while Marek set his left foot against the stone and placed his left hand behind Leena’s elbow.

“Keep your eyes on me,” he told her. “If you feel me pull too far, tell me.”

“I will tell you before you decide that I have no opinion.”

“Tams, the lamp.”

Tams lowered the cracked lamp until its light ran across Leena’s face. Pell crouched nearby, his scraped ankle stretched out before him, and began counting under his breath. It was not a medical number. It was a number to give the room a rhythm.

Marek eased Leena’s arm forward. She locked her teeth. He felt the head of the humerus shift against the socket, then stopped.

“More,” she said.

“Lean your weight toward me.”

“That is not more, that is a different direction.”

“Do it anyway.”

She leaned. Marek drew her arm down and outward while Iven and Oren held her body. The shoulder went back with a hard, ugly click. Leena cried out and folded forward. For a moment her forehead rested against Marek’s shoulder. Then she drew a breath, sat up, and moved two fingers of her right hand.

“Give it time,” Marek said.

“Give it less time,” she replied. “The water is coming.”

Marek tied the sling more securely. “No weight through that arm. You can call marks, hold a line with your left hand, or tell me I am wrong. Nothing else.”

“Those are acceptable duties.”

“Break two liters for the first watch,” Marek said. “Six left after that. The water in this room is not drink water. Nobody touches it without a test.”

“Six liters for six people,” Tams said.

“For six people until we find a better source,” Marek replied. “Small sips. No one gives a person water because they look frightened. Fear makes people waste it.”

“Look at me,” Pell said. “I am extremely frightened.”

“Then you get the same as everyone else.”

Iven had moved to the wall while they worked. He ran his palm along a vertical joint, then pressed his ear to it. “The room is shifting,” he said. “Not fast. A few millimeters at a time.”

“Which part?”

“The whole floor, but not together. Here.” He pointed to a pale seam. “And here.”

Marek placed his left hand on the stone. The contact was ordinary at first: cold, gritty, slightly damp. Then a pressure gathered beneath his palm, not a sound and not a vision, only a sense of weight moving somewhere behind the wall. It was strongest for a breath, then vanished. If he had not been braced, the absence of it would have made him lean forward.

Leena noticed.

“Did you feel that?”

“Something changed.”

“Did you know what it was?”

“No.”

The admission settled between them. Marek had spent years trying to make uncertainty presentable. In a tunnel, a clean statement of what he did not know was often more useful than a confident guess.

He rose and looked over the chamber. A narrow opening led toward a darker recess. The air moving through it smelled of stone and old damp, but there was no clear draft. The broken ceiling shaft supplied fresh air only at irregular intervals. A room that looked sheltered could become a pocket of stale air, especially if they opened a door and gave the pressure somewhere else to go.

“Lean-to,” he said. “Against the stable side. Six people, one litter, two hours. One exit. If the floor moves, we leave.”

“Where is the stable side?” Pell asked.

Marek pointed to the wall with the sound in it. “That one is less stable.”

Iven gave a short, unwilling laugh. “You have a gift.”

“I have a job. Find the wall that does not answer back.”

They worked in pairs. Iven and Oren carried the fallen slab far enough to expose the base of the wall. Tams used the pry bar to lift a flat piece of stone. Marek laid the two short timber uprights under a broad section of masonry, not as a permanent brace but as stops for the lean-to. The nine-meter line was frayed, but its middle section remained strong enough for a low frame.

It took them nearly an hour to build something that resembled a shelter. It had a sloped roof, a shallow bed of dry grit, and a side opening wide enough for Leena’s improvised litter. It was not a certified refuge. It was a place to rest while the water found its level and the room showed them what it intended to become.

Leena made him state the limits aloud.

“Six people,” she said. “Two hours. One exit. What happens when the two hours are over?”

“We reassess the water, air, floor, and exit.”

“And if the room looks stable?”

“Then we still reassess.”

“Good.” She leaned against the frame, careful of her shoulder. “Say the last part again.”

His attention returned to the dark recess. “A room that looks stable can still be moving. We do not call this safe until we know what it is doing.”

For the first time since the collapse, the crew began to move as though the work might matter. Oren took the first watch without being assigned it. Tams counted the water skins and wrote the level on a piece of case board. Iven checked the timber uprights and pretended not to watch Marek do the same. Pell kept returning to the sealed door at the far side of the room, where a metal plate had been hidden beneath a shelf.

When Marek finally followed his gaze, Iven lifted two fingers.

On the plate was a narrow blue mark, half worn away. A wheel crossed by a line. Beneath it, three old words had been cut into the stone.

Iven read them aloud. “Cartwright inspection route.”

Pell’s face changed. “That means somebody has to open it.”

“It means somebody once did,” Iven said. “And the mark is still deciding who gets to.”

He looked at Marek, and for the first time the old guild caution was joined by something sharper.

“We’re in licensed territory without a valid route,” he said.
