# Chapter 219 — What A Wall Does

The second day of the forty-second week is a Tuesday, the corridor is shut, and the school did not run and lost nothing, and an air engineer went down a wet stair at the second bell with a lamp and a piece of chalk and was gone for three hours, and nobody had asked her to and the clerk entered that nobody had asked her, because she had said on Friday that she was not going to be the one who decided it and had then decided it on a Tuesday morning without telling anybody, and both of those are in the book.

Jo Verrick had the frame at the first bell, a finger and two-thirds, and the party wall was a quarter of an inch, the thirty-ninth reading, and the same as thirty-eight, and the string was where the string was. She put her thumb under it and the thumb came out of it right, and she did not give the four refusals, and she gave the reason instead, in one sentence, in the same words as Sunday, and the clerk entered it and entered that it was the same sentence.

Renn Vale put her ear to the wall at the first bell, four times over, and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, and the run of mornings out of mornings is twenty-four.

The terms were said at the first bell, all six, and the third of them was said without anybody being asked whether it was right. Ten including one litter for no more than two hours, and it did not change, and on the same morning an air engineer read a nine-hundred-year-old fall off a wall and the sixth of the six terms turned out to have a second line in it that this room had been obeying for nineteen weeks without knowing that it had. The outer service door is the one a body went out of. The fifth of the six sits in a room with both of the people it names in it, and he named it and read nothing. And the sixth was the pump, and it ran, and the pump is the low point of the fall, and the clerk entered that she entered it and wrote not a word beside it.

Then the pump was started, two people turning it, and it ran, and nobody in that room connected the fact that it was running with the fact that a woman was three hours away in a room that this building's water has to get to.

She came back at about the fifth bell with her boots full and the chalk in her hand and nothing written on anything, and she came to the end of the trestle and put the chalk down and did not pick it up again for the rest of the morning.

"**I am going to say one thing and it is going to be about a rule, and I am not going to have it in a book for an hour,**" said Nera Oduya, "**because I have had it in a book in my head since the third hour and I have been carrying it up those stairs and I can feel it turning into a thing I found, and I did not find it, I read it off a wall.**"

"**Say it,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**A fall,**" said Nera Oduya. "**Cut. Nine hundred years old. It comes out of a cistern in the raw rock at the top end of the gallery, which is a basin cut by a person about the size of a cart and which is above the chamber and not in it, and it runs down into the gallery floor and out through the dressed wall at the base and along the rock, and it arrives in this building.**"

"**Say where in this building,**" said Dalla Sen, and her voice was not different at all.

"**In the pump station,**" said Nera Oduya. "**In the floor of this anteroom, under this pump, and it finishes in the head of this pump's own frame, in a groove about the width of my thumbnail, a hand's breadth under a line of chalk, that a woman in this room found nineteen days ago and could not read.**"

The pump was between strokes, and it made no sound at all.

"**Then say the half against,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**The half against is that I have been down there three hours and I have read a fall and I have not read a rule, and the difference between those two things is the whole of what I have to say and it is about nine hundred years wide,**" said Nera Oduya. "**A fall is a channel that goes down. Every cave in this district has channels that go down. That is what water does. If I have only found a channel then I have found the ordinary behaviour of water and I have wasted three hours and a pair of boots.**"

"**Then what did you find,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**A rate,**" said Nera Oduya. "**That is all. I found a rate, and a rate is a rule, and this is the whole of my morning and I am going to say it slowly.**"

She put her hand flat on the trestle.

"**From the cistern to the chamber is about the length of this room and the length of this corridor twice. From the chamber out through the dressed wall it is shorter. And then along the raw rock to here. And I had a piece of chalk and a lamp and I could see the bed of it for most of that, and I am a person who has spent nine weeks looking at floors and I am telling you the bed of that channel does not change its rate once. Not once. Not where the rock is soft and not where the rock is hard and not where it turns and not where it comes in under this floor. It goes down at the same rate the whole way.**"

"**Say why that is not a cave,**" said Jo Verrick.

Nera Oduya turned round.

"**Because a cave is water finding a low place,**" Jo Verrick said, before Nera Oduya could, and then stopped, because she had not been going to speak and had spoken. "**That is what I said on Tuesday last week about the floor of that chamber, and I said a cave's floor has a low place and I put my hand down in four places. I have been in eleven flooded rooms in nineteen years and I have never once found one where the way out was the same height all the way. Not one. You have to go down and around. You have to go down, and then along, and then down, and the down is never the same twice, because the down is whatever the rock let happen.**"

"**Go on,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**And a channel that has been cut does not care what the rock would have let happen,**" said Jo Verrick. "**Somebody chose a rate. Nine hundred years ago somebody with a string and a piece of chalk worked out how much it has to fall in a length and cut the bed to it. And that is not water. That is a person telling water what to do, in writing, in stone, and the writing outlived the person and it is still there, and it has not changed once, and it is under this floor.**"

"**Then say the sentence,**" said Dalla Sen, with her hand near the book.

"**There is a drainage rule in this building,**" said Nera Oduya, "**and it is about nine hundred years old, and it is a line from a cistern, through a gallery, to a pump station, and the pump station is this building's. This building's pump has been running nineteen weeks against it.**"

Nobody in that anteroom said anything for what the clock in the corridor would have called about nine seconds.

"**Say it again,**" said Bel Ordry, and his voice was not steady and he did not pretend that it was.

Nera Oduya did not say it again. She turned round to the pump and put her thumb under the string Jo Verrick had taken out of her belt and laid on the frame about an hour before, and took it out again, and the thumb came out of it right.

"**Say the half against that,**" said Bel Ordry, "**because a man in this room argued in this room ten days ago for running a pump against a nine-hundred-year-old groove in the head of that frame, and said he would argue it again in nine hundred years, and said he was not a person anybody should take advice from about a pump, and every word of that was correct and none of it was what he meant.**"

"**Your half against first,**" said Nera Oduya.

"**My half against is that I was arguing for a groove when I should have been asking who cut it,**" said Bel Ordry. "**I have spent forty weeks telling rooms that a mark is not a rule and a rule is not a mark, and on the sixth day of the fortieth week I stood in this room and argued that a groove nine hundred years old was a rule, and the reason I gave was that a thing that is already there is not a reason, it is a thing that was already there. And I was right that it was a rule. I was wrong about what being right costs. The pump has been sitting on the low point of a nine-hundred-year-old fall and pumping the water back up it for nineteen weeks, and the reason nobody noticed is that a groove is a groove and a groove does not look like an instruction. I have been wrong in this building four times and I knew about three of them.**"

"**Now my half against,**" said Nera Oduya. "**It is not clear. I want that said before anybody writes a sentence about it. The channel is nine hundred years old and it is full of scale — that is ordinary. Scale is what a channel makes when it has been doing its work for nine hundred years, and scale is what you expect, and I have said in this room that I would not explain a seam and I am not going to explain this. But I could not see how far down the run is clear. It is clear at the cistern end. It is clear at the chamber. It is not clear where it comes in under this floor and I cannot see that part at all from the top, and I am not going to stand in a room and tell you where it stops being clear, because I do not know, and a person who guesses about a rate gets hurt.**"

"**Entered, in the air engineer's words, and entered that the two halves were given in the order she chose and that she was asked for neither,**" said Dalla Sen.

Then the clerk turned the book round on the trestle, which she does perhaps twice in five volumes.

"**I am going to say the list,**" she said, "**because there is a person in this room who has been carrying a list of things for three weeks and I am the clerk and it is mine to say, and I am saying it out loud in a room and not writing it, and I want everybody in here to hear where it ends.**"

The anteroom was quiet.

"**A rule is not the dowel. It is not the charter. It is not the mouth at the end of the school corner. It is not the four treads nobody has walked. It is not the ninth room. It is not the air engineer's theory, which is hers and has not been asked for. It is not the plate on that shelf. It is not the three facts of the thirty-seventh week. It is not the sound in the wall under the pump, which four people sleep next to and which is this pump and this fall and has been all along.**"

"**And it is not the second line on that form,**" said Nella Bray, from the anvil end.

"**No,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and I am glad, and I am going to say why I am glad, because it is the most dangerous join available to this room this morning and there are two of them. There is a salvage claim on that trestle with three places on it, and a store at the top of the middle passage and a pump house and a passage off the second gate. And there is a fall out of a cistern through a gallery to a pump station. And those are two lines. And I do not know that they are the same line and neither does anybody in this room, and I am putting both of them in this book today and I am putting them on facing pages and nowhere near each other, and if a person in a year reads them both and sees one shape, then that person has done something this room did not do.**"

"**Entered,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and entered that the clerk has done the thing she has refused to do three times in a month in reverse, which is to keep two things apart on purpose and to say so.**"

"**Now the cost of the looking,**" said Marek Venn, from the end of the trestle.

"**Say the cost out loud,**" said Dalla Sen, "**because a room has found out something at the fifth bell and it is a bad moment to be quiet.**"

"**The cost is a sentence,**" said Dalla Sen, and she had the book open and she did not write in it. "**A fortnight ago a woman came up nine steps and stood at the end of this table and said: water is not a thing anybody keeps, it is a thing that goes somewhere, the question is where it goes when it goes. That is in this book. It was said in a room and it was not written down in a book, and it has been said by two other people in this room in their own words since, and I have entered all three, and I have not joined any of them to anything.**"

She looked up.

"**It has been answered,**" she said. "**Not by her and not by anybody in this room. By a wall, this morning, at the fourth bell. The water goes to our pump. That is where it goes. It has been coming here for as long as anybody has been keeping records about it, and this building has been taking it out of a room four days away and putting it back up a fall nine hundred years old, nineteen weeks, every working morning, second bell to fifth, two people, and the reason this building has water is that the low point of a drain that was cut before anybody alive was born is under a pump frame in this anteroom, and the pump is on top of it.**"

Nobody said anything.

"**So this boundary has been keeping water,**" said Dalla Sen, "**for nineteen weeks, by accident, on a rule it did not know was there, and it has been telling a woman at a second gate for two volumes that it will not keep water because keeping water is a post. And I want it entered that I do not know what to do with that, and that I have not got a column and I am not going to make one, and that there is a woman at the bottom of four sets of steps who asked this building for the water and was told no, and that she has still not had it, and that I have not told her any of this and I am not going to this week.**"

"**Then say the half against that,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**The half against is that knowing is not draining,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and that this room has known a great deal about this water since the fifth day of the fortieth week and has not moved one pail anywhere, and that a rule that is nine hundred years old and is a fall and not a cistern does not empty itself, and that the pump on top of it is the only reason this building has had water at all and stopping the pump will not empty a chamber four fathoms deep. Everything we have learned this week has made this problem harder and not easier and I am the clerk and I am going to write that in the space where a finding goes.**"

At the sixth bell the day's figure and the total went into the book and neither was read, and the reason went in beside them: a room which has been told at the fifth bell that it has been keeping water by accident for nineteen weeks is not going to be read a total over by a woman who has spent forty weeks insisting on reading totals.

Three today and three hundred and sixty-six since the keeper signed. The boundary is at four hundred and eighty-three and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.

The count went in underneath that on its own line, in the ordinary hand, and was not read, and the reason was given, and it was that a clerk who has just entered the sentence *knowing is not draining* is not going to stand up and say a number she has said for fifty-three weeks, and that is a reason about the clerk.

Wen Pask is one hundred and sixty-four days and one hundred and sixty-four nights, fifty-three weeks of a clerk saying out loud that she is going to keep writing it down. It is not going to be one hundred and sixty-five.

The board was not walked, and the reason was entered, and the reason was that a clerk has put two lines on facing pages in a book and told a room she is keeping them apart on purpose, and a board is a column, and a clerk does not walk one on the morning she has told a room what she is refusing to do with it.

The crew are at eighty and did not move, and the reason for the day is in the book, and the reason is that a foreman has four and a half hours and the first open day after the shut days is in four days, and that a man who reads walls has said a sentence about a rate this morning that a foreman would like a reason for before he spends an hour of five men's time on it.

The pot is three days, and the seam gave nothing this morning, and it is at four threads, and the fire in the room at the kitchen-line end has now not been lit for twenty-five weeks, and the four households at a second gate have nothing and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody, and the water in them is going somewhere, and this building now knows where, and has not given them one pail of it.

And at the seventh bell the anteroom was nine steps and a trestle and a pump standing over the low point of a cut channel that went down nine hundred years without changing once, and a woman on the second of three steps with a piece of chalk she had not marked anything with, and a woman on the floor with a folding rule back in a belt, and a room in which a thing that had been a groove for nineteen weeks had turned out to be the last visible piece of an instruction, and not one person in that room had any idea what was between the cistern and the pump.
