# Chapter 20 — A Route That Must Be Kept

The review began with the pump already talking.

The delivery check gave a hard knock before Tams touched the lever, then a second knock as the frame took its first load. The wet mineral seam beneath the housing had widened overnight. A dark line showed along the edge of the service well, and the water mark in the lower gallery stood one finger above the level recorded the day before.

Marek stood beside the public work sheet. The names were all there: Jo at the pump, Nera for air, Sefu for the boundary, Dalla for the record, Renn and Mikka on their watches, Iven for the craft sequence, Oren and Tams for the frame, Pell for the water calls, and Leena for corrections. The sheet was not a charter. It was a promise to inspect the next condition.

The anteroom held ten people, including Leena on the litter. That was the working limit from the first test, and Sefu counted the room himself before the pump began. Renn was on the upper landing and Mikka was beyond the clinic gate on their watches, neither part of the ten. Before the count, Oren and Tams had returned Leena from the clinic cot to the litter under her direction; the cot remained beyond the gate. The deeper Hearthhold gate remained closed for new admission. The returning stair was restricted beyond the service bend. The wrapped plate lay on its cloth at the far side of the bench, untouched. Dalla’s local record of the guild papers and Iven’s suspended-bond receipt lay on their separate board, and the locked inspection door gave no route to the surface.

“Two hours,” Nera said. “The pressure conditions may shorten it. The pump is attended. The outer door stays open. The clinic return is watched from the kitchen pass. The service bypass hatch stays latched.”

Sefu looked toward the gate. “New admission only through the gate if the people in the rooms agree. No one calls the test a public route.”

Jo put her hands on the timber lever. “I have the pump.”

“Iven has the explanation,” Marek said.

“Iven has the explanation,” Jo agreed. “I have the work.”

The distinction had become a rhythm. It was not yet a ceremony, but it was no longer a dispute.

Tams and Oren took the frame. Iven showed Jo where to place the wrench when the retaining bolt was next opened. She did not need him to take the tool from her hand. Pell called the water mark from the bench. Dalla recorded the time beside each stroke. Nera watched the dyed cloth above the air valve and the wet seam at the service well. Renn’s lantern covered the outer door. Mikka had gone to the clinic return with a clean strip of cloth.

The pump began its measured work.

For the first ten minutes, nothing changed except the water level. It fell a finger, then another. The delivery check’s chatter came and went with the sediment. Jo stopped at the first hard knock, cleaned the seat, and resumed only after Nera’s air cloth returned to its original color. The room was doing what the sheet said it would do.

Then the returning-stair wheel turned below.

This time the crew knew what the sound cost. Nera called the stop before Marek reached for the bearing strip. Jo released the lever, Oren caught the frame, Tams set the relief chock, and Sefu opened the outer service door. Renn moved the enclosed lantern to the landing. No one reached for the service hatch.

Marek felt the load shift anyway. It ran sideways through the common beam and answered the stair below, then vanished. He had no new reading to add. The air cloth drew inward, the wet seam widened, and the anteroom was cleared to the landing by the rule they had written the day before.

Nera kept the pump chamber isolated. Mikka watched the clinic return from the named position beyond the gate, and Dalla listened for her bell. When the stair wheel turned a second time, the cloth snapped once, then settled. Mikka called the clinic clear. Renn called the outer door open and the air moving outward.

The practiced response held. The pump did not.

When Nera allowed the room to reopen for a short interval, Jo opened the inspection cover. Sediment had packed beneath the bronze seat, and the seat had shifted far enough to leave a bright scrape along its lip. The gland remained dry, but the check was no longer merely chattering under grit. It had taken a set.

“This needs the retaining bolt out,” Iven said. “I can clean it for short strokes. The lip will not take many more cleanings before it leaks.”

Jo looked at the scrape. “And if the next turn catches it open?”

“Then the water comes through faster than the pump can draw it.”

Marek looked toward the locked inspection door. The guild’s pressure dial, route lamp, upper-catch chain, and tool case were frozen behind it. Dael’s order had not opened a rescue route or supplied a part. If the pump failed now, no one could call on the surface line for a replacement without breaking the order and the law around it.

“How long does the water give us?” he asked.

Pell looked down the marked shaft. “Two notches before the sill. Less if the pressure turn holds the seam open.”

Dalla wrote the number without rounding it.

The surface route was not an option they could use simply because they were afraid.

Sefu watched the deeper gate. “We can close the boundary and keep the anteroom empty. That protects the rooms beyond. It also means the crew has no attended route.”

“Or we can ask the Hearthholds to admit us beyond the gate,” Marek said. “The clinic and kitchen have room. We could bring the litter inside, put the pump work under Nera’s air plan, and make the anteroom a staging space.”

Mikka’s voice came from the clinic return. “That is not a maintenance decision. It is an admission request.”

“The rooms are occupied,” Sefu said. “You cannot move people into them because the boundary is inconvenient. That is exactly the claim the old charters make.”

Marek’s first answer had been a plan. His second had been a pressure that made the plan look necessary. He could feel the old temptation to turn a temporary condition into a necessity and call it consent because people were in danger.

“No deeper admission,” he said. “Record the request as refused.”

Sefu nodded. The refusal was not a punishment. It was the condition that made the remaining choice real.

They could also retreat toward the Cinder Steps. The service bypass was physically continuous, but Leena’s litter would have to turn through the narrow upper bends, Pell would need to move on his crutch over uneven stone, and the lower chamber was flooding behind its temporary gate. The retreat would put the crew back into the unstable route they had left. It would not be a safe withdrawal. It would be a different set of people carrying the same injuries through worse ground.

Leena looked at the service bend. “If we go back, I can be carried. Pell can be helped. I am not saying we should. I am saying the cost is not theoretical.”

Pell’s jaw tightened. “I can move on the crutch.”

“You can move short distances,” Leena said. “You cannot run, and I cannot take the lower landing on a litter if the stair repeats the return.”

Marek looked at the restricted stair beyond the bend. The black cord and Hearthhold mark were out of sight, but the mechanism was still there. The dead pack had not returned the slab. A walking human load had. The missing lower receiver meant a stretcher route could not be treated as an alternate exit.

“Retreat is possible,” he said. “It is not safe enough to call a solution.”

Nera looked at the air cloth. “The pump can be cleaned, but the next pressure turn will make the same seam wet. We can run short intervals with a person at the lever and a person on the landing. We cannot promise continuous running.”

Jo wiped her hands. “Then the route stays a maintenance route. Not a lodging place. Not a public passage. We keep it only while the people named here keep the conditions.”

Marek could hear the cost in her voice. Every short interval would take another part of Jo’s tool-route work. Renn and Mikka would spend more time watching air that had already reversed once. Tams and Oren would carry the frame again. Dalla’s ledger would grow. Iven’s guild name would remain suspended. The Hearthholds would be paying in labor for a route that had not become theirs.

It was better than a claim. It was not free.

Dalla brought the public work sheet to the landing. “Write the decision where everyone can see it.”

Marek took the chalk with his left hand. He began with the route: *Anteroom and service bypass remain conditionally open for attended maintenance and limited boundary work only. No deeper admission. No public route. No guild custody.*

Nera added the air conditions and the three pressure-turn watches. Sefu added the right to close the boundary and the rule that no one could be admitted beyond the gate by emergency pressure alone. Jo added the pump’s short intervals and the cost of each cleaning. Iven added that the gland was dry, the delivery seat was scored, no spare bronze seat had been released, and no permanent repair had been made. Dalla added the locked guild line, the absent rescue, and the cost of retreat.

Leena read over Marek’s shoulder. “Add the clinic access.”

“Care, not residence,” Mikka called from the return.

“Add that,” Leena said.

Marek added it.

Pell pointed to the water line. “And add that the lower mark is not a safe retreat.”

“It is already there,” Marek said.

“Then leave it where everyone can see it.”

The returning wheel moved once more below the floor. Everyone on the landing became still. The air cloth held. The wet seam did not cross the sill. The stair remained closed, and the missing receiver remained a recorded absence.

When the sound passed, Sefu spoke. “If the pump cannot be restarted under the short intervals, the route closes for use. The crew may retreat toward the Cinder Steps if the retreat can be made under a new review. We do not force the gate open because the alternative is frightening.”

Nera added, “And if the air reverses, the route closes regardless of the pump.”

Jo looked at the retaining bolt in her hand. “If I can clean this, the pump gets one more attended interval. If I cannot, we stop and write that it stopped.”

Marek looked at the six names on the sheet. They were not beneficiaries. They were not claimants. They were people who had agreed to do work under limits they could not privately revise.

“Dalla,” he said, “put the next review beside the agreement.”

She wrote: *Second work-bell after the next complete pressure turn, or immediately if the air, water, pump, stair, or boundary condition changes.*

Nera, Sefu, Jo, Dalla, Iven, Oren, Tams, Pell, Leena, and Marek marked the sheet in their own places. Renn and Mikka added their watch marks at the gate after Dalla carried the board to them. The marks were not a public charter. They were an accepted maintenance record among the people who would have to live with its next correction.

Jo cleaned the retaining bolt and set the delivery check back into its seat. The bright scrape on the bronze lip remained. She did not promise that the pump would hold. She placed the wrench where the next person could see it, named the cost of the work, and waited for Nera’s cloth to settle.

“Short interval,” Nera said. “Outer door open. Landing clear.”

Tams took the lever. Oren held the frame. Pell called the water. Renn watched the door. Mikka watched the clinic return. Dalla’s pen moved. Leena’s hand rested on the corrections. Sefu kept one palm on the civic wheel. Iven explained each part of the check without claiming it.

The pump drew water for three strokes. The delivery check knocked once, then twice, and did not chatter. On the fourth stroke, Jo stopped it before the next count.

“The seat is holding,” Iven said. “The lip is not. We can use short intervals until a replacement is approved. We cannot call this a continuous pump.”

Dalla wrote the new limit beside the old one: *delivery seat scored; short attended intervals only; no overnight run; replacement or a safe local substitute required at review.*

Nera looked at the water mark. “Then the route continues only for the next named work period.”

“Agreed,” Sefu said. “No deeper admission, and no one calls the pump a permanent solution.”

It was not a victory. It was a limited continuation with a cost everyone could name.

Marek set his left hand on the public work sheet. The wrapped plate stayed under its cloth. The guild door stayed closed. The stair stayed restricted. The air gate stayed conditional. The route was not theirs.

It was a route that six outsiders and the relevant Hearthhold maintainers had agreed to keep—for a named review, under named stops, and only while the work remained answerable to the people who lived with its consequences.
