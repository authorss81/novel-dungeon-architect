# Chapter 8 — The Stair That Refuses

The copied route ended at a stairwell cut into the chamber’s upper terrace.

Marek saw the first landing from the bottom of the approach and stopped. The stair was not a single flight. An upper flight climbed along the left wall to a broad landing; a lower flight descended on the right and rejoined the same landing through a second opening. Between them, a rectangular stone throat dropped into darkness. A heavy slab sat over the throat, held in a crude frame of rollers and guide rails.

A thick rope ran from the slab’s far edge through a wheel above the landing and down into a counterweight chamber. The rope was old, but not severed. It moved when the slab moved.

“This is how it returns people,” Iven said.

The group climbed close enough to see the mechanism. The upper walking flight ended at a fixed landing. From there the movable slab ran in short rails across the visible stone throat to a lower receiving landing. A pressure-fed seam in the wall shifted the slab when a person’s weight crossed it; the rope and counterweight then drew the slab back toward the first landing. The lower stretcher route had once continued through a receiver beneath the far landing, balancing the walking route. That receiver was gone.

Marek watched a loose pebble roll onto the slab. The stone dipped a fraction, stopped, and stayed there while the rope held its new line. He could see every guide, tooth, and gap in the mechanism. The return was a physical cycle, and every part of it could be watched.

A line of old paint remained on the wall: a white upper arrow for people who could walk and a lower mark for those carried on a stretcher. Beneath it, someone had scratched a count of beds and a date, then worn most of the date away.

“School and infirmary evacuation stair,” Leena said. “The marks are old enough to be honest.”

Iven crouched beside the guide rail. “The lower receiver collapsed. Look.”

A broken stone socket lay below the lower flight. The stretcher side had once ended on a support that carried its weight back across the landing. The socket was empty. The upper walking side still ended at the landing, but the lower side had no equal return.

Marek looked at the old marks. “The landing was a balance.”

“It was meant to be,” Iven said. “Walking weight above, stretcher weight below. The two routes were supposed to keep the slab level. When the receiver went, the lower load never finished its return. Now the slab repeats the unfinished part.”

“So it takes people back.”

“It takes the walking part back. If the stretcher side is empty, the difference remains.”

Marek touched the guide rail with his left hand. A pressure moved along the stone, faint but distinct. It rose under the upper flight, paused at the landing, and returned through the lower rail. He could not tell whether the room was remembering a procedure or simply repeating a load. The distinction did not make the danger smaller.

“Map it,” he said. “No one crosses until we know what the slab responds to.”

Iven took a piece of chalk and marked the slab’s near edge. Tams tied a short cord to a stone and placed it on the upper flight. Oren watched the rope wheel. Leena called the marks from the litter. Marek used the brass rule, held in his left hand, to measure the gap between the landing and the guide rail.

A dead pack crossed the upper flight and came to rest on the slab. The pressure line did not move it. The rope stayed slack, and the pack remained where gravity had placed it.

“An unmanned pack does not start the return,” Marek said. “Try a walking load.”

Iven frowned. “A person is not a test weight.”

“A person is the condition we need to understand.”

Marek looked at Leena. “You cannot cross the lower flight. Your shoulder cannot take the litter.”

“I can call the marks.”

“Call them.”

They sent Iven across with a coil of rope in one hand. The slab dipped as his weight reached the upper landing. When he stepped toward the throat, the lower side remained light. The seam shifted the slab toward the far receiving landing, then the counterweight drew it back. Iven stepped onto the first landing as the stone arrived beneath him, his face pale in the lamp light.

“Now the litter,” Leena said.

“No,” Marek said. “Not yet.”

Pell stepped onto the upper flight. “I’m lighter.”

“You have no reason to stand on a settling edge.”

“Which means I’m testing the step.”

He was annoyed enough to be brave and frightened enough to be honest. Marek let him go, with Iven behind him and Oren at the rope wheel. Pell reached the landing. The slab dipped, the counterweight dropped, and the lower opening presented itself as a dark gap beside the moving stone.

A step settled.

It was a small movement, no more than the width of a finger, but Pell’s left foot was on its edge. He went down hard, catching the guide rail with both hands. The slab shifted under his weight. The old return began before he could pull himself clear.

Marek caught the rope, not Pell. Iven caught the strap of the boy’s courier satchel. Oren hauled on the line. The stretcher side of the landing dipped toward the broken receiver, and the slab slid with them.

Pell’s ankle twisted beneath him.

He screamed once. The sound bounced through the stone throat and returned as a second cry from somewhere above, though there was no one there.

Marek ordered, “Withdraw.”

Iven and Oren pulled Pell back along the upper flight. The slab followed the change in weight, but the broken lower receiver caught the edge with a grinding blow. The rope went taut. The counterweight dropped, then stopped. A crack opened in the upper landing.

They got Pell back onto the first landing, below the moving slab. His left ankle was swelling already, the outer side darkening beneath the skin.

“It twisted,” he said through his teeth. “I felt it turn.”

“Why were you on the edge?” Leena asked.

“I was trying not to be afraid.”

“Fear is allowed. The edge is not.”

Marek crouched beside him. The ankle could bear weight only with support. He split a narrow strip from the shortened timber, bound it above and below the joint, and left the remaining length with the gear. Pell’s face went grey, but his toes remained warm.

Marek’s left hand shook as he tied the last knot. The compensation had begun to make itself felt in the wrist and forearm. His right hand was still useless for a fine pull. He had no hand that could draw a perfect line and no time to wait for one to recover.

The old stair groaned.

The slab returned to its first position. The rope remained attached. The broken lower receiver remained empty.

“The lower side was the stretcher route,” Iven said. “When the receiver failed, the landing kept asking for a weight that could no longer arrive.”

“Can we add a counter-support?” Oren asked.

“For one crossing,” Iven said. “Not for a route. The landing is already taking the difference into the surrounding stone.”

Marek looked at the map. “Then we do not cross.”

Pell stared at him. “You said it was the route.”

“It is the route the old room used. It is not a route we can use yet.”

“That is worse,” Pell said. “A locked door would at least be honest.”

“A locked door can be opened by the right person,” Leena said. “That does not make it safe for us.”

Marek accepted the correction. “A usable clue is not a usable exit.”

He marked the stair with several names: upper flight, lower flight, movable landing, throat, counterweight, missing receiver. He drew arrows for the return and a cross through the point where the group had nearly lost Pell. The map looked ugly, but it recorded what had happened.

Tams tested the counterweight with a dead load. The slab responded to the added weight, but the movement pulled dust from the upper wall. Iven tightened the temporary chock, and Oren set a second line. The support held long enough for one cautious person to approach the landing, not long enough for a litter. When the lower receiver took the line, the rope chattered and the stair returned to its first landing.

Leena watched from the litter. “What happens if we put the litter on the lower route and a person on the upper?”

“The old balance might be restored.”

“For how long?”

“Until one of the supports changes.”

“That is not a plan.”

“It is the limit of this stair.”

They withdrew before the next pressure pulse. The group had spent the better part of a morning and learned that the stair’s rule was not a monster’s appetite. It was an unfinished evacuation procedure repeating itself through a physical machine. The old people had needed the landing to separate walking patients from a stretcher line. The lower receiver had fallen, so the difference remained trapped in the slab.

The copied map showed another line around the stair’s outer wall, barely wide enough for a litter carried lengthwise. Only one team could pass at a time. It ran behind the maintenance shaft and down toward the failed pump. At its far end, a small air grille moved air in a steady, human rhythm.

“The bypass,” Marek said.

Iven looked at the narrow opening. “It ends at the pump gallery.”

“And the air gate.”

Pell, pale and propped against Oren, looked toward it. “Then we take the pump route.”

Marek checked the litter, the water count, and the stair one last time. The stair had given them a map and a warning. The bypass waited beyond the wall.

He pointed to the narrow line on the map. “We move the litter before the next pulse. No one crosses the landing.”

The group gathered what they could carry. Iven secured the wrapped plate in his pack, where it would not strike the stone. Behind them, the slab settled with a long stone sigh, carrying the old stair back to its first landing.
