# Chapter 163 — The Shape Four Hands Hold

**Open for the corridor on the third day of the thirty-second week, a Wednesday, and the school ran for the twenty-seventh morning in the room at the kitchen-line end, and a girl of nine sat on the fourth step of the anteroom with her foot up on a crate and watched four people try to be three.**

**Nobody was forced to hold anything. A man said no in a room, and gave the reason in his own words, and the reason was about a page that has been blank for thirty-two weeks, and the price of the no is a day of delay and a name in a clerk's book.**

They came in at the fourth bell and the fourth of them came in sitting down. The crate board under the ninth hearth stone was holding, and both flags were where they had been, and a heel had been rubbing the clay beside that board for four weeks and had left a mark anybody could read from the doorway. Jo Verrick said the standing term out loud in the doorway, the way she had said it every morning since a physician gave it to her. Mero Senn said it out of his own hand against hers. The schoolteacher said yes to it.

Eleven weeks now without a fire in the corner of that room, and Mikka Hale asked to be asked about it for the seventh time and refused a seventh time in one sentence, standing in the doorway with the fire-frame off behind her and the cold coming off the wall in a sheet that nine children could feel and did not mention.

Then the work started, and the work started by discovering that the six cut pieces of a school's own beech were not going to be long enough.

Iven Tallo put them end to end on the trestle, which is what they were cut for, and then took a piece of string from his own pocket and ran it from the place on the anteroom floor where the foot of the thing would sit up to the underside of the lintel over the school corner's mouth, and came back and measured the string against the row of wood with his own thumb.

"**Say that number out loud,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and say whose thumb it is.**"

"**It is a thumb and it is not a rule, and I will not pretend otherwise.**" He laid the string along the beech and let it hang. "**Six pieces laid end to end, and the span is about a hand and a half longer than six pieces, and that hand and a half is the head.**"

"**Then the head is a hand and a half,**" said Marek Venn.

"**No, because the head has to fit the underside of that lintel and the underside of that lintel is not flat, it is a bed that was cut nine hundred years ago by somebody who was not in a hurry, and if you cut a head to fit a bed you have got no head left for the splay.**"

He turned round to face the room, and there was a piece of chalk in his left hand, and he put it on the table instead of using it, which was the thing he had been doing for a month.

"**Say the answer then, because you have described the problem twice,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**The answer is that the head is not cut square. It is cut on a splay, so that a foot of beech meets the bed of that stone on a wide face instead of a narrow one. A splayed head is longer than a square head for the same bed, and it is the correct joint for a thing that is being pushed down and along at the same time, and it is the only answer in this building that costs nothing and needs no second piece and no pin and no nothing.**"

"**Say what it costs, because everything costs.**"

"**It costs a cut that has to be true along its length, and it is going to be cut by a man whose left hand does not close, and I have watched that hand cut eleven pieces of beech in a fortnight and I am not going to say it cannot do this. I am going to say that a splay cut coarsely is a wider face than a splay cut finely, and a wider face on a thing taking four tons is better than a narrow one, and that is not a compliment to the hand. It is arithmetic.**"

Nobody thanked him for that, and the room got on with it, and that is what the arithmetic was for.

Then the string came out and the piece was put on the trestle and the question of the hands arrived, and it arrived in the worst possible way, which was that everybody in the room already knew the answer and had been carrying it since the second bell.

"**Four people hold the shape and one person cuts,**" said Marek Venn. "**That is not an opinion. That is what happens when a length of beech is being cut to a splay, because a cut to a splay has to be made with the piece held at four corners, and if a corner moves the cut is wrong and the head has to come off and start again.**"

"**Say how many corners are held now.**"

"**Three, if the clerk is counted, and the clerk is not going to be a counter-support in a building where she keeps the book.**" He put his own hand flat on the beech, and then took it off it again. "**Iven, me, and whoever else a room can find, and Jo is reading a wall at a first bell tomorrow and you named her this morning as a person you are not going to ask, and you have not argued with yourself about it, and I have not either.**"

"**Then it is three,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and the terms say four, and a room does not discover this at the fourth bell with a saw in somebody's hand.**"

Marek Venn had spent a month in this building being the man a room asked when a number was wanted, and he had got comfortable in it in a way he had not admitted to anybody, and standing on a shut-week Wednesday with a shape that could not be held by the number of hands in it he found that the comfort had a shape of its own. Four hands, four corners, one cutter. It was not a number anybody had put in a plan. It was a number that had come out of watching people, and he had never once written it down, and a man with a slate in a hand and a beam under a table had just written it down out loud in front of nine people before he could, and he found that he was frightened of how much he had wanted her not to.

That is when Tams Quill said it, and he said it from the wall, and he had been standing at the wall for about a quarter of an hour doing nothing that anybody could later describe as work.

"**I will hold it. I want that on the table before I say the rest.**"

"**Say the rest,**" said Marek Venn.

"**No.**"

For about two seconds the anteroom did what it does perhaps twice in a month, which was nothing at all, and let the man finish.

"**The page at the low end of that corridor says somebody stands there and watches the water, and the page has been blank since the fifteenth week, and it is blank now for thirty-two weeks, and the reason it is blank is that a person who is willing is not a person who is standing there.**"

He put his right hand up, open and flat, the way he did when he was reading the board, and it did not go back down for a while.

"**I am willing. My hand does not close and I have been told that by a physician and by a woman with a rule and by my own hand every morning since the second week, and I am not going to be a second blank line in this clerk's book. If I hold this brace then the four that hold it are three and a man who reads numbers, and when this thing is standing in ten years the record of who held it will be a record of a man who was willing.**"

"**Say the part you have not said,**" said Dalla Sen, quietly.

"**The part I have not said is that I would be good at it. That is what I want entered. I would hold that corner well. I have watched three people hold corners for a fortnight and I know what a held corner looks like and I would be one of the best four in this building at it, and I am not doing it, and I would like the reason entered before anybody in this room tries to be kind to me about it.**"

Renn Vale came down three steps, which she does not do.

"**The reason is not about your hand and it is not about your willingness and it is not about the ten years. The reason is that a man who is good at a thing and refuses the thing is the only kind of refusal in this building that nobody can argue with, and this room is very tired of the other kind.**"

"**Enter all of it,**" said Dalla Sen, "**including that he said he would be good at it, and enter that he asked for that to be entered before anybody was kind to him about it, and enter that the price of a brace without a complete crew is a day of delay and a name in my book and that the name is his and he gave it to me on purpose.**"

Nobody argued with him for about two minutes, and the two minutes were the part of the morning that Marek Venn would remember, because in them the room did everything it had been told not to do, one thing at a time, and did it out loud.

"**Say the first thing the room is going to try,**" he said, to nobody.

"**I am not going to try anything,**" said Dalla Sen. "**I have asked four people in this anteroom to be told no and I am not going to be the fifth clerk in this building who asks a man to be talked into a counter-support. Nobody is going to be made to hold this. Not by me, not by a term, not by a pot at nine.**"

"**Say the second.**"

"**There is no second thing from me. From the room there is going to be a second thing and it is going to be a name, and I am going to hear it and enter it, and I am not going to do anything with it.**"

Talla Roke said it from the step, with her three behind her and her hands loose, and she said what she was before she said anything else, which is the shape of her in this anteroom now.

"**I am not here about a boy. And I have a question and it is a courier's question and it is not a good one.**"

"**Say it, Talla.**"

"**What is a person in this building supposed to do when it lets go?**" She turned her palm up. "**I have walked a road of yours as an ordinary resident with a bundle that is not mine and thirty-one people behind me and nobody at the front, and that worked because every person on that road could see what was holding it up. A stone. A wall. A door. And now you are putting a piece of beech across a door in the busiest room in this building and there is going to be a day when somebody in this building has to decide whether it is still doing its job, and I want to know in advance what that person is supposed to look at.**"

"**That is a fair question and I do not have an answer to it this morning,**" said Marek Venn.

"**Then I will tell you what the answer is going to have to be,**" she said, "**and I am not being kind, I am being a courier. It is going to have to be a person who is not the man who built it. If the only person in this building who understands that piece of beech is the man who cut it, then in ten years that piece of beech is a piece of wood and a story, and a story is not a thing somebody can stand next to.**"

"**Enter that,**" said Dalla Sen, "**in her own words, and enter that it is the fourth time she has given this building a rule that was written about a road, and that I am not going to improve on it and I am not going to say which road it came from.**"

They cut at the fifth bell, with three hands, and the man who cut it was not the carpenter. It took eleven minutes for the first face and it came off coarse, and the coarse place showed as a pale band across the end of the beech where the saw had gone across the grain at a low angle, and nobody in the room said anything about it, because the carpenter had said the arithmetic in advance and had not called it a compliment.

The girl on the fourth step had been watching the whole of it with her foot up on the crate, and at the end of the fifth bell she asked the only question anybody asked that afternoon, and it was not about a brace.

"**What is the pale bit.**"

"**That is where the saw was not straight,**" said Iven Tallo. "**Grain does not care what a man intends. When you cut across it you leave a place the wood did not agree to, and it shows, and it stays showing, and that is the only honest mark in this trade.**"

"**Does it break there.**"

"**No. It breaks where it is thinnest, and that is not the same place, and a person who thinks they are the same has not cut anything.**"

"**Say the last of it in a louder voice, because a woman at the kitchen-line has asked me twice to come and sit down,**" said the girl, and did not move.

"**Come and sit down,**" said Iven Tallo, "**and if anybody in this building tells you that a thing is safe because a man says so, you come and ask me, and if I am not here you ask the woman who keeps the room, and if she is not here you ask nobody and you go out of the room in the ordinary way.**"

Dalla Sen entered that a child had asked a carpenter two questions about wood and had been given a rule about safety by a man who keeps nothing. She entered the rule and not the child. She said she was not going to put the child's name on it and not going to call it a good question either, because a good question is a thing a person gets praised for, and nobody in that room had praised her and she had not asked to be.

Then at the second bell of the sixth, before the numbers, the carpenter put the beech down flat on the trestle and squared the splay against the underside of the lintel by eye and by the four inches of a drawing he had made with a burned stick on a board offcut. It stood up on its own, and he stood looking at it long enough that a room full of people did not interrupt a man looking at a piece of wood.

"**Say what you are looking at,**" said Marek Venn, "**because you have said that a joint with no pin in it has to be a shape that stands up on its own, and I would like a room to know whether it does.**"

"**It stands up,**" said Iven Tallo. "**It stands up because it is short and heavy and the splay is a triangle, and not because I am a good carpenter. A piece of wood that stands up on its own is doing the one thing it was cut to do, and that is all the praise it gets.**"

---

**At the sixth bell the clerk read the day's number and the total out loud, both of them, and the board was not walked, and the count was read at ninety-eight.**

"**Three today and two hundred and two since the keeper signed. The boundary is at three hundred and nineteen and a quarter, and it adds by the day the work was done, and the two columns reconcile, and they have reconciled for fifteen weeks. The crew are at seventy-one and the fifth asking is in the book with the split in it.**"

"**I read those because it is a school morning and a day that nobody reads a number out loud on is a day that this building starts keeping its own figures somewhere else.**"

"**The pot is nine. The seam gave one jar yesterday and one on Friday and two on Wednesday, and Jo says four threads and was eight in the nineteenth week, and has not measured it in between, and there is no number in this building for how fast that is going and the clerk is not going to be the one who makes one.**"

"**The count. Wen Pask is ninety-eight days and ninety-eight nights into a count that is his own, and it is not going to be ninety-nine, and I have said in nine weeks running that I am going to keep writing it, and I am writing it, and a man who keeps a road has been in this room and has not thanked me and I am glad.**"

"**And I did not walk the board, and the reason is that a man said no to me this morning and I am carrying his no about the way you carry a pail you did not ask for, and I would rather not have a column in front of me for about a week.**"

---

**The brace is six pieces long and a hand and a half short of the span, and the answer was not more wood. The head is being cut on a splay, which is wider, and the wider face is carrying four tons, and the carpenter said the arithmetic before anybody was kind to him about it.**

**Four people hold the shape and one person cuts. There are three, and a man who would have been good at it said no, and the reason he gave is a page that has been blank for thirty-two weeks, and the clerk entered that he would have been good at it before anybody tried to be kind to him.**

**A courier asked what a person in this building is supposed to do when a piece of beech lets go, and the answer she gave is that it will have to be a person who is not the man who built it, and no such person has been named, and nobody in this room has sent for one.**
