# Chapter 51 — The Number on the Shelf

The tenth week came in wet and the anteroom smelled of wet stone and hot iron and the pot, and the room was still saying eleven weeks, because the week the crew came up in is counted along with the weeks they have been here, and only Tams Quill keeps the other figure and he has never once said it out loud. The first thing Renn Vale did with the morning, as she had done every morning since the week the shore went in, was go down three steps to the service bend and put the back of her hand on the foot of a shore.

She did not touch the cord. She never touched the cord in the morning. She looked at the place where the black line came over the open face of the wall, four feet up and behind the shore's head, and she put her thumb beside the bright inch of band and not on it, because her mother had told her once, in a kitchen, a long time before any of this, that you do not put a thumb on a thing that is working.

Then she went up the three steps and tied the bell rope's other end to the newel, which was hers, and which nobody had argued with.

Down in the anteroom the wheel had gone at the second bell and gone again at the third, the way it always had, and the pot was on, and eleven people were in a room that holds ten including one litter, and nobody said anything about the eleventh because the eleventh was a child of the fevered household who had been sent to fetch a pot and had stayed, and a building does not announce its own population, and what the eleventh is nobody's business unless the room is counting for a reason.

Jo Verrick had a string, a chalk, and a piece of flat iron bent into an L, and she had them on the bench when Marek came down, and she was not doing anything with them, which was itself unusual enough that he looked twice.

He knew what that was. He had spent ten weeks learning to read a room in which the most competent person put a tool down and then waited, and the waiting was always for him, and it was almost never hostile and it was never anything he could answer. In Marrowgate a man who put a tool down and waited was making a point about you. Here it was a woman of forty-one with a delivery seat and two cleanings on it who had decided that the number under her hand was going to be said in a room with people in it, and who was not going to be the one to say it to four walls and a clerk, because a number said to four walls in a building like this is a number that comes back wrong.

He could have gone down and held the L for her. He would have held it badly. She knew he would hold it badly, which was why the L had a handle that a hand could not get round, and he understood the handle, and he was not going to say he had understood the handle, because saying so would be the same as not understanding it.

"Fourteen mornings," she said, before he had asked.

"You have been measuring it every morning for a fortnight."

"Every morning the route is used and nobody is on the stair, which is not every morning, and it is fourteen of those." She turned the string over. "I did it on purpose. I did not tell the room I was doing it and Dalla is going to be annoyed with me about it, and she is entitled to be, and I am going to do it in front of the room this morning anyway, because a thing done on purpose and not said is the exact thing that killed Oren and I am not doing it twice."

---

The reading was done in the anteroom with the bench cleared and the tally board turned round, and it took nine minutes, and four people argued with it, and the fourth argument is the one that changed it.

Jo had a figure and it was not a pretty one. She read it out the way she read the seat's estimate, with the time on it and in her own hand, and Dalla Sen wrote it and made her add her own name next to the figure rather than in a column, and then read it, and the room took it apart.

"Say the method first," Leena Orr said from the frame, with her right arm in the sling and the slate on her knees. "A number without a method is a rumour with a decimal on it."

"String, chalk, and the L," Jo said. "The L goes on the stone's own top face at the inside edge, where the stone is bedded and not over the hole, and the string is hung off the wall at four feet — the same four feet the lead comes over at, and that is not a coincidence, that is the same wall — and the string is pulled tight and the chalk is put where it crosses the stone's edge. Pull, let go, pull, let go. It is the same as a survey reference except I do not trust a survey reference and I have never trusted one, and I would rather have a string and a person than a mark that a person put there once in September."

"Fourteen readings," Leena said. "Give me the smallest and the largest."

"Smallest was about a finger and three quarters," Jo said. "That was the first one. Largest was about two inches, and that was on the fourth morning, and that is the one in the ledger from the Thursday."

"Those are the same measurement made in the same place by the same person eleven days apart and they differ by a quarter of an inch," said Ilsa Norr, from the frame's other side, in the flat voice. "Which means one of two things. Either the stone is moving and it is not coming back all the way, or your string is at a different tension on different days. Which is it, Jo, and do not be kind about it."

And Jo Verrick, who had been the one to insist on the method, took it properly.

"Both," she said. "I do not know how much of the quarter of an inch is the stone and how much of it is me, and I have put both numbers on the board and the range is on the board, and the range is the finding. A finger and three-quarters to two inches, with the stone going back nearly all the way each time, and a hair that does not go back, and I have watched it eleven days and I am not going to tell this room which of those two is the stone and which is the string."

"Range," Dalla said, and wrote it, and read it back.

"Then say what it is for," Mikka Hale said, from the frame, with the pot's cloth in her hand. "I have listened to that bench for nine minutes and I have understood every word of it and I have not understood one thing about the school. What does a finger and three-quarters mean to a person who has to walk over it?"

"Less than the Thursday," Jo said. "That is all. Not nothing. Less."

"And a man is still under it," said the woman from the wash rooms, from the doorway, where she had come in without the room hearing her, which she did, and which the room had stopped remarking on in the ninth week.

---

Then Renn Vale said the thing that turned a number into a work, and she said it standing up, and she said it to nobody, and the anteroom had the good manners to stop.

"I have been down there every morning for a fortnight with my hand on that foot," she said, "and I have had it in my head the whole time and I have not said it, and I am saying it now because Jo has just told the room that she has been doing a thing on purpose and not saying, and that is how a page becomes a lie."

"Renn—"

"Listen to the size of it. Two inches. That is not a collapse and it is not nothing and it is not going to be a collapse, because that stone is a foot thick and dressed and it was cut by somebody who knew what they were doing. It goes about two inches and it comes back. And I have been standing here for nine weeks telling anybody who will listen that the thing under that lip is a road and not a wall, and a road is a road whether it is a hand's breadth or a hair." She put her hand flat on the bench, the way Jo did. "The number is not the problem. The number is *known*. Jo has made it known and it is on the board. The problem is that the reason it is known is that a man was under it, and the next time it is known will be for the same reason, and the man will be Jo."

"That is my business," Jo said.

"It is your business and it is my floor and it is my mother's thing in a hole and it is the route I keep," Renn said, "and one of those four is a reason and three of them are not, and I have decided this morning that I am not having the first one do all the work again."

---

And then Sefu Aran put both hands on the wheel, which is what he did before anything that was going to have a name on it, and asked the only question in that building that could turn a wish into a piece of work.

"Nobody has named this," he said. "There is a number on the board and there is a stone and there is a man, and there is no work, because a work is three things. It has a person at the end of it, and it has a date, and when it is finished there is something different than there was before. If it is not those three things it is a complaint, and complaints are honest and I have got nothing against complaints, and a complaint does not let a person go into a flooded room."

"It is not the crew's to name," said Marek.

"It is nobody's to name, which is the problem," Sefu said. "You cannot name it because the six of you are not tenants and not neighbours and not residents, and everything you have said out loud in nine weeks is true, and one of the true things is that you do not get to put your name on a work in my boundary. Jo cannot name it because Jo is not a resident either, whatever anybody has started calling her. Nera cannot name it because that is air and this is a floor. So the naming is mine, and I am going to do it out loud and in front of these people and I am going to be careful about the words, and Dalla is going to write down the words and not a summary of the words, and if I say it wrong I want it struck."

"Struck how?" said Dalla Sen.

"By whoever I say it to, out loud, in the room," Sefu said. "That is how. That is the practice in this building and I am not going to be the one who improves it."

He stood with his hand on the wheel for a while, working it out in the open, and nobody helped him, because that is also the practice.

"Right," he said. "Here it is, and I am not saying *a post*, because a post is what you want and I cannot get you a post. What I am saying is this: **a shore under the near landing's lip, so that the route can be used on a day when the bearing hand is Jo Verrick and Jo Verrick is on the lever.** That is the work. The person at the end of it is me, and I am the receiver of anything that comes up out of that room, and it goes in Dalla's book with a time on it and it is not one of the five of you. The date is inside this week if the room can find me a plan and if the air will allow it. And when it is finished, something will be different, and I will be able to say what, and so will you."

The room sat with it.

Sefu looked at the board. "That is the work," he said. "Now somebody tell me why it cannot be done, and I would like to hear it in the next five minutes, because I have said it out loud and after this I own it."

---

Tams Quill had the stores on the tally board before anybody had finished being pleased with themselves, because the tally was the part of the world that could not be argued with, and he stood up with his right hand in its sling and turned the board round with his left, which he did everything with now.

"Material," he said. "Nobody in this room is going to like this and I am going to say it in the order it is going to hurt.

"A post under that lip wants nine foot and a bundle. That is Dalla's figure from the second hour of the worst day and it is in the ledger and it is not a guess, it is Iven's figure with Jo's number on the end of it. Nine foot is the reach. A bundle is three pieces scarfed, because you do not cut a nine-foot thing in a building with no saw and put it up in one piece. So: a post wants nine foot and a bundle, and a bundle wants three pieces scarfed end to end, and three feet a piece is nine feet before you have cut a scarf, and a scarf takes a foot out of every joint it crosses, so honestly it is four pieces and twelve foot, and twelve foot is not in this building either, and the honest count of what is in this boundary is four foot eleven and a bit of offcut in this anteroom and three lengths in the shore on the common landing, and those three are the head of a shore and a shore's head is worth nothing to a shore. Which is the sentence I have said in this room before and I am saying it again because the number has not moved.

"Now the good part, and I want the room to hear the good part before it hears the rest, because I am not a villain and this is my board." He put his finger on the line. "There is five foot nine."

"There is five foot nine under six feet of water on the bottom shelf of a flooded pump gallery," said Dalla Sen, "with its inventory number chalked on it in Nera's hand, and the inventory line is open, and it is on the register of public-works equipment that is guild-frozen pending a hearing, and it has not moved since the sixth week."

"Five foot nine is not nine foot and it is not a bundle," Tams said. "That is the whole of the good part. It is the longest single piece of sound timber in the whole of this boundary that is not somebody's wall, and it is three foot three short of a post, and the missing three foot three is not in this building and is not in any other building that any of us can walk to."

---

The anteroom went quiet in the particular way it went quiet, which was not quiet at all, it was thirty people not saying the same thing.

Nera Oduya had been at the valve the whole of it and had said nothing, and now she said the thing that stopped the morning, and she said it flatly, and she did not put it on the board.

"Before anybody in this room plans a piece of timber out of a room with water in it," she said, "we are going to read the card. The first card. The one I wrote for that room in the seventh week, which is on my board and has been on my board ever since with one line added under it in my own hand."

She reached up and took it down, and it was a slate and it was not long, and the room had seen it before and had not read it since.

"Line one," she said. "The interval. Line two, the isolation. Line three, the cord. Line four, the turn: if the cloth moves, the person is out, the well is closed, the gallery refloods, and **the day is over** — not shortened, over. Line five, the finding I cannot write. Line six, the seat. Line seven, the person, and on the first card line seven is open and was still open when the first descent was aborted. Line eight, the receiver, and it is not one of you six. Line nine, and line nine is the one, and I am reading it out because I wrote it on purpose and I have never been thanked for it."

She turned the slate so the room could see it.

"**This is a method for a room that one person is going into once.** In the dark, in water, for eleven minutes, to take four things off a shelf. It is a method and not a promise, and I have put the word *once* on it on purpose, and the reason is that a second trip is a different document and the difference between the two is not a formality, it is the air."

Nobody in the anteroom interrupted her, which they had learned to do.

"So," Nera said, and set the card back on its hook beside the valve board, and it hung there, and under it on the same board hung the second document and the third, and all three of them had different dates on them and none of them was this week's. "There is a card in this room that says *once*. And there is five foot nine on a shelf. And the difference between those two sentences is the whole of the next four days, and I am not going to be the one who pretends the difference is a technicality, because the first time somebody in this building pretended a thing like that was a formality, a man died under a lip with a figure on a board that everybody had agreed with."

The anteroom held still.

"Sefu," Nera said. "You have named a work. Now go and find out what *once* means this week, because I am not going to hand you a fifth card until the room has decided what the word is for, and if you go down into that room on the first card then the plan Oren Sill was inside is the plan this is inside, and I will not do it twice."

"Then what is it for," said Sefu Aran. "Say it in one line, and I will take it to the room."

Nera looked at the valve board for a moment, at three cards in three different hands' worth of dates, all of them hers.

"It is a word about a work and not a word about a room," she said. "And that is not mine to decide, because if I decide it then the air engineer decides how many times this boundary may want a piece of timber, and I will not be that. Put it to the room. Put it to them in the anteroom, with the card in your hand, and let them say it out loud one at a time, and if they cannot agree then the answer is that the room has four hours of standing air and no plan, and the five foot nine stays on the shelf, and that is a real answer and it is allowed."

She turned back to her valve.

"And Marek," she said, without turning round, "no. Not today, and not this week until the room has a word for *once*. I know what your face is doing and it is the same face it made in the seventh week when you wanted a jack, and the jack was a thing and this is a length of timber under a stone that a man's weight moved. There is no work yet. There is a keeper and a stone and a number on a board. Go and be somewhere else."

There was a sentence in him and it was a good one. It was that a stone which moved a quarter of an inch under a man and came back was a stone carrying a line, and that the line was already there because Iven had made it, and that a person who wanted a post did not need a post first, he needed the number that said the stone was working. It was a true sentence. He had built a career out of sentences like it and he had never in his life said one of them into a room without checking who was going to have to hold the load afterwards.

Here the answer was on the anteroom bench in the middle of a range, in Jo Verrick's hand, with her name next to it and not in a column. Somebody had already done the thing he wanted to do, and had done it better, and had done it in front of a clerk so that it could not be improved on later by a man with a rule. He would have been the one to do it worse.

So he shut his mouth about the sentence, and the shutting took about two seconds, and it was the first honest thing he had done all week.

"Aye," he said. "Somewhere else."

The wheel went round twice under the floor. The bell rope hung at the threshold. Down the shaft under the common landing, the water came up over a cache mark and a chalked number at the rate it had come for seven weeks, and nobody in the anteroom was going to be able to look at it for at least two days.
