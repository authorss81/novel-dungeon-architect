# Chapter 43 — What Came Up Out of the Water

The school corner's fall was cut on the second day of Nera's plan and the floor went over it on the fourth, and Ilsa Norr reported both of those things in her own words at the end of the draw period, standing in the anteroom, and nobody asked her to.

"The fall goes to the channel and the grit that comes up it goes where the fall's grit already went, and I have watched it for six days and it has not come up once," she said. "The two days are mine. The pot was on both days and it was Mikka's pot and I am not going to thank her for it in front of nine people, so I am thanking her now in front of the same nine people. The floor is laid. I have walked it with nine children and none of them has put a hand on a joint, and one of them has not walked on the far end at all, and that is his business and I have not asked him about it."

"Then we do not," Jo said, and wrote it down, which was the correct response and which everybody in the room recognised as such.

Which left the draw.

Tams had the arithmetic on the board before anybody had finished arguing about who held what, because the arithmetic was the argument and it always had been.

"Here it is and nobody can improve it," he said. "In the ninth day the pump drew fifteen centimetres in five minutes with the check chattering at the fifth. The bay's lip is four feet under the well's lip — I have been down the ladder eleven times measuring it with my own arm and my own thumb and Jo has checked me twice and written down that she checked me twice. Four feet is twelve hands. A hand is five minutes of pump. Twelve hands of pump is an hour and ten minutes of the pump actually running."

"And the pump runs for how long?" Mikka said, from the frame, where she was already standing.

"Twenty-six strokes, six minutes off, nineteen strokes. Thirty-four minutes of pump in a forty-minute cycle, and I have never once seen it do better than that, and the six are the part with the water up. So: one cycle leaves us about a foot and a half under the lip, and the second cycle gets the rest, and we need two cycles, and between the two cycles there is a six-minute rest with the water climbing, and nobody is going to be in that room during the six-minute rest because the plan says so in the first line."

"How many intervals of the pump does a cycle take," Jo said, to nobody in particular, and then to herself, and then out loud, because she had worked it out and wanted it on the page.

"Two," Tams said. "Twenty-six is the first and nineteen is the second, and the six is not an interval, it is a hole. So a cycle is two intervals, and a cycle is forty minutes, and two cycles is four intervals, and we are opening one well and taking one person down once, and the arithmetic works, and the arithmetic has worked since the ninth day, and it worked then too and I did not think to ask what it would cost."

"Jo," Marek said.

"I know what it is going to cost," Jo said. "It is going to cost a day and a half of a route that is already shut for the season, and it is going to cost the seat, and I am going to say the number out loud before I start, because I said in the twenty-ninth day that the fourth cleaning is the one where a person starts cutting corners and then blames the seat, and this is the fifth, and I am not going to start cutting corners at five."

"Nobody has offered you any," Iven said.

"Nobody has offered me any, and I am going to want one at about the two-thirds mark, and I am telling you now so that when I want it you do not look at me like a thief." She put her hand flat on the bench. "A day and a half. Recorded before, not after. And the fifth cleaning, and after it the seat is worse than it was this morning, and I want the estimate read out in this room when it is read out and not at the end of the month when somebody wants to be generous."

"Recorded before," Dalla said, and wrote it, and read it back.

---

The period was named for the first bell on a day the school was not running, which meant the corridor was shut, which meant the sixth watch at the waist was not a post at all, because the waist is only a post while the corridor is open, and Jo said so out loud before anybody could find a way to leave it empty and call it uneventful.

Nera at the valve, with the cloth. Sefu at the wheel. Dalla at the threshold with the bell rope and the ledger. Mikka on the pump frame, with the delivery valve, and the wrench, and the old flat file with the cord-bound grip, because the frame was hers and the frame was not moved tonight by anybody's opinion. Oren on the long timber lever, two-handed, in the slot in the grating, because Tams Quill's right hand had been opened in front of nine people that week and had not opened.

Tams was on the count and on the board and nowhere else. Isel Ferrow had the water mark and was not to be first at it for a month and called it out loud every half hour anyway, and Pell Roke was on the bench beside Dalla with his ear against the stone of the step above the stair head, because a man on a bench with a good ear is not a decoration.

The anteroom's ten were its ten, and Leena's litter was in it, and the rating held: the outer service door open a shoulder's width, the pump attended, and either Nera or Sefu able to stop it, and it was true.

The well was opened on the third interval, and the ladder came up out of the dark on Oren's shoulder, six feet of it, and the ladder was green with six weeks of standing water and it moved when Oren put his weight on the second rung, which they had all known it would.

Pell Roke's question was answered on the morning of the day, in one line, and the answer was that the other one gets out because there was one of them and it was short, and the plan's seventh line said it in Nera's hand and Jo Verrick read it back from the slate without softening it: *one person, one interval, and out before the lever comes off, and the answer to how the other one gets out is that there is not another one, and the reason there is not another one is that there is one ladder in that well and it is a person wide and there are eleven feet under it.*

Jo had chosen him, and had asked first, which she said was not the same as choosing, and had been argued with and had held.

"Line first," Nera said, from the string, "and not the man first."

Oren took the end of the draw cord in his left hand and got himself down into it, and the cord came off the board in front of everyone, and at the bottom of six feet there was a foot still out and a hand's width of slack, and there was nothing above that hand but a length of cord and a person at the far end of it, and Nera had said that out loud in the anteroom two days before and had not softened it since.

He was down about two minutes.

The cloth moved.

"Out," Nera said, and then, on the string, to the whole of the room, not to the well: "Out, and shut the well, and the day is over."

He was up in one minute and eleven seconds, and the last part of that was him coming up the last three rungs with the jack in his left hand because he had been told twice to leave it and had decided, apparently, that a screw jack was not a thing you left in a flooded room, and Jo Verrick stood at the well and said, in a voice with no expression in it whatsoever, "I said leave it," and Oren said, "Aye," and put it down on the floor of the anteroom, and it was the heaviest thing in the room for the rest of the day and it stayed where it was.

They shut the well. The wheel turned twice under the floor. The cord held, because the cord was on the stair and not the well. The bypass hatch stayed latched. The gallery began, slowly, audibly, to fill again from below, and the anteroom got its ten back at the fourth bell, and the pump went back on the lever at the fifth, because the plan was for a draw and not for an opinion, and the draw was half done and half undone and the whole of the rest of it was Nera's sentence going round the room with nobody in a position to argue with it.

The turn had come at the second minute of the six. Nera's count, the private one, the eleven-day one, said thirty-four of forty-one had come between one and nine minutes after the lever came off, and this was the second minute, and she did not say a word about it, and Dalla wrote the time of it in the ledger in her own hand and did not put a cause beside it, because Nera had a cause and it was on her own slate and she would not put it on a page she did not hold.

They ran it again the next day, on the same terms, and Oren went down twice, and the second time he left the jack and came up with the pry bar and a canvas sack of the five public-works wedges, and the third time he came up with three of the four lengths of spare timber, because the fourth is five foot nine and the ladder well is a person wide and he is a large man.

The five foot nine never came up. It stayed on the bay's bottom shelf, on the cache mark, with the inventory number chalked on it in Nera's own hand, so that whoever comes next knows which shelf it is on and that it is counted and that nobody has taken it.

And on the fourth trip, before he got in the water, he tied their line to the ladder.

It was the frayed nine-metre line, the one the six of them had come into the Cinder Steps with in the first week, the one that had been cut and re-knotted twice and was the only line any of them still owned. He tied it four times round the ladder's stile above the top rung and set it on the ladder's own foot, because the ladder had been standing in water for six weeks and nobody in the room was willing to be the first person down a wet ladder on their own two feet and Jo would not have the ladder move under a man with a jack.

"Do not tell me not to," he said, to Marek, who had opened his mouth.

"I was not going to," Marek said.

"You were going to write it in the record and you were going to say it was recoverable."

"It is not recoverable," Nera said, from the string. "It is in the water on the ladder foot and in six weeks it will be part of the ladder. Write it down that way, because a line that is *down there* and a line that is *lost* are two different sentences and I have spent seven weeks watching this building confuse them."

And it was written down, by Dalla, in that form, and read back, and Tams put it in the tally under a heading he had not used before and had to invent, and the heading was *spent*, and there was one item under it.

---

The tools came up into a room with about nine people in it and were received, item by item, in the ledger, by name, with a time, by the keeper.

Sefu did it himself, and stood at the bench and read the register out loud while Dalla wrote, and it took twenty minutes, and nobody left the room, and it is the least dramatic twenty minutes anybody in this building has spent since the collapse and the one that decides what happened.

*Screw jack, public-works, one, with its screw and its handle and the handle's grip bound in cord, found on the bay's bottom shelf at the cache mark, condition as found, no claim of any kind made by the person who brought it up.*

*Pry bar, public-works, one, condition as found, the business end under two inches of silt.*

*Public-works hardwood wedges, five, sound, and these are new to this building and they are on a register that is not the Hearthhold's and they are here because a named work required one.*

*Public-works spare timber, four lengths, of which three came up and one is five foot nine and is on the bay's bottom shelf with its number chalked on it, and it stays there, and the inventory line stays open.*

Sefu stopped on the last one and looked up.

"Read the next line and read it twice, and read it into the record rather than into this room, because in a hearing the record is the only part anybody will have."

Dalla read it.

*The items above were recovered by named persons of the trapped crew, from a room under this boundary's air engineer's inventory, under a named work recorded at the boundary's ledger, on a plan written by the boundary's air engineer, and received by the boundary's keeper. They remain public-works equipment, guild-frozen pending a hearing, and no person in this room owns any of them. The crew did not take possession. The crew moved them.*

Nobody argued with it, and Iven said the thing that was underneath it, out loud, because it was underneath it and he was the one who had put the condition there in the twelfth day.

"In five weeks' time there will be a hearing, and in that hearing somebody will ask who put a public-works jack into a Hearthhold work," he said, "and the answer is on that line, and the answer is good, and the answer is also the reason that the jack is now four feet away from being somewhere it can never be taken out of. Once that jack goes under a shore, it does not come out without taking the shore out. So write the other half now while I am standing here: the crew will not be able to take its own tools back, and that is not a loss, because the crew has never owned them, and I want it said before the first of us finds it convenient to say we gave them away."

Tams's tally that night, read out loud because he always read it out loud, and he read the new column first, because that is what he had invented it for.

"Spent," he said. "One nine-metre line, on a ladder foot, in six weeks of standing water, and it is going to be part of that ladder by the end of the month and it is not coming up and I am not going to pretend I will be able to look at it again without feeling sick about it."

And then the material, and then the thing at the end of it that he had been carrying in his head all day and had been waiting for the room to be quiet enough for.

"Stores," he said. "No sound timber over six feet. No lath. No bronze. Seven sound wedges and two dead sticks, and where the seven are, and the head wants four and has three. And, off the register, not ours: a screw jack, a pry bar, five hardwood wedges, and four lengths of timber of which three are on this floor and one is under six feet of water with a number chalked on it."

"And the seat," Jo said, from the frame, before anybody could get to the end of the list, because she had asked for it and she had asked for it before she started.

"Twenty-six, six, nineteen, and a day and a half of a route that pays me," Jo said, "and the fifth cleaning is done, and I have been in that housing twice today and I have taken my own fingers out of it and looked at the lip and I am not going to describe the lip in this room, and the estimate we have been living on since the twenty-ninth day was about four, and the estimate on the valve board since the thirtieth has been four or fewer, and the estimate as of tonight is two or fewer, and I have said it out loud and I would like it written under the material with my name on it and not in a column."

"Written," Dalla said.

"Written, with a time, in my own hand, next to the name, and read back before the ledger closes," Jo said, "because in a month somebody is going to ask whether the crew knew what the draw cost, and the answer has to be on this page and not in my head."

And the anteroom, and the plate, and the bundle it had been wrapped in since the sixth week, sat on a shelf above the bench now because the bench was needed, and it had been on that bench for eight weeks and had not said one word about the jack or the pry bar or the five wedges or the five foot nine that stayed on the shelf with a number chalked on it.

And two floors down and eleven feet under, in a room that had four hours of standing air left in it, the water came back up over the cache mark, over the chalk number, and over the five foot nine, at the rate of a hand's breadth every five minutes, the way it had done for six weeks, and nobody in the anteroom was going to be able to go and look at it again.
