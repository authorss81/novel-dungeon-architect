# Chapter 5 — A Room That Looks Safe

The dry room beyond the broken wall was twelve paces long and eight wide, with two massive piers standing on either side of a broad lintel. Six sleeping pads were set into the floor along the left wall. Shelves held empty jars, a cracked basin, and a stack of thin boards that might once have been school slates. A door with a lockable handle opened inward onto a narrow landing.

Marek stood in the doorway and felt the urge to approve of it.

The floor was dry. The piers were thick. The shelves were built for storage, not treasure. The lock on the door had a solid pin, and the room had an air grille cut high in the far wall. It was the first place below the collapse that looked as if it had been designed for people rather than for stone.

“Declared occupancy,” he said. “Six people, including Leena on a litter. One litter, no more than one hundred and twenty kilograms of gear, water, and pump parts. Four-hour rest period. The room is a candidate, not a certificate.”

Leena looked down from the litter. “Say what happens if the candidate fails.”

“Then we leave.”

“Say all the things that can fail.”

Marek pointed to the piers. “Bearing. Breath. Drainage. Escape. Change.”

“Those are not five things that can fail. They are five ways a room can kill while it remains standing.”

“That is why we test them.”

He divided the work. Iven and Oren would move the litter and place the heaviest gear. Tams would set dead loads on the pads. Pell would time the escape and watch the landing. Leena would call observations from the litter, and Marek would mark the floor and lintel with chalk. Six occupants were present, not six equal loads.

They began with bearing.

At first the piers seemed sound. Marek tapped them, checked the base of each, and ran a strip of grease pencil along the joint where the floor met the wall. The left pier carried its share without visible movement. The right pier did the same. The lintel between them looked massive enough to hold a small building.

Iven placed a stone slab on the left side. Oren set a second slab on the right. Tams added the pump parts and the heavier gear, bringing the total close to the declared load. The litter remained in the center, Leena’s right arm secured in its sling.

“All six present,” Marek said. “Normal declared load.”

He watched the chalk line.

The left pier dropped a hair. The right followed half a beat later. The lintel did not crack, but the hollow note inside it became a whisper.

Leena spoke first. “The two piers are not independent.”

“They have separate bases,” Iven said.

“They share the same lintel.”

Marek went still.

He had looked at the supports. He had measured their widths. He had not followed the force from one to the other. A structure could have two legs and still stand on one rotten beam. He knew the principle. He had failed to apply it because the room looked complete and the deadline was pressing close.

“Mark the lintel,” he said.

Pell drew a line across the stone.

Marek moved to the second check. “Breath.”

Tams found a dry scrap of curtain cloth in the cracked basin and soaked it in lamp oil. He lit it in a covered clay cup, and the smoke rose toward the high grille. At first it vanished into the opening. Then it curled back down the wall in a thin gray ribbon and collected beneath the lintel. The room had an intake, but the exhaust path shared the same shaft as the supply. A gust from the landing would draw smoke and used air back into the room.

Marek opened the door a handspan. The smoke bent toward it, then reversed when the pressure changed.

“Fail,” Leena said.

“Breath fails,” Marek agreed. “We need an independent return.”

Iven pointed to the grille. “There is a baffle behind it.”

“A baffle is not a second opening.”

The smoke continued to return. The cloth on the cup blackened at one corner.

Drainage came next. Tams poured test water into the shallow channel at the floor’s center. It should have run toward the lower drain. Instead, it spread across the pads, then gathered beside Leena’s litter. The lip of the lower passage settled a fraction as the group moved, and the water reversed toward the room. A dark stain rose along one floor seam.

“Drainage fails,” Marek said. “The drain depends on a grade we do not control.”

Pell marked the time. “How long?”

“Less than a minute.”

He tried the door next. The litter had to pass through a narrow landing and turn inward. With six occupants in the room, there was no clear space for the people at the door to stand. Iven and Oren lifted the litter, but its frame caught the handle. The door swung farther inward, pushing the landing occupants toward the weak lip. Pell had to step backward. A thin cascade of dust fell from the edge of the landing.

“Escape fails,” Marek said. “The door consumes the space needed to escape.”

“Change?” Leena asked.

Marek looked at the crowd gathering toward the door. Nothing extraordinary was happening. Six people were simply responding to a narrow opening. A person moved to make room, another followed, and the group’s weight gathered at the landing. The floor there was not designed for a moving crowd. It was designed for one person entering a school room.

He raised his hand. “Treat the room as full. Everyone moves toward the door as if the air has failed.”

They moved.

The landing lip shed dust. The lintel made a hollow sound. The water in the floor channel shifted toward the room. Smoke curled beneath the door.

Pell backed away. “It gets worse when everyone does the right thing.”

“That is change,” Marek said. “The room has no plan for ordinary panic.”

For a moment, no one spoke. The room had passed an empty inspection. It had two piers, a lock, shelves, and a high vent. It had failed every promise the group needed from it.

Leena’s voice came quietly from the litter. “Keeping it would not be caution. It would be choosing who died first.”

Marek looked at the door, then at the shared lintel. “Then we open a controlled breach.”

Iven’s face hardened. “You mean through the wall.”

“Only the infill. Iven, Oren, take the timber line. Tams, clear the shelves. Pell, watch the landing and call any movement. Leena, call the receiving side.”

They found the adjacent domestic room through a thin section of masonry between the lintel and the outer jamb. The jamb belonged to a wall that had been built later and bore into stable rock. Marek marked two receiving paths: the side pier and the outer jamb. Neither would be enough alone. Together, they could keep the lintel from dropping into the occupied room while the non-bearing infill came out.

The wedges had taken the gate’s weight by then. Iven and Oren pulled one short upright free while the other stayed as a chock. Iven set the freed upright beneath the lintel. Oren braced it with the second chock line. Tams and Marek used the pry bar to remove the infill in pieces. Each piece was dust-coated and light enough to carry. The opening widened. Cold air moved through it, carrying the smell of old cooking smoke.

Marek braced the jack beneath the timber and called for Iven to take the load. The screw turned. The lintel shifted. For one breath, the room held.

Then pressure moved through the wall.

It was the old load finding a new path because the jack had taken a fraction too much. The screw jack kicked sideways. Marek caught it with his right hand to keep the head seated against the timber. Stone dropped from the edge of the opening and trapped his fingers against the metal.

Pain came up through his wrist. He tried to pull free. The jack’s head was pinned, and the upright had begun to bow.

“Iven!” he shouted.

Iven dropped the pry bar and seized the timber line. Oren took the weight beside him. The counter-supports groaned, then caught. The short upright split along its grain, leaving a shorter length that could still be used for a splint or a chock. The stone around Marek’s hand shifted enough for Iven to get a wedge into the gap. He pulled Marek free by the coat, then worked the jack head away with both hands.

Marek’s right hand opened and closed. The fingers answered slowly. The thumb moved a fraction too far sideways. He tried to place the grease pencil between two fingers and could not keep it there.

“I’m sorry,” Iven said. “I should have taken the jack.”

“You were holding the line.”

“I should have taken the jack.”

Marek looked at the hand. It was already swelling. “It is not a lesson yet.”

“It will be when you cannot write.”

Pell had stopped marking time. The landing was quiet, but the dust from the lip continued to fall.

“Clear the useful gear,” Marek said. “Leave the bulk.”

They pulled the remaining water, the lamp, the line, the tools that still worked, the route maps, the flat repair boards they could carry, and the sound short upright. The old room’s spare stock stayed behind: the cracked jars, the boards, the stone dead loads, and the sealed supplies that had made the room look like a refuge. None of it could be reached safely if the lintel moved again. Taking it would mean crossing the same landing with a loaded litter and a floor that had already shown them its limit.

Marek looked at the opening into the domestic room. A washline hung beyond it. A low shelf held a covered pot. The room had been used, not stored like a vault.

“Go,” he said. “Move Leena across first.”

Iven and Oren took the litter. Tams went ahead. Pell followed with the chalk, looking back once at the abandoned refuge.

Leena caught Marek’s left sleeve. “Your hand.”

“Later.”

“Now I know you are hurt.”

“Now you know I cannot do the next job alone.”

He let her see the failure plainly. The words hurt more than the swelling.

In the new room, Marek tried to copy the old floor plan onto the back of the route card. His right hand could hold the grease pencil only if he clamped it between two fingers. The line wandered. He stopped, handed the card to Iven, and asked him to hold it flat.

“Your left hand is not much better after compensating,” Iven said.

“No. But it is still mine.”

Marek copied the route slowly with his left hand. The line was uneven. He did not hide it.

The old plan showed a service shaft beyond the domestic room, with several independent routes converging on a maintenance space. Behind the wall, a faint pulse moved through the mineral seam. Dust slid downward, then stopped.

Leena looked toward it. “What is behind there?”

Marek pressed his left palm to the wall. The pressure was there, distant and uneven, like a heartbeat behind stone.

“Another room,” he said. “Or the part of this one we have not met.”

The abandoned refuge groaned behind them.

Marek folded the uneven plan against his chest.

“We follow the civic route,” he said. “Before the water decides we have run out of time.”
