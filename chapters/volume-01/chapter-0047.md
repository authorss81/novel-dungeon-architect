# Chapter 47 — Four at a Time

Nobody could put a name at the end of the page, and the reason they could not was a woman on a litter who had struck a clause off the plan in the thirty-ninth day for exactly that reason.

"A plan with a person on the end of it has already chosen the person," Leena Orr had said, "and the person chosen is the one who cannot say no."

So the page ended with a room, and the room was the wash rooms, and the person who asked the room was Renn Vale, and she did it at the woman's own door, standing on the step, and she did not send anybody.

"You lifted our cover at the second bell this morning and you said *grit* to Mikka and went away," Renn said. "Mikka told me. And I am not here about a cover. I am here because there is a line in a stair and we have to find out whether a person can go over it and come back, and nobody in this building is going to make that decision except the people who will be standing on it. So I am asking you. And I am telling you what it is first, because the crew did not tell me either."

She told her. She stood on the step and told a woman who had said nothing for seven weeks what the line was, where it went, how long the stone lip was, and what happened in the four minutes at the end, and she said that nobody would be on the far side of it except two men with nothing in their hands and that the far side had a rail in it and that nobody had ever stood behind the rail.

The woman said one word, which was *yes*, and then she said, "And my mother."

"Your mother is not asked by me," Renn said, "and I am going to say why, and it is not kindness. Your mother is a person who was not walking in the fourth week and is walking now, and the only reason she is walking is that Isel Ferrow's fever broke and she got a bed back, and if I ask her to go over a stair then I am spending the best thing this building has produced this month on a test, and I will not do that. So she is not asked, and if she says she wants to go then I will take that as the answer and I will not argue with a woman on a step."

The mother was inside and heard, and came to the door, and said: "Then I am going, and I have not been asked, and I want it written down that I was not asked, because I do not want to be asked and I do not want to be *protected*." She looked at Renn. "Put it in your hand. Not hers."

Renn put it in her own hand, and Dalla Sen read it back at the anteroom, and it is in the ledger with a name on it, and it is the first entry in nine weeks that is a person's own demand to be left alone.

---

The two of them went over at the fourth bell, in a named period, on a day the school was not running, with the terms read at the threshold in Jo's hand for the sixth time, and the crew on the far side of the cord with nothing in their hands, which Jo Verrick had insisted on and had been laughed at for, and which turned out to be the whole of the finding.

The bell at the threshold that morning was rung by Ruk Sella, who had done it once before and who was asked again in person at the door by Dalla Sen and who said yes in one word and repeated the whole of it back again, because that is the practice now and it is not the practice she was got the first time.

The slab was away when they stepped to the lip. It had been away for about a second and a half, which was a different length of time from the nine weeks it had been away before, and Nera had said that on the valve board in a column nobody read.

Leena was on the frame at the service bend and she had the slate and she had the four minutes and she said the number out loud at both ends, and it is the sixth time she has done that and the number has never once been in dispute.

The woman went first, because she was faster than her mother and knew the floor. The lamp went across on the line. The mother went second, with the woman on one side and Oren's hand under her other elbow, and Oren put his hand there and took it away again inside a second because she did not want it, and did not say anything about that afterwards to anybody.

The slab came back. The line came in through the block, up the open face of the wall, into the head of the shore, and the shore took it, and the common landing's floor took it, and the wall did not come out.

Then there was a wheel and a rope and a delay, and the slab went out again, and both of them came back on it, and the whole thing from the first step to the second step off was two minutes and a bit, and Jo read the two numbers back and nobody had anything to say about them.

"What was behind the rail?" Marek said.

And the woman from the wash rooms, who had not said anything for seven weeks, said nine words, and they went into the record, and they are the reason there is a stair in this building with a name on it, and not nine weeks of a pump with a rail on it.

"There are steps," she said. "Behind the rail. Four of them, going down. And the landing that used to be there is not there, and the first one is a step and not a hole, and it is cut out of the same stone as the wall you cut your face in, and it is dry."

Nobody said anything.

"They took the landing out in three pieces," Iven said, after a while. "The door, the lintel, and the sill. Three pieces. And the steps are not the landing. The landing was the floor you stood on at the top and the steps go down from it, and nobody has taken four steps out of a stair in forty years because four steps are not worth a school and a wash door." He sat down on the step, which he did not often do. "There is a stair going down under that slab. It has been there the whole time. Nobody has been able to reach it because the first landing was a terminus and the slab kept bringing everybody back to it, and that is the whole of what this mechanism has been doing for nine weeks, and it is not a monster and it is not a trap and it is a machine doing the only job it had."

"And where does it go?"

"Down," Iven said. "Which is the only direction a stair goes. And I am not going to tell you how far, because the answer is that nobody in this building has any idea, and a man who says *down* about a stair in the dark is telling you the truth and nothing else, and I would like it written down in those words."

It was written down in those words.

---

Then the arithmetic, which is where the whole of the next two days went.

Tams Quill had the board turned round and had the pump's rhythm on it and had a piece of chalk, and he did not want to do it and did it.

"Four at a time," he said. "That is the slab and the near lip, and the near lip is the working floor a person has to stand on to get onto a slab, and it is about two foot six from the cord to the stone, and there is a rail on the far side and nobody is going to stand on a rail. So the honest number on the slab at once is four, and Jo is going to check me and tell me if it is five."

"It is four," Jo said. "I measured the rail in the thirty-third day for a different reason and it is four."

"Four. And a crossing is four minutes, and that is Leena's number and it has been hers since the thirtieth day and I am not touching it. And the pump goes twenty-six, six, nineteen, thirty-four minutes of pump in a forty-minute cycle. So: four at a time, four minutes, and you have to be off the stair and clear before the lever comes off, because the six minutes after the lever are the ones Nera's count lives in and nobody is to plan to a number she has not given them."

"Which gives us eight crossings," said Marek.

"Which gives you eight crossings if the arithmetic is being done by somebody in a hurry." Tams put the chalk down, which is how he corrected himself. "Thirty-two minutes of crossing in thirty-four minutes of pump. Two minutes of margin, and two minutes is nothing, and two minutes is a third of the rest, so if the pump takes one extra minute on the way up then you have no margin at all. Six minutes of margin, and six minutes is nothing, and six minutes is also exactly the length of the rest, so if the pump takes one extra minute on the way up then you have no margin at all. And the pump does. It took eleven minutes to get going in the ninth day and everybody has seen it be slow."

"So how many people is this boundary?" said someone.

That was the question, and it took a day and a half, and it produced the number twenty-nine, and the number was wrong.

Ilsa Norr counted her own: nine children and herself, which is ten, and twelve on a market day, and she said the two extra were not to be counted because they were not there and would not be there on a Thursday. Mikka Hale counted hers and the clinic's, and would not give the number in a room, and gave it in Dalla's ear, and Dalla wrote it. Renn counted hers and did not give it, and Dalla did not write it, and Renn said why, and it was the same reason she had given for the door, and it was the reason the crew was on the far side of the cord with empty hands.

Dalla read the total out: twenty-nine.

"Write it," Pell Roke said, from the bench, "and write under it, in Tams's hand, *twenty-nine is the number of people who were in four rooms at the counting hour on the day of the counting*, and write under that, in Jo's hand, *a building does not announce its own population and a count is a photograph*, and read both back, because I have been in this room for nine weeks and every number we have ever written down has turned out to be true on the day it was written and wrong by Thursday, and we have got into the habit of writing the number as though it were the fact instead of the photograph."

They wrote it, and it did not help, and the test was still run, because the alternative was a route nobody had ever used.

---

Ilsa Norr released her own term, and it was the biggest thing anybody in this boundary has done since the day they all arrived, and she did it in eleven words and then had to explain them for an hour.

"The school corner is not a work site, the school does not suspend again, and I am not losing days to a hole somebody else is digging," she had said on the thirty-eighth day, in front of nine people, and all three of those were in the terms in her own hand.

"The school runs on a Thursday," she said now, to the whole anteroom, with the board in her hand and nine children not in the room because it was the fifth bell. "It runs four days in seven and not on the three the corridor is shut, and the days it runs are the days this building is fullest, and the days it is fullest are the days there is most to move. And the fourth term says no work at that stair on a day the school runs. I wrote that term. I am lifting it for one day, for one hour, for a route, and I want it read back that I lifted it and when and why, and I want it in the same hand as the rest of my terms and not in Jo's."

"Read the reason back," Dalla said.

"The reason is that a route that cannot carry a school is not a route, it is a hole with a rail on it," Ilsa said, "and I have been a person in this building for nine weeks saying no to things on the grounds that they cost me days, and I have been right every time, and this is the one time that being right is the wrong answer, and I would like the room to notice that the first thing I have ever unsaid in this building is in the direction of *yes*, and that I have noticed it too."

Mikka Hale lifted hers in the same breath and would not be talked out of it.

"The covers get lifted at the second bell and the fifth and the woman from the wash rooms lifts them and nobody else, and if she is not on that stair then nobody is in her room, and I am not having that on a day when she is on a stair." She put her hand flat on the shelf, which she did before she said the thing she meant to say. "So: the crossing is between the fourth bell and the fifth, and the fifth-bell cover check is done by me, on my own, at the fifth bell, whichever side of that bell the stair is on, and I have told the woman and she has said yes and she did not look at me when she said it, which is how she says things that cost her."

And then the collision, which is the reason Thursday is what Thursday is, and which was raised at the review and argued for two hours and not solved.

The near landing is a working floor and it is the crew's work site, and the plan for the shore had left the last three days of work on it: the packing out of the cord's anchorage, the review, the record, and the packing of the tools that were going to be taken up the nine steps to the anteroom.

"We are on the near landing on the same day," Dalla said, reading it off the two pages. "The crew are on the stair for the last of the work. The boundary is on the stair for the crossing. One landing."

"That is two jobs on one floor," Iven said, "and a floor is not bigger on Thursday."

"And the lip," said Jo.

And that was the one nobody had said until Jo said it, and once she had said it the room could not put it down.

The lip is one stone, the whole length of it, and the line-block is set into its base, and Oren Sill had proved in the thirty-sixth day with a line tied to the pump plinth and a stone and a pull that the whole length of that lip is one piece and that it moves about an inch when it is pulled at both ends.

"The lip takes the crossing," Nera said. "Not the shore. The shore takes the line. The lip is the edge of the hole and it is where a person stands to step onto a slab, and if a person is standing at the edge of that stone then the stone is carrying a person at its edge and a line at its base, and those are two things on one piece of stone, and it has been taking two things on one piece of stone for six weeks without anybody noticing because nothing has ever been on it."

"It has taken a line and nothing else until this morning," Iven said.

"It has taken a line and a bell and a crowd's worth of nothing for nine weeks," Nera said, "and it is about to be asked to take a line and a man, and I am the person in this room who stops things, and I am going to say this and I am not going to be argued with: if the lip is carrying a crossing and it moves, then the crossing does not finish, and the people who are on the far side of a slab when it moves are on a lip eleven feet over water with a rail and no floor behind it. I do not know what happens to them and neither does anybody in this room and that is why I am saying it."

"So we do not run them at the same time," said Marek.

"No," said Dalla Sen, from the anteroom, with her pencil down. "That is a hundred per cent correct and it is also the answer to a question nobody asked, and here it is: the crossing is the boundary's work and the last of the work is the crew's, and the terms say the crew's hours are given and not owed and the boundary does not get to move them, and the boundary does not get to tell six people who are going to leave that they can stay another four days because the boundary is ready. And the crew does not get to say *not on a Thursday* to a room that has a route in it and no day to use it."

"So which," said Jo.

The room did not answer it. That is in the record, because Dalla wrote *the room did not answer it* and read it back, and four people in that anteroom said afterwards, separately, and in Dalla's hearing, that they had not answered it because they did not have an answer, and not because they were being careful.

What was written, and agreed, and marked by nobody because there was nothing yet to mark, was this, and Jo read it twice and it is on the board and in the ledger in her own hand:

*The crossing is the boundary's. The crew is on the near landing for the last of the work at the same time, because the crew is not a thing that leaves a room in order that a room may be used. The lip takes both, and the lip is one stone, and Nera Oduya has said in the record that the lip carrying a crossing and a line is the condition she will stop the work for, and that the stop is the cloth and the cord and a keeper's word, and that she does not know what happens to a person on the far side of that slab and neither does anybody else.*

*Nobody has run a full relay yet, and nobody is going to run one at four minutes a crossing without finding out that the four minutes is a plan and not a measurement.*

That last line was Tams's, and it is the sentence that everybody in this building would point at afterwards, and he wrote it at the fifth bell without knowing what it was a warning about, and Jo read it back twice, and neither of them knew yet.

The relay was ordered by Mikka Hale and she gave her reasons and they were reasons about people and not about arithmetic.

"Four at a time and thirty-two minutes and the slow ones in the middle," she said. "The fast at the edges. The school first, because the school is nine children and they will do what they are told and they will do it fast, and Ilsa has told me they are to be sent out at a walk and not at a run, and I have written that down and I am not having a school run anywhere. Then the clinic and the wash rooms and the household with the fever in it — the slow ones, in the middle, where there is margin on both sides. And the fast ones last, because the fast ones can be out of the door in a minute and the slow ones cannot, and a relay whose last crossing is a slow crossing is a relay that ends in a doorway with a person in it."

"Which means the middle crossing is the one that is always the most loaded," said Leena, "because the fast ones have gone both ways by then and the slow ones are all still in."

"Yes," Mikka said. "And that is the plan, and it is the right plan, and I want it on the page that I made it and that I would make it again, and I want the page to say what happens if the window comes early while the middle is out. And the answer is that the people on the far side come back on the slab and the people in the middle are on the near landing with a rail at their backs and a lip under their feet, and it is four minutes to get four people off that landing and there is one landing and one bearer and one cord, and the bell is rung and the people at the anteroom steps are told to go up, and I have said the whole thing out loud on the day I made the plan and nobody wrote it down until tonight."

Dalla wrote it down, and read it back, and nobody in the room disagreed with a word of it, and nobody in the room had said any of it out loud before.

The count at the end of the first hour was twenty-nine, and it was in the room at the end of the second, and the room did not know it.
