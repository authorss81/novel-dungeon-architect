# Chapter 122 — Nothing Here To Be Sent For

**Shut for the corridor on the second day of the twenty-fourth week, and the room at the kitchen-line end stood with a lamp in it and a lamp is not a light in a cold room, and the two people who were in it all morning had a third thing to say to a room on the top end, which is that a woman with a tally and no power had sent a question up four days of road about a person who does not exist.**

She was not the same woman.

"**I am not her office and I have not met her,**" said the road hand, in the anteroom, with her hands open and empty and a wooden tally against her knee. "**I am a road hand and I carry what is on the sheet and I am going to say what I am before I hand it over, because you are going to want to hand it back and I would rather you did not.**

"**It has come off the surface inspection line. It has an office on it and it has a number on it, and the number is a number and not a name, and the line is a line of stations on the surface and I have been on it. It is not the guild's. It is the other one.**"

Bel Ordry read it standing up. It was half a sheet and it had been folded twice and the fold had been made by somebody in a hurry and pressed with a thumb.

"**Then you are the third body this building has had that our four sentences do not name,**" he said, "**and I wrote those four sentences for one office and used them for a second, and I said out loud in this room that I would not write a second one in a fortnight, and a document that gets amended every time it is inconvenient is the guild's counter-draft with better manners.**

"**So I am not going to write anything. Say what it says and I will hear it in a room and I will answer in a room and there will be no piece of paper at the end of it, and that is not a refusal, that is the only shape this boundary has.**"

"**Read it out,**" said Renn Vale.

He read it out. It was two lines and a space for a reply.

"**One: an inscription bearing or otherwise was observed on a structure within a boundary beneath this line, and the condition and the limit under which it acts have been given. Two: this office requires the name, or the description sufficient to identify, of the person by whom the condition is kept, so that enquiries may be addressed and so that the condition may from time to time be confirmed as holding.**"

Then he turned the sheet over, and there was a printed line under the fold that he had not reached, with a heading over it and three things under it in a small competent hand, and he had it halfway to the table before the air engineer's voice came off the wall three steps above his head.

"**Out.**"

One word, not loud. Nera Oduya had not come down the steps and had not touched the table; she had read it upside down on her way past, which she has done once before in this building, and the clerk saw her do it and did not get a full sentence out of her.

"**That is a line on a form and not a mistake,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**Out,**" said Nera Oduya.

Bel Ordry asked her why, once. She looked at him, and he put his hand down.

She took the clerk's chalk out of Dalla Sen's hand, and Dalla Sen let her, and she struck the heading and the three things under it in nine seconds, in her own hand, and gave the chalk back, and went up three steps and said nothing else about it.

"**Enter it,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and enter that it is the fourth time, and enter that she was not asked and gave no reason, and that I did not ask her, and that nobody in this room asked her, and I want that read out as a different sentence from the one before it.**

"**And enter that the line was headed for structures in the boundary whose condition is kept by a person, and that three things were under it, and that one of the three was a room with a row on this boundary's own plan and an empty third column by a woman's own condition, and that somebody four days up the middle passage has put a keeper against a row on a page that has never left this building.**

"**I am not going to write the rest of that sentence and I am not going to explain why, and that is not an omission, that is me declining to be the person who puts a page and a stranger into the same line.**"

For long enough that the pump's check came off and went again, the room said nothing.

"**Say the second half of that back to me,**" said Renn Vale, "**and say it slowly, because I have been a person in this building for twenty-four weeks and I want to hear it the way a person on a road would hear it.**"

Bel Ordry read it again. When he had finished, the road hand said, "**That is the whole of it. And I will say the thing that is not on it, because you are going to be asked for it later and I would rather you had it now: the sheet has a line for a reply and the line is short, and the office that sent it has a file with your boundary in it, because a boundary that signs a charter with no name on it gets cited, and a boundary that gets cited gets a line with a name on it eventually, and I have carried that shape four times this year.**"

"**What is in the file,**" said Marek Venn.

"**A charter with an office's number at the top of it and no name, and a copy of clause four, and a note from a surveyor that the sector is stable.**"

"**Then the road is four days up the middle passage to the middle gate and forty days on from the top of it to the guild file,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and the answer this boundary gives to that line is going to be given in a room to a person, and it is going to be seven words long, and no piece of paper is going to leave this building carrying any part of it.**"

"**I have carried four of these,**" said the road hand, "**and I am not going to carry a fifth one back down a road that goes four days and then on, and I would like that in your book as being a decision a person made and not as being a service this boundary got for nothing.**"

---

**Renn Vale answered it herself, standing on the landing, in about nine seconds, and Dalla Sen wrote it down exactly, and the woman with the tally wrote it out on the back of her own hand in her own letters before she went, and there was no clerk's mark and no ring and no name.**

"**There is no such person here.**"

"**That is the whole of it?**" said the road hand.

"**That is the whole of it and I am going to say the other half so that it goes back down that road in your head and not in a pocket.**" Renn Vale had her hand flat on the landing rail and she was not looking at the man with the tally. "**There is a woman with a slate and seven terms and an eighth, and a cook, and a clerk, and a woman with a rule who is in a room before anybody else. None of them is a post. None of them can be addressed by anybody. If that office wants to confirm a condition it can come up the middle passage and stand in the room the way a competent person stood in it a fortnight ago and be told the condition by the person who wrote it, in words, in a room, to a person, and that is what the four sentences say and it is the only thing anybody in this building has ever offered anybody and I would like it written down that I have said that and that it is the same offer I make to a stranger as the one I make to a neighbour.**

"**And I have been asked in a room in the last month whether I would rather be named and refused than titled and found, and I said that I did not know which of the two I would survive, and I have had a fortnight to find out and I have not found out, and I am going to say the other half of that too, because it is the same road: a name can be refused and a title cannot. A title is a box with a person's shape in it, and any body that has never met me can put a letter in the box and the letter will find me because the box is the point.**

"**There is no such person here. And if that office writes to this boundary again, a person will read it in a room and answer it in a room, and there will be nothing on a page that can be carried down a road, and I am not sorry about that, and I am not going to pretend it is a service.**"

She would take it back, she said, and she would take the shape of it and not the words, because the words were a woman's and the shape was hers; and she went down nine steps and along a corridor two foot eight wide and past a waist whose third wedge is up a quarter of an inch and a bit, at a walk, and did not put a hand on the fill, and nobody walked her out past it because the corridor was shut.

---

**The rest of the day was in a room with a lamp in it, and it was about clay, and four people in that room had opinions they had earned.**

Jo Verrick had the sill off the top of her list of things nobody had looked at. It is nine inches deep and eleven feet of laid stone, and it is a sill, and it is the best sill in this building, and the water comes up under a floor that is dished toward it.

"**I have got it out,**" she said, "**on the low side, the last two feet of it, and I have put the stones back exactly as they were and I am not going to leave them out, because a boundary with a hole in its own sill is a boundary that has decided to lose the room and has not admitted it.**

"**And the bedding under the low side of that sill is stone in clay. Not stone in lime. Clay. Somebody packed this sill in clay about nine hundred years ago, and I have found the tool marks on the underside of the bed and they are not a mason's, and I am not going to tell you whose they are, because a hand is not a name and this Hearthhold has been very clear about that for twenty-four weeks.**"

"**What does that do to us,**" said Marek Venn.

"**It tells you the only repair available in this building is the repair that was last done to it,**" said Jo Verrick. "**And that is a good sentence, and I am not going to say the other one.**"

She did say the other one, about a minute later, and she said it to the room and not to him.

"**The other one is that clay shrinks, and I have watched that anteroom frame go out a finger and a bit in a fortnight because of clay, and I am not blaming anybody for it because the frame is a frame and a sill is a sill, and the same thing goes wrong in both.**

"**So if we pack that sill in clay we are buying the room. And I am telling you now, out loud, before we do it, that in four weeks that sill will be a finger narrower than it is tonight, and in eight it will be a finger narrower than that, and there is no stone in lime in this Hearthhold and no timber and no bond, and nothing anybody can put in there will stop that, and the only thing this packing will do is move the water from under a floor to under a threshold and take the corridor's low end out of it.**

"**That is a good repair. I want that said first, in my words, in a room, before anybody in here starts feeling sorry for me. It is a good repair and it is temporary and it costs clay we do not have an account of and a room with nine children in it.**"

Iven Tallo had been on the hearth stones at the far end of the room with his own two hands and a slate and no lamp, because he says a lamp is for finding a thing and not for looking at one.

"**Then say the thing I have been waiting all morning for somebody to ask me,**" he said, "**which is whether I would pack it.**

"**I would. And I am not going to, and the reason is not pride and I would like it in the book that I have said that, because a man in my trade who says no for pride is a man in my trade who is about to be asked for something he cannot hold.**

"**A sill is nine inches deep and eleven feet long and it is the one place on this run of corridor where you can put something into a joint and have it go the way you want it to go. If I packed that sill I would be doing the best piece of work I have had in eleven months. And if I packed that sill then the room at the kitchen-line end belongs to me, because I would know what is under it and how far and in what direction, and nobody else in this building would, and a man who is the only person who knows what is under a room gets asked about that room, and then he gets asked about it by somebody four days up a road.**"

"**You would not be the only one,**" said Marek Venn. "**I would be.**"

"**Then there would be two of you and it is worse,**" said Iven Tallo. "**And I am a suspended bond and an apprentice off the roll and an account of my own unpaid trade in a clerk's book, and I am not going to be a man in this Hearthhold that a form can be filled in about. The clerk can put my no in her book in my own words, and I would like it in there in a hand somebody else can read, because I have watched two men in my trade say a thing on a landing and have it repeated wrong in a month.**"

Dalla Sen wrote it in her own hand and read it out and Iven Tallo said yes.

---

**A fourth person in that room had not been asked for a work and had not given one. She was the cook, she had been in the room since the first bell, and she had said almost nothing, and what she said at the fourth bell was the sentence the whole week is built on.**

"**I have a hearth in that room and I have not lit it in six weeks,**" said Mikka Hale. "**That is a thing I do every day and I have not done it for six weeks, and I would like somebody in this room to understand what that is, because it is not tidiness and it is not religion.**

"**Nine children sit on those stones. I have lit a fire under a floor in a bad room twice in my life and once the flags cracked and once a child was sick behind the plinth for a day and a half. So I do not light it. And I have told nobody why, because nobody asked, and I have been carrying that for six weeks as a thing about myself and it is not a thing about me. It is a thing about a room with a hearth in it that nobody can be trusted with.

"**And I am going to say the rest of it, and I want the clerk to have the chalk out, because this is the kind of sentence that gets said once and then gets improved.**"

"**The chalk is out,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**I can light that fire the day that room is shut and the nine of them are not in it, and not one day before, and not one day after, and I am not going to be asked to make an exception and I am not going to be asked to give a figure for when I might.

"**And I want it entered that I know exactly what I have just done. I have said a thing about a fire in a room and a cook in it, and there is a hole in that floor, and I have said the fire waits. I have not been asked to be hard. I have been asked what I do, and I do that, and I would like somebody to be angry with me about it after this and I would like them to do it in a room and not in a month when something has gone wrong.**"

Not one person in this building was angry with her about it that week. Dalla Sen entered that, and entered that she had asked for it, and entered that the cook had said she would rather somebody were.

**A man of twenty-two was fifty-seven days and fifty-seven nights into a count that was his own, and he was at the kitchen line with a tally and four pails and no standing anywhere in this argument, and at the end of the day he said one thing into the kitchen, to nobody, and it went in the book because the cook was there.**

"**I can hold a lamp and count and I have never packed anything in my life,**" he said. "**If anybody wants a body on that sill I am the body. I am not asking for anything. I am telling you it is there.**"

"**That is not an offer,**" said Mikka Hale. "**That is a boy telling a room he is cheap. Go to bed.**"

He was at the fourth step at the sixth bell. The count did not move, and nobody asked it to, and Dalla Sen read out the day's increment anyway because she reads it every day, and the two columns reconciled, and a boy of fourteen was on the bench at the top of the nine steps with his ear against the stone and his crutch across his knees and had said nothing all day, which is his trade.
