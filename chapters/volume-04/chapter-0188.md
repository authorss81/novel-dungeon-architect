# Chapter 188 — How Many Of Us

The third day of the thirty-sixth week is a Wednesday, and the corridor is open, and the school ran for the thirty-sixth morning in the room at the kitchen-line end. Nine children went up the nine steps at the first bell with the woman who keeps that room ahead of them, and nine children crossed the anteroom twice, and the light came off the flags at the slant it comes at on open mornings.

Twice is the ordinary figure. The nine go up to that room in the morning and they come down through the anteroom to the far door at midday, and they go up again in the afternoon, and on a shut day none of it happens and nobody in this building has ever thought of that as a loss. On a day when six strangers are standing about the floor it becomes a thing a room counts, and this morning it got counted four times before the second bell by four different people who were not counting it on purpose.

It was seen by the woman who empties a room before a work on a landing and puts nine along a corridor wall, last one first. It was seen by the woman who keeps a corridor, who has a figure for a room and has said twice in this room that the figure is what a room is permitted and not what is in it. It was seen by a foreman of nine years who looked at nine children go past him and then stood looking at a wall for about a minute and a half without saying anything at all. And it was seen by a clerk, who entered the number of a crossing in a book the way she has entered it every school morning since the second week, and who did not think about the other three while she was doing it.

The wall is on the other side of the room from the trestle. It has five and a half shores standing on the common landing side where a district's book says six, and a brace of a school's own beech pushing on it from the kitchen-line side, and a line of nothing about two fingers wide along the join on that side, with nine children sitting on the other side of two feet of it.

Renn Vale watched it at the first bell, which is what she does on an open morning, and came back and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times. She did not say anything else about it, and a clerk entered it as a figure of nothing, and a man who reads a board told her on Monday that a figure of nothing is not a number, and she entered that too, and neither of the two entries is a rule.

At the second bell Dalla Sen turned her slate over and put the six terms on the anvil end of the trestle, face up, where anybody could get at them.

"**The obvious thing,**" she said, "**and I am going to say it before anybody has said it and I am going to say it as a clerk and not as a person who has been waiting all week for somebody to say it, because I have. So here it is. Two crews, one wall, one morning. Five of them and five of ours, the same six terms on both sides, and one set of hours in one book with the two numbers on two lines under the same heading.**"

"**Go at it, then, and put the part that is wrong with it in the room,**" said Renn Vale, from the fourth step, with the slate against her hip and the trestle between them like a thing neither of them was going to lean on.

Dalla Sen had the chalk down. She had had it down for two days, on and off, which is a habit and not a decision, and the reason she had it down was that a figure was coming and she did not want to be holding a chalk while one did.

"**The part that is wrong with it is that one book is one place somebody can come and read a crew's hours, and this boundary has spent thirty-five weeks refusing to be a place a person can be sent for.**"

Dalla Sen had said it in a clerk's voice, which is a voice that has been trained to find the one object in a sentence that can be carried out of the room by somebody else, and the object was the book, and she knew it before she finished the sentence.

"**That is your half against and you have had it ready since Monday,**" put in Bel Ordry from the step, "**and you have said the other half of it every time for five weeks, so say the other half now, or this time do not bother with the half at all.**"

"**I am not going to bother with the half,**" said Dalla Sen, "**because there is a better half and it is not mine. If two crews are in one book, then the book has a column in it headed for the person responsible, and I know what happens to a column with a heading on it in this building, because a man who reads a board has refused three things in eight days for three different reasons, and a woman who keeps a corridor has refused to head one for the whole of her life in this building.**"

Emrys Callow came off the bench where his packs were and stood in the middle of the floor.

"**You have got to the middle of it in two sentences and I am going to make you listen to the rest of it,**" said Callow, and the anteroom went quieter in a way that had nothing to do with a pump. "**You have told a room that a form cannot tell the difference between a crew carrying for a man and a crew standing next to a man, and that is true, and I have been saying it to myself for four days and it has not once made me feel better. Here is the rest of it. I do not want my five in your book. I do not want my hours in a book that a district can read, and I do not want them in a book that a foreman four days up that passage can read, because a foreman four days up that passage is the man whose name is in both lines of the form that made ordinary ground into a working road, and the whole reason I came down here is that he is.**"

Dalla Sen did not look up from the slate. "**Then do not put them in,**"

"**Then we are not in the same book, and if we are not in the same book then I cannot read what my five did, and neither can you, and that is the whole of the problem and I have been carrying it up and down nine steps since Tuesday.**"

Then the woman at the counter who does not carry put her hand on the edge of the trestle and asked for the thing she had said on Monday she wanted, and asked for it in the plainest words anybody had used in this room in a fortnight.

"**I want a figure of what is in this building,**" said Nella Bray, and it came out of her in the voice of a woman reading a line off a counter and not the voice of a woman asking a favour, and both of those are the same voice in her and nobody in the room had heard it before.

Nobody spoke, and the pump got four strokes in.

"**I am not asking for a number of the children,**" she said. "**I am not asking for a number of who is standing in this room. I am asking for a number of bodies in a building on a morning, and it is a thing I have stood at a counter for nineteen years and have never once been sent. Every instrument that has ever come through that counter about a building is a number of what a building is permitted. Not one, in nineteen years, has been a number of what is in it.**"

She put both hands flat on the trestle then, which she had done once in this room before, and the two hands made a shape that a clerk would recognise and a foreman would not.

Renn Vale came down one of the four steps and stopped. "**And you need it for a reason,**" she said, "**and not because you would like it.**"

She had not said it as a kindness. She had said it the way a person says *tell me the load* before a man hands her a bar, and Nella Bray, who has watched four hundred people not do that, gave her the load.

"**I need it for a reason,**" said Nella Bray. "**I have to stand in a room on Friday with two crews in it and a clerk writing, and if I do not know how many bodies are between the walls then I cannot tell one crew from the other in anything I have ever read, and I have been trying to do that job for six weeks with a figure of what rooms are permitted and it does not work. Ask a counter clerk to separate two crews in a room using a permitted occupancy and see what happens.**"

Renn Vale had not sat down all morning and did not sit down now. "**Then the answer is no,**" she said.

She took the slate from under her arm and held it against her hip and went round the room, which is a thing she has done twice in thirty-five weeks.

"**No, and the reason belongs to me and not to a rule, because there is no rule. A room's rating is a number said to a page, and it is not a count of the people who cross it, and I said that out loud in this room on the Friday of last week for the first time in thirty-five weeks and I would like it noticed that it is the same sentence and that it is now going to be the answer to a stranger.**"

"**Mine is shorter and it is not mine,**" said Bel Ordry, from where he was. "**The first column of that page has been a permission for thirty-five weeks and it is not going to become a count because a stranger asked for it in a room with strangers in it. If it changes today then everything anybody has ever read off that page was about a different thing, and I will not do that in a room with a road hand's tally lying on the table.**"

"**I read walls,**" said Jo Verrick, with the string still in her hand from the first bell. "**I have been asked twice this morning whether I will say how many people are in a room, and I am not able to, and it is not modesty. A figure of people in a room goes stale in a morning. I have watched a figure of a wall go stale in thirty-five weeks, and it took two of us a week to work out which half of it we had been reading. I am not going to be the instrument for the other kind.**"

Then the cook, from the kitchen-line doorway, with the cloth over her arm.

"**I know how many I feed,**" said Mikka Hale, and she did not come through the doorway. "**I am not going to write it down and I am not going to say it out loud. A number said out loud in this room becomes a number this room has agreed to, and I have watched what happens to the ones that do. Ask me again in a year, when the answer is different, and I will give you a different number, and it will be the truest thing anybody in this room has ever said.**"

"**Then the half against all five of those, and it is mine, and I am tired of it,**" said Bel Ordry. "**A room that cannot say how many people are in it cannot say how many people are not in it either. Both of those are the same silence, and only one of them is the one that kills somebody, and the one that kills somebody is the one nobody asks about.**"

Nobody improved on it, and nobody thanked him for it, and nobody has thanked him for any of them in thirty-five weeks.

Tams Quill said nothing at all, and the clerk entered that he said nothing, and entered that she had not asked him, and entered that a man who reads a board being asked what a room holds and saying nothing at all is not a refusal and is not an agreement and she was not going to enter it as either.

"**And I have a number,**" said Marek Venn.

Every head in the room turned and he had not meant it to come out the way it came out, which was too fast and too flat, and he went on with it because there was nothing else to do with it now that it was in the room.

"**I have watched nine people cross this floor twice a morning for a week and I have watched a corridor and a landing and a kitchen-line doorway and a set of nine steps, and I could give you a figure for any of those that would be within two or three of right and I have had it in my head since the second day of last week. I am not going to give it to you.**"

Nella Bray did not take her hands off the trestle. "**Why not,**"

"**Because it comes from me,**" he said, "**and I have no post, and the moment this room has a figure from a man with no post then the figure has a source, and a source is a post, and this boundary has spent thirty-five weeks refusing posts, and I am not going to be handed one by accident because I happened to be the only person standing in the room who could count.**"

He had thought about it for four days, on and off, and the reason he had not said it on Monday was not that he did not have the figure. It was that on Monday it would have sounded like an offer, and he has wanted to make an offer in this room for thirty-five weeks and he has not been able to work out whether wanting to is the same as being useful.

He put his hand flat on the shut book, and it was the hand that does not close, and nobody looked at it and it did not matter that nobody looked at it.

"**And I want it on the record that I had it,**" he went on, "**and that the half against me is not that it is wrong. It is that I have wanted for thirty-five weeks to be the man in this room who is useful, and a number of what is in here is the most useful thing anybody has offered me in six weeks, and I have just turned it down because I know what it turns into.**"

Then Emrys Callow crossed the floor and put one hand flat on his own chest, which is a thing a man does when he is talking about the five people behind him and not about himself, and he did it in the middle of an anteroom on a Wednesday morning with nine children on the other side of a wall.

"**Right,**" he said. "**Then let me say what that costs, because I have been standing here for two days waiting for somebody to make me say it and none of you has, and it is not a matter of trust and it never was.**"

"**I am a shoring foreman. My trade is a wall. What a shoring crew knows about a wall is what is behind it. I have stood in front of nine hundred walls in nine years and every single thing I have ever done came out of one question, and the question is how many people are on the other side of it. That is not sentiment. That is the trade. A wall with a known number behind it gets a certain timber and a certain number of hours and a certain man holding the other end. A wall with an unknown number behind it gets a shore a person can take out in four minutes, and if it goes in four minutes then the four minutes is a hundred times too slow and the person holding the other end is the person who is under it.**"

Mero Senn had been in the doorway through the whole of it and had not once been asked for anything, which is his ordinary condition in this room and which he has complained about twice in a month. "**That is the first time in this building somebody has said a number was about safety,**" he said. "**I have sat in nine rooms in this district listening to people tell me that a figure of occupancy is a matter of paperwork. I have never once heard a working man say it was about a man's back.**"

Callow took his hand off his own chest. "**It is about my five's backs,**" "**and I would rather say it in a room than have somebody else work it out in a year and put it in a district book with a column over it.**"

Then Iven Tallo, from the anvil end, with the chisel still in his hand and his left open and flat on the anvil.

"**Two refusals this morning,**" said Iven Tallo, "**and they are the same refusal in two coats, and I would like a clerk to hear me say so before she writes either of them down. The first was a woman who will not put a number of people into a room's rating. The second is a man who will not put a number of people anywhere. And the woman at the counter did not ask for a number of a room. She asked for a number of a crew, and a crew is not a room, and if anybody in this room has an answer to that it is not the two sentences that have just been said and I would like that entered before I say anything else.**"

Dalla Sen did not pick the chalk up. "**Then nothing enters it,**" "**I am entering that nothing enters it, and I am entering the reason, which is that a clerk who puts a blank under a heading has printed a lid on a hole, and that is the reason a man who reads numbers gave in his twelfth week in this building, and I am not going to beat it.**"

Sefu Aran had been at the head of the nine steps through the whole of it with his hands behind him, and he came down two of them, which is as far as he has come in a week.

"**I am the elected keeper of this boundary,**" said Sefu Aran, "**and I have been here nine days, and I have not decided anything this morning. I would like it entered that the elected keeper of a boundary was in a room for forty minutes while two of its crews worked out whether they can stand on the same landing, and did not open his mouth, and that this is now the second time in three weeks.**"

"**Then nothing is decided by the keeper,**" said Renn Vale, "**and I am going to say what is decided, and it is not much, and it is a refusal.**"

She came down the four steps, all of them, and stood on the anteroom floor, which she does not do for anybody.

"**The two crews do not work the wall together. Not this week,**" said Renn Vale. "**And the reason is not a name on a line and it is not a number. It is that this room has just refused a figure and a foreman has just told us what a figure is for, and I am not going to put those two facts in the same sentence and go and work.**"

"**Then I will enter it,**" said Dalla Sen, and her chalk was up, and she stopped with it up for a moment longer than she usually does.

"**Stop,**" said Bel Ordry, from the step.

"**I know what you are going to say, and you are right,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and I am going to write the word anyway, and I want it entered that I knew. It is *refused*. And it is the same word I would have had to write on Monday about a schedule, and on Saturday I refused to write that word, and this morning I am writing it, and the difference is that a schedule is an office and a man is a man.**"

"**Is it,**" said Bel Ordry, from where he was, and the room waited.

"**No,**" said Dalla Sen. "**And I am going to write the word, and put the reason underneath it, and the reason is that a man is in the room and a schedule is not, and I do not know yet whether that is a reason or a comfortable way of drawing a line under something.**"

The anteroom's terms were said by a person and repeated by the clerk at the fourth bell, and the count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change, and the outer service door was the one a body went out of, and the fifth of those six terms was in a room with both the people it names in it, and the pump was attended.

No sixth piece of work has been asked of anybody this week, and the reason is in the book, and the reason is that a room spent the morning refusing a foreman and a clerk is not going to ask five people to carry a piece of work in the hour she has done that.

At the sixth bell the clerk read the day's figure and then read the total, and the order was the other way round from every morning this week because a room had said a number out loud and been told it did not have one, and she wanted the running figure to be in the air before the total was. Four today and two hundred and sixty-six since the keeper signed. The boundary is at three hundred and eighty-three and a quarter, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen. The count was read at one hundred and twenty-three, and it is not going to be one hundred and twenty-four, and the clerk said out loud that it was a number of days and not a number of anything, which she has said in thirty-two weeks running, and nobody thanked her for it then or now.

The pot is four days and the seam gave one pot's worth on Monday and nothing on Tuesday, and the four households at a second gate have nothing, and no figure is attached to that gate.

The nine went up the nine steps at the fourth bell with the woman who keeps that room ahead of them, and for about two minutes the anteroom was a room with a floor and a pump in it and six packs along a bench and a hole in the far corner of the floor. Then the light came off the flags at the same slant it had come off them at the first bell. The wall was still there, and there were nine people on one side of it, and five and a half shores and a brace on the two sides of it, and nobody in the building knew how many of them were on either side of anything.
