# Chapter 30 — The Second Threshold

The line on the valve board was read out before anything else, at the work-bell, with everyone standing.

*Any future use of that corridor is a use of a hole we have corded off, and it cannot be described as a room until somebody can see down that shaft.*

Nobody argued with it, which was itself remarkable, and what they did instead was spend the rest of the morning finding out what it cost to accept it.

It cost the corridor its name first. For six weeks that stretch of floor had been the shared room in everyone's mouth — the room that took a family, the room that took a stretcher, the room that the crew had built and the Hearthholds had let them build. Nera's line took the word away from it, and the taking was correct, and everybody felt it go.

"Then it is a corridor," Jo said, when the reading was done. "It is a corridor we use while the pump runs. That is what it is called, and if anybody calls it a room again I will take the chalk away."

"It is still a place a person can stand," Mikka said.

"Yes. And a place a person can stand is not a room, and the difference is that a room has a condition that does not change with a bronze seat."

That became the whole of the decision, and it took four hours to write and an entire afternoon to be argued over, and the argument was not about whether the corridor was safe. It was about the words, and the words were the decision.

Sefu read the interim page at the threshold, in the order he always read, and the reading took time because there were a great many names on it.

The anteroom and the service bypass continue as a conditionally open connection for attended maintenance and limited boundary work, for a named work period, with the marked conditions and no deeper admission. The pump continues in short attended intervals, on a seat that is scored, with no spare released, and the interval governs everything else. The lower corridor may be used for nine people including one litter, only while the pump lever is moving, only with all six watches filled, with the lever announced, and with the kitchen line as the way out. The school door is a relief and not an escape. The upper gallery is closed, barricaded at the ninth tread, and is not a route, a room, an air space, or an occupancy. The returning stair is restricted, corded, and recorded, and nothing in this decision touches it. The inspection incline is locked under a guild order that did not open anything and has not been appealed. The wrapped plate on the bench is evidence, and this decision says nothing about it.

And then, at the bottom, the sentence that made it an interim page and not a charter: *This is a decision among the people named on it, for the people named on it, until the next review. It creates no public route, no ownership, no charter, and no obligation on any person who did not agree to it.*

Sefu put the pen down. "That is the boundary part. It is mine and it is the only part of this page that is simply mine, and I would like it read first next time, because everything else on it can be argued."

He marked it and read on.

Renn took the sixth watch on the days she could stand it, and named the days she could not before anyone asked her to name anything. Four days out of seven. On the other three the corridor does not open, and everybody in the room understood what that meant, and Mikka said it out loud so it could not be softened later.

"It means on those three days, when Jo is on somebody's shutter, there is nowhere to put a household," she said. "The kitchen line is a walkway. It is not a waiting room and I will not make it one while there is a pot on."

"Then the school corner takes it," Ilsa said, and Jo looked at her, and Ilsa did not look away. "On those three days the school corner is a waiting room. I have nine children and a wall, and the wall is on the kitchen side, and my children are not in the room while it is being used. That is the price and I have decided to pay it, and I want it in the record that I decided it and not that it was arranged for me."

"Recorded," Dalla said, and wrote it down in Ilsa's words, which she had learned to do.

Jo took the corridor watch and the pump cleaning and a shutter route, and put all three on one line on the crew board, and read the line back to make sure the room could see it. Mikka kept the kitchen provision and did not waive it. Renn's hours went in at a number. Dalla's hours went in at a number, and her own line said *record-keeping, 31 hours, and this is a cost, not a duty,* and she read that out too.

Iven's line was the hardest, because the guild could use it.

"I have given twenty-nine hours of instruction that the Hearthholds are not going to pay me for," he said, "because if they pay me it becomes guild work done under a suspended bond, and Dael's office will use it. I am not a defector. I said in the anteroom that I was refusing to let the guild put the repair under its custody, and I meant it, and it cost the same either way. I want that written exactly like that, so that in a hearing nobody can say I was taken on by the neighborhood."

"And when the guild asks who keeps the pump sequence when you are not here?" Dalla asked.

"Then the answer is a person in a ledger, and it is not me, and that is the point of the last two months." He looked at Jo. "You can clean a check and you can read a housing. You do not need me for that. If you ever do need me, that is a day of somebody's route, and it will be written down as one."

Leena read the litter condition and the exit plan, and then she said the thing that had been sitting in her since the first test.

"Two and a half feet of door and four bearers and a fouled frame," she said. "There is a number in this document and it is not nine. It is how long it takes four people to get me out of a room that has been approved, on a wet floor, while a pump is running and a bell is going. Call it four minutes. Put the four minutes in, because the next person who reads this is going to count the people and not the minutes, and the minutes are the room."

Marek wrote it with his left hand, the letters uneven, and read it back twice, and did not improve the sentence.

Nera's part was last and it was the shortest. She read her own line, which said that the air of the corridor was the pump's air while the pump ran and the stair's air the moment it stopped, and that she would not certify anything about the shaft, and that her stop authority over the air route was unchanged and was not a permission from anybody.

"Add one thing," she said. "I am not the person who has to fix the shaft. There is no one in this building who is. Whoever fixes it will have to come from somewhere else, and when they do, they will not be repairing a room. They will be repairing a mechanism that belongs to a stair that is corded. So the honest position of the corridor is this: it is a good use of a floor while a machine lasts, and the machine is the reason it is a good use, and the machine is going to fail."

"Then what do we tell people?" Sefu asked.

"The truth, which is that it is better than the corridor we had, and that it is on a machine, and that when the machine goes we will be back to arguing."

And then they did the thing that was not on the page, which was the change.

Marek had been holding the board. He had been holding it since the anteroom test, since the six of them were temporary maintainers with a sheet and a name column, and the habit had become so ordinary that nobody had noticed it going into ownership by inches. The board was the record of what the work was, and the record of the work was the record of who could be asked to do it, and the man holding it was the man who had proposed all of it.

He put it down on the bench in front of Jo.

"Any change to a room that people use gets written by the person who will keep it," he said. "Not by me. If I see a failure, I say so, out loud, and then whoever keeps the thing writes the correction and I do not get to write it first. That is the rule. I am not proposing it. I am asking whether anyone objects to it, and if nobody objects, Jo holds the board from today."

Jo looked at the board for a while. "You understand what that means."

"It means I will be told I am wrong in a hand I do not control, in front of the people who live here, and I will not be able to fix the wording."

"Yes."

"Good."

She picked it up. She did not soften anything on it, and she did not add anything to it, and that was the correct behavior and he disliked it enormously.

Dalla read the unresolved list one more time, because the list was the true part of the page, and it was still long: the bronze delivery seat, which no one would release; the timber for the school-door lintel, which no one in the building had; the carpenter's wage for a man a guild would not pay; nineteen days of meals, given and not waived; a sealed liter of verified condensation in a stoppered jar; a suspended bond with a name on it; a form unsigned in a Marrowgate office that named who answered for a tunnel collapse, to which six people had now added a floor of a school; and the only physically continuous way down, restricted, corded, and not openable by anyone in this building.

"The next review," Dalla said, and read the line she had written, which was longer than the previous one because the situation was longer: *the third work-bell after the next complete pressure turn, or immediately if the pump stops, if the air cloth moves, if the water reaches the sill, if any watch is empty, if the stair cord moves, or if anybody proposes reopening the gallery.*

"Add one more," Iven said. "If the guild's door opens."

Nobody argued with that either. Dalla added it, and read the whole page back, and nobody marked it as agreed until every person whose name was on it had read the line their name was under, which is what they had learned to do in a room where a sentence about a route is more expensive than a sentence about a wall.

The afternoon was ordinary, and the ordinariness was the point.

Mikka's queue came through the kitchen line and past a chalk figure on the floor that told each person their own width, and every single one of them looked down at it, and one child asked why there was a number written where people walk, and Ilsa told him, and he said it was stupid, and Ilsa said that it was, and that it was also going to be there on Thursday.

The pump came on at the fourth bell. Tams took the lever, Oren took the frame, Jo was at the grate with the wrench within reach, and Pell called the water from the bench with his slate on his knees, and the check knocked twice and settled. Nera watched the cloth. Renn was on the landing. Sefu was at the wheel. Dalla was at the threshold with the bell rope, and the board was on the bench beside her, and it was not in her hand.

The corridor filled to nine while the pump ran, and emptied when the lever came off, and the sun was not doing anything dramatic to the light; it was a wet afternoon in a stone building and the light came in where it always came in.

Above them, on a stair nobody was climbing, a barricade plank at the ninth tread held a slate with four dates and a word, and behind it a school floor went on holding the shape of forty years without anybody in it.

On the anteroom bench, under a spread of work cloth, the wrapped plate lay where it had lain for six weeks with its empty seat in the middle and its lines going out to supports nobody had identified, and it had not said one word about any of it.

Marek Venn picked up the ninth wedge from the tally board, where Tams had left it, and turned it in his left hand, and did not go and get a board.
