# Chapter 176 — What The Map Claims

The fourth day of the thirty-fourth week is a Thursday, and a shut Thursday is nine steps and one pump and a building with the sound of water finding its level in it, and the anteroom is the only room anybody is in twice in the same hour. The school did not run and lost nothing. Marek Venn had his book open on his forearm and had not put a line in it since the second bell of Tuesday, and the reason was that he had been reading a printed leaf off a trestle from the corners inward, the way he goes at a wall, looking for the place where the shape is convenient. A lie is a thing he knows what to do with.

There was no lie in it. He gave it the middle of the morning and it did not give him one. The figures were certified. The certification was somebody's job and the somebody was named on the bottom line in a clerk's own hand, Aubrey Tace, engager of clearances, ninth district, and the seal number under it was a number and not a word. Every room on it existed. Every figure on it was a figure somebody had been paid to certify, and the certification was correct, and it was correct about a permission.

The pump got four strokes in at the first bell while a road hand stood at the bottom of the nine steps with a single leaf of printed paper in his fist, and he held it out at arm's length and would not come up for it.

"**I have carried this down from the gate and not from the middle passage, and I am going to say why before anybody touches it,**" he said. "**It came off a board at the gate with nothing else on it, and the man who put it in my hand said it was for this Hearthhold and that it was not urgent, and he said that last part twice, and a man who says a thing is not urgent twice is telling you he has been told it is not.**"

Dalla Sen took it on the sill, square to the trestle, and turned it so that everybody at the table could get at it. She did not read it, which is not what she does.

"**There is a covering on it?**" she said.

"**There is no covering. It is one leaf, printed both sides, and it has a wax head on the top corner and a wax head on the bottom, and the wax is the insurance office's and not the Cartwrights', and I know the Cartwright wax because I have carried a hundred and forty of them.**"

"**Nobody touches it until I have said what it says on the top of it. Read it back to me, all of it, including the small print at the bottom, because a document is not what its title says and a room that reads a title out loud is a room that has been had.**"

The road hand read it, slowly, the way a man reads a thing he has been asked to read twice.

"**Occupied rooms, the ninth district of the cleared-risk office. Compiled from the clearance records held in this office. No surveyor of this office has entered any room shown. Every figure is the last certified permitted occupancy of that room. This office certifies permissions. This office does not count.**"

Nobody said anything for about three strokes of the pump. Marek Venn found that he had stopped looking for the lie somewhere in the middle of that silence and had started listening to the second line instead, and he did not like what he had found himself doing.

"**Again,**" said Bel Ordry, from the step. "**The last line.**"

"**This office certifies permissions. This office does not count.**"

"**Then that sheet has told us what it is in its own hand, on its own paper, and it is going to cost somebody a job to have printed that,**" Bel Ordry said. "**And there is a word at the top of it under the wax that I want read as well.**"

"**Independent,**" said the road hand.

Dalla Sen put the six rows of the district onto a slate in her own hand, one to six, with the room names as the sheet gives them and not as this building calls anything, and then made Jo Verrick read the slate back against the leaf without looking at the leaf. Jo Verrick read it. It was the same, and the clerk entered that it was the same, and entered that she had read it out and had not improved on it and had asked for the check anyway.

"**Entered, and entered that I have now got a district of six rooms on a slate in a building that has a page of sixteen rows in a coat and the two are not going in the same book,**" Dalla Sen said. "**And entered that two of the six on that sheet are rooms in this Hearthhold, by the name the sheet uses, and that a name on somebody else's paper is not a row on ours, and that I have not put a row in and am not going to.**"

Renn Vale came down the fourth step and put two fingers on the slate.

"**The two figures,**" she said. "**In the order they are in, and not in an order that makes them better.**"

"**The anteroom, ten. The room at the kitchen-line end, ten.**" The clerk put the chalk down. "**And I am entering that those two figures are the figures in the first column of a page in a coat, for the same two rooms, and that they are the same figures, and that I am entering that I have not joined them to anything else and am not going to, and that a clerk who puts two agreements next to each other in a book is a clerk making a document.**"

Then she stopped, and looked along the table until she found the book on the forearm, and did not say anything for long enough that the pump got a stroke in.

"**You have been at that leaf for an hour and a half with your book open and no line in it,**" she said. "**This room has learned that a blank page is a thing you are thinking about and not a thing you have forgotten. So we are waiting.**"

Marek Venn shut the book.

"**I have been looking for the lie.**" He put it on the table instead of back on his forearm, which he had not done in a room in a fortnight. "**And I am going to say the rest of it before somebody hands it to me, because I have been in this building thirty-four weeks and I have got very good at wanting to be the man who catches a thing, and that wanting is the reason I have had a bad three days and not the leaf.**"

Bel Ordry had been standing very still on his step, and he came down one, which is more than he has done in this room for a month.

"**You have answered the wanting,**" he said. "**You have not answered the leaf. Take it from the top.**"

"**The leaf is a map of the rooms in a district this boundary sits inside of, drawn by an office that has never walked into one of them, out of records of what each room was allowed to hold.**" He turned the leaf so the legend faced the ceiling of the room's light. "**And the falsity is not in the figures. Every figure is true. The falsity is in the noun. Somebody certified a permission thirty times over thirty years and then printed a map of the rooms, and a room that was permitted eight and held eight and a room that was permitted ten and held ten are the same room on that sheet, and neither of them is a room anybody has counted.**"

"**The word,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**Independent.**"

"**Of what,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**That is the question and it took me an hour and a half, and the answer is in the sheet's own fourth line.**" Marek Venn did not look away from the wax at the top corner. "**No surveyor of this office has entered any room shown. That is the independence. The sheet is independent of the rooms. It is not independent of the office that keeps the records, and it does not claim to be, and a reader who hears *independent* and puts the office's records behind it and the rooms in front of it has been had by a word and not by a figure.**"

"**Then it is not false,**" said Renn Vale.

"**It is not false and it is not true, and that is worse, and I would like a room to understand that those are three states and not two.**"

The clerk took up the chalk, and made a line, and then read a line back out before she entered it, which she does. What she entered was this: the word falsified, against her own professional instinct as a clerk, entered by a woman who has been this boundary's clerk since the nineteenth week and has not had to do that before. Then, in the space where a reason goes, and in her own hand, the objection to the entry itself. *Falsified* is a word for a person. There is no person on that sheet to be a word about. The man who engraved the wax and the clerk who certified the figures and the printer in a town she has never been to would all read that entry and be right to be insulted by it. And a sheet which says out loud that it certifies permissions and does not count is a sheet that has been honest, and the honest sentence is the one that does the damage.

Mero Senn said it from the doorway, where he had been standing with his bag against the wall since the first bell.

"**The half against your own entry. You know what it is and so do I.**"

"**And I am not going to soften it because I do not like where it came from,**" she said. "**Enter that at the end of it.**"

Bel Ordry put his hand flat on the table. It was not on the leaf and he was careful about where it went.

"**I have got a question and it is not fair,**" he said, "**and I am going to say that about my own question first, because I have learned it from this room and I am not going to be the last one here. I would like it put to a room and not to a person.**"

Nobody answered him. The pump got two strokes in.

Renn Vale waited until the second one was finished.

"**Why you are not going to answer it today,**" she said. "**And do not let it be a virtue.**"

"**A man who keeps a page in a coat has spent thirty-four weeks saying out loud that a page in a coat is not a system, and that he is the only person who has to say it,**" said Bel Ordry. He took his hand off the table. "**There are forty-one people on a market road and about the same again under this roof, and a man in an office forty days on from the top of the middle passage has just printed a page about the district they are all in, and it is better than ours, because it is printed, and it goes everywhere, and it has a wax head on it. I want to know, out loud, in a room, whether a page in a coat with no name on it is better than that. And I want it answered by somebody who is not me.**"

"**Because the honest answer is that I do not know, and I have had a question in a clerk's book since the twenty-eighth week and it has not been answered by anybody, and I am not going to get answered by a leaf.**" He put the hand back in his pocket, where it had lived for thirty-four weeks. "**And I am also aware that I asked a room to prefer our page, and our page has a first column that has never counted anybody in thirty-four weeks, and so the man who asked is the man with the worst page in the district and he asked it because he was frightened.**"

The clerk entered that, in his words, and entered that the reason had a clause in it that was about him, and entered that she was not going to put that in a column. A question asked in a room can be answered in a room, and a question asked in a book is a question a person finds out about in a year.

Then the man with the book shut on the table asked for the room, and the room gave him the objection first, because the objection was on the step and nobody had to look for it.

"**Put it to them,**" said Bel Ordry. "**And do not soften it into a question. I have watched you do that twice this week.**"

"**It is a hole.**" Marek Venn turned the book round and did not open it. "**A second way out of the anteroom. It goes low through the party wall, into the void between this anteroom and the room at the kitchen-line end, and up through the floor of that room. The object of it is to turn a word into a place.**"

"**Why a man's week is worth spending on that,**" said Renn Vale.

"**Because a woman on this landing said in a room in the second week that a word is not a way out, and a carpenter said in a room in the fourth week that a person does not go out through a word, and neither of them has been answered. I have decided to stop asking the question and build the answer.**"

"**And who is standing under it this morning,**" said Renn Vale.

"**Nobody, because I do not know what is in the void, and because a hole is not a route, and because a hole into a place nobody has a row for is a hole into nothing.**" He put two fingers on the table. "**And there is a third reason and it is the real one. Our first column is a permission. A room that has never been surveyed cannot have a row. A room with no row has no keeper and no condition. And a hole from a room with a condition into a room with nothing in it is not a second way out. It is a place to fall into.**"

"**What you need,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**A survey. Somebody competent has to stand in that void with a lamp and come back and tell this room what is in it, and then a room has to agree whether it is a room, and then a keeper has to write a condition on it, and then I can cut a hole into it with a person standing on the other side who has agreed to be standing there.**"

Iven Tallo had been at the anvil end for two hours not carving anything, and he turned the chisel over and did not use it.

"**In a sentence, then, and make it a true one,**" he said. "**Four people, three days, and a lamp you do not have.**"

"**Four people, three days, and a lamp I do not have.**"

"**And the next sentence, which is the one that matters, and I am going to say it because I am the man in this room who builds things and you are the man who draws them.**" The carpenter put the chisel down. "**A survey needs somebody to be allowed in. And the sheet on your table says in its own hand that no surveyor of that office has been in one of those rooms, and it is printed, and it is waxed, and it is a district, and your void is in that district. The office has not refused you. The office has never once been asked. It has simply printed a page saying it is not in there, and a page like that is harder to argue with than a door, because nobody has to stand behind it.**"

Nobody improved on that either, and Ilsa Norr came to the kitchen-line doorway and said the thing she says on shut days, which is that the room at the kitchen-line end has nine hearth stones in it and that the fire has now not been lit for eleven weeks, and nobody asked her about the fire and nobody asked her about anything else, and she went back.

At the sixth bell the clerk put the day's figure and the total into the book and did not read either of them out, and the count was read at one hundred and eleven. Two today and two hundred and thirty-four since the keeper signed, the boundary at three hundred and fifty-one and a quarter, adding by the day the work was done, both columns reconciling, the counter carried at seventeen. She wrote down the reason for not reading them too, which is that a sheet with a district on it has been open on that table since the first bell with a word at the top of it in a font larger than the rest, and a clerk does not stand up and read a total over a document that is making a claim about a room.

The crew are at seventy-one and have not moved since the second day of the thirty-second week, and the sixth asking has not been made, and the reason is that a road hand walked in with a piece of paper, and that is a thing that arrives and not a thing anybody can be asked five people for.

The pot is nine days. The seam gave nothing this week. And the count. Wen Pask is one hundred and eleven days and one hundred and eleven nights into a count that is his own, and it is not going to be one hundred and twelve, and she has said in twenty weeks running that she is going to keep writing it.

And she did not walk the board, and the reason is that she has put a word in that book this morning that she did not expect to put, and a board does not get walked in the same ten minutes as a word like that. It will get walked tomorrow and it will be eighteen.

Twenty-five weeks is how long the coat on the hook has been carrying a brass survey rule that has said nothing, and the plate above the same bench has now been wrapped for thirty-four. Neither was in a room and neither moved.
