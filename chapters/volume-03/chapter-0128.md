# Chapter 128 — A Row In The First Column

**Open for the corridor on the third day of the twenty-fifth week, a Wednesday, and the school went back into the room at the kitchen-line end for the fourteenth morning. A physician stood in the corridor with his bag at his feet and did not go in. A woman with a rule stood in the doorway with a lamp and did not go in either. A cook stood at the kitchen line and watched nine children walk past her into a room whose sill is packed in clay and whose ninth hearth stone is propped two flags off its bed on a strip of crate board, and who would not be taught in by anybody this morning and would be taught in by the boundary in the afternoon.**

**The argument about going back in was had in a corridor at the second bell and it took eleven minutes, and the woman who argued hardest for not going back in lost it and lost it in her own words.**

"**A bed of that stone is a hole the width of my hand and there are nine of them in there in a cold morning and the light in that room is off the stones,**" said Jo Verrick, from the doorway, with the lamp down. "**I am not going to be the last thing that was said about that bed before it went.**

"**You are going to be the second thing,**" said Mero Senn, from the corridor, "**and I am going to be the first, and I said it in this corridor yesterday and I said it in six sentences and a clerk wrote all six down, and I am not going to say it again to save a room, and if I say it again I am using it as a thing I own and I do not own it.**

"**Then say the thing that is not yours,**" said Jo Verrick.

**What is not mine is that a hole under a stone is not a hole under a stair. This building has a slab over eleven feet that is packed and not held, and the woman who packs it has said in a room that the next time that slab turns somebody will be under it and it will be her. A stone that has gone over a hand's width in a fortnight, in a room where nine children sit on it in a cold morning, is not that. It is a stone, and it is propped, and the fire in that corner is not lit, and the light is bad.**

"**I am a physician and not a floor. I have said my piece and I am going to go and look at a foot.**"

"**Then say one more thing and go,**" said Jo Verrick. "**Say what I have to do every morning for the rest of this term, so that I have not got to be asked.**"

"**Put your hand on that bed at the first bell and say out loud in a room whether the strip is still holding, whether the answer is yes or no, and whether or not anybody is going to be in the room. And if the answer is no twice, take the nine of them out, and do not wait for me or for anybody in this building.

"**And if you ever find yourself doing that every morning without resenting it, tell somebody, because that is the day you have stopped being able to see it.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**in the physician's words and read back twice, and entered that it is the second thing he has put on a page in this building, the first having been on the Tuesday, and that both were put on at the request of other people and neither was put on to be agreed with, and entered that the woman with the rule did not thank him for it and I do not know whether she heard it.**"

Ilsa Norr went in at the fourth bell and taught one bell, and came out, and said the sentence that closed the argument in her own words and not the room's.

"**I have taught in a room with a wet floor and a room with no shelf and a room nine children had to cross a fill to reach. I am going to teach in this one. And I am going to say the thing I said in the third week about a dry room that was held up by something a woman could take off a wall, except that it is not the same and I am not going to be allowed to pretend it is: the difference is that a person cannot take a packed sill off a wall. It is not grease. It is stone in clay, and clay shrinks, and the two people who laid it have to be in a room together to say it is done.

"**And that is the whole of what I have. I would like somebody to write down that a schoolteacher's standard has come down to *somebody can tell me when it is finished* and that I am not complaining about it, because I have taught in a worse room than that in my life and did not have anybody to ask.**"

**And Marek Venn, who had laid half the stone in that sill, put his hand in the joint at the first bell that morning, before anybody was in the room, said one number out loud in a corridor, and then wrote it in a book of his own, and did not say it to a room.**

"**One millimetre less than a nail.**" He said it to Jo Verrick at the door, and she had a thumb in the same joint inside a minute and said it was not as much as she had, and he said it was not as much as he had either, and neither of them said what that meant.

"**Say the other half,**" said Jo Verrick.

"**The other half is that in a week it will be nothing, and I have no idea what happens after that, and I said in a room on Thursday that I am not going to hand a stranger a figure, and I have just handed you one to a millimetre, and I am writing it in my own book and not on a page. A man who will not put a figure on a condition is perfectly capable of writing one in a private book at a first bell, and the two things are not in contradiction, and that is the trouble with me.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that he gave it to a woman and not to a room, and that she checked it and disagreed with it, and that neither of them told anybody else before the fifth bell, and that I found it out at the fourth because I am the person who looks in books. And I am not going to enter that he is right and I am not going to enter that he is careful. I am going to enter that a man in this boundary now has a number in a private book and no condition anywhere that it answers to.**"

**And then the boundary wrote a row on its own plan, and there had never been one in twenty-five weeks, and the reason it was written on a Wednesday afternoon is a child on a step and a word that opened nothing.**

Bel Ordry brought the plan up in his coat at the fifth bell, in four parts, and did not put it down on the table, and said the thing first.

"**Say the reason out loud before you open it, because this boundary has spent twenty-five weeks putting reasons before documents and I am not going to stop in the twenty-sixth.**

"**The reason is this. A room was closed on Friday with eleven people in it and a condition on it, and a man said the second way out of that room is a word, and eleven people said the word in a corridor, and the word opened nothing. And on Monday morning the door was opened on a child's foot and a woman with a slate lifted a condition she wrote, and nobody in this room can point at one object that says what that room is allowed to hold.

"**So I am going to say the part I have been not saying since the first week of the twentieth week. There is a room in this building with nine children in it three days in seven and a packed sill in it and a child in it with a broken foot, and it has no row. It is not on the plan and it is not in the charter and it is not on my slate, and a boundary that keeps a plan and does not put a room on it is a boundary that has decided it does not have to know.**

"**And I want it known that I am aware what I am proposing. Every day of the last six weeks I have said that a document about a room travels, and a page is a thing a stranger can be given, and a name on a route is a name forty days away. And I am asking this room to write a row about a room with a child in it.**"

"**The other reason is worse and I am giving it to you anyway. A room with no row is a room nobody has promised anything to, and I have known that since the fifth day of the twenty-first week when our clerk said it out loud in this room, and I did not do anything about it for six weeks because it suited us, and it does not suit us any more, and I am not going to be a woman who waited for a bad week to arrive and then said she had no choice.**"

**It was written at the sixth bell on the back of a day-board, in the cook's hand, and it is the plan's second hand, and it is the only hand that has ever added a row to it, and the number in the first column is a ten, and nobody in the room had counted anybody.**

Mikka Hale wrote it. Dalla Sen read it out and then read it back against the board, and the cook said yes to the reading, and the clerk then said the part that has to be said out loud about a number that has just been written into the first column of a public maintenance plan.

"**Say the number out loud and say where it came from,**" said Dalla Sen, "**because the fourth column of this page carries a reason about not putting a promise on it, and this page has never had a new number put in its first column by anybody, and I am not going to have one go in without the room hearing where it came from.**

"**The number is a ten. It is in the cook's hand in the first column, and it is a permitted occupancy and not a count of people, and it is the same figure as the anteroom's and it is not the same number, and the cook did not count anybody to get it, and she is going to say so in a room.**"

"**I copied the rule,**" said Mikka Hale. "**Ten is what this page says a room like that is allowed to hold, and it is what the anteroom's is, and I did not look in a room and I did not ask a child and I did not use the number that was read out of a corridor on Friday, which was eleven, and I want that entered as a thing I did on purpose and not as a thing I did not notice.

"**And I want the whole of the reason entered and I have not said it out loud yet. I did not put eleven in that column because a permitted occupancy is not a count of people, and if I put eleven in it then anybody in a year who reads this page is going to think eleven is a number of children, and it is a number of bodies, and a body is not a child. A room that says it holds eleven bodies has been counted. A room that says it holds ten has been permitted. I am not going to be the woman who put the second kind of number on a public page in this Hearthhold.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**in the cook's words, and entered that she did it on purpose, and entered that the number in the first column of that row is a permitted occupancy and not a count of people, and that a boy of fourteen is going to read that column out loud in a room in about a minute and that he does not know any of this and that he is not going to be told any of it by me beforehand.**"

"**Say it,**" said Bel Ordry, "**because you wrote it a fortnight ago on a separate page and it is a different line and I want the room to hear both.**

"**The room at the kitchen-line end ran and was closed with the room in use and was opened on a child's foot, and the second way out of that room is a word, and the word is in a clerk's book and not on this page, and the sill is packed in stone in clay by two people and is opened when those two people say in a room together that the clay has stopped, and I am not going to be blamed for closing it and I am not going to be forgiven for the stone.**"

**And then a boy of fourteen read the first column out loud in a room, because that is what he is for. He had the kitchen part of the plan in his hands at the top of the nine steps with his crutch across his knees and his ear against the stone, and he did not go down the steps and he did not go into the corridor and he did not go over the waist, and nobody asked him to.**

"**First column.**

"**Ten. Four. Eight. Ten. Six. Nine. Five. Two. And a dash. And ten.**"

The room did not fill in after it.

"**Say the last one again,**" said Dalla Sen, and she said it in the flat voice and she said it once and then a second time herself, out loud, because that is what the clerk does.

"**Ten,**" said Pell Roke. "**It is ten. It is a new one and it is a ten and I do not know anything about it and I am not going to say I do not know anything about it because I read numbers and that is the end of what I do.**"

"**Then read the second column,**" said Dalla Sen.

He read the ten conditions with no number and no day of the week on any of them. The fourth was the word *wet*. The ninth was new and had no number and no day on it. The tenth was a dash.

"**Third column.**

"**Nine lines filled and one empty,**" he said, "**and I am not going to say which one is empty because the man who keeps it has said in a room that he is not going to say, and I read numbers and that is the end of what I do.**"

"**Fourth column.**"

He looked at it. He had it in his hand the right way up, and he had read that column in a room three weeks ago and had said four words about it that are on a page in a clerk's hand.

"**There is nothing in it.**"

"**Read the reason,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and read it out of the plan and not off your own memory, and the reason is in two hands, and I want the strike read as well.**

"**The fourth column is empty, nothing has been put in it, it has no heading, this is true as at the day in the top corner, the keeper is permitted to put something in it, and the person who does it has to be in the room.**"

He read the strike too — the modal in Renn Vale's hand, struck out, and Nera Oduya's replacement in her own hand — and Dalla Sen said the thing she had come to say, which was not a fact.

Dalla Sen said the thing she had come to say, which was not a fact.

"**I am going to enter a decision on a page tonight and I am entering it because a room in a book is allowed to look like an accident and this one is not going to.**

"**A condition in this building is checked by being read out again, word for word, against the original, by a second person, in a room, and compared. That has happened in this building four times in three weeks: a mark's condition when it was written and when it was met, a floor's condition when it was met, a room's condition when it was written and when it was lifted, and a column's reason just now.**

"**That is a decision and not a fact and it is in the book in my hand and the room heard it.**"

**And the board was walked, and a fourteenth thing went on it, and it was the first thing on that board in twenty-five weeks that was not about a wall.**

Tams Quill had the chalk in his left hand and his right one open and flat in his lap, and he read both columns, and named no day, and gave no reason for not naming one.

"**The crew are at fifty-six and a half and have not moved since Thursday. The boundary is at two hundred and eighteen and a quarter — four today, a hundred and one since the keeper signed — and it adds by the day the work was done, and both columns reconcile, and they have reconciled for twelve weeks. A man of twenty-two is sixty-three days and sixty-three nights into a count that is his own, and he carried a lamp in here at the first bell and put it down and went away without being told, and I noticed, and I am not going to say anything else about it.**"

Then he got the chalk to the side with nothing over the top of it, and stopped with it a finger off the wood, and Dalla Sen said **Go on**, and he did not for about four seconds.

"**Fourteen entries. Eleven nulls, one claim, one mark, and a row. Nine in the ordinary column and five in the one with nothing over the top of it, and the row is in with nothing over the top of it and not in the ordinary column and I am going to say why and then I am going to say the other thing and the other thing is worse.

"**The row goes in with nothing over the top of it because a row is not a wall. Eleven of the entries on this board are things a man went and put his hand on. The twelfth is a floor. The thirteenth is a mark. The fourteenth is a line in a column of a page that says a room may hold ten bodies, and there are nine children in that room, and there is no version of this board that I can think of where a number somebody wrote down about a room belongs in the ordinary column next to a man's hand.**"

"**Six batches. I have refused six batches running to head this column, and on the Thursday of last week I told this room I had three reasons and had not chosen between them, and on Monday I gave a fourth without choosing, and today I have got five, and I have chosen.**"

"**I am not going to head a column that has a room in it. A heading is a claim about what a column is for, and for twenty-five weeks what this column has been for is things a person brought in and nobody could file. A leg. A bundle. A man's count. A hinge. A wage. A line somebody refused. A mark. And now a row with a number in it about a room nine children are in.

"**If I put a heading on that, then the row is under the heading, and the row says a number, and the number is on a page, and a page is a thing a stranger can be given — and Bel Ordry stood in this room an hour ago and said he was asking us to write a row anyway, in the open, with the reason out loud, and he was right to ask.

"**So the column has a room in it and I am not going to head it. A heading would put a room under a word. And a word is portable, and this building has spent six weeks learning that words travel and rooms do not.

"**There. That is the fifth reason and it is the one I have chosen and I am going to give it once and not again this month, and the next time somebody in this room asks me, I am going to say that I gave it on the third day of the twenty-fifth week and that I am not going to improve on it, and if that is not good enough then the day to be frightened is the day I have nothing at all.**"

Frightened, nobody was. And a cook asked him one question, and he answered it.

"**Then what happens to a wage in a column that has a room in it,**" said Mikka Hale.

"**Nothing,**" said Tams Quill. "**Nothing happens to it. That is what a column with no heading is for, and it is why I have not headed it, and it is why the reason I am giving you today is better than the four before it, and it is also why it took me six batches and a room.**"

"**A column with a heading on it that holds a room is a column a man at a counter can point at. A column with nothing over the top of it that holds a room is a column nobody can ask for, including us, and that is the only protection a wage in a bad month has ever had in this Hearthhold, and it came out of a hearth stone.**"

He put the chalk down against the board then, which he does at the end, and Dalla Sen read the fourteen entries out and the clerk's own reading of them back, and the corridor was open behind her and the school had run a bell at the far end of it in the worst room in this building, and the room was on the page.
