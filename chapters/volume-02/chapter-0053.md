# Chapter 53 — The Draw

The well was opened on the second interval of the third morning and Jo Verrick went down it with an eight-foot cord round her middle and a lamp in her right hand, and the cord came off Nera Oduya's board in front of everybody, and Nera released it herself and wrote the release and the reason, which was that the bay's working depth from the ladder foot was six feet, and there is one ladder in that well and it is a person wide, and there are eleven feet under it.

That was the fifth time that cord had come off that board. Dalla Sen wrote the fifth mark beside the other four and read the row back, and Nera made her say the row twice.

The anteroom held what it held. The outer service door stood open a shoulder's width, the stair-side grille was shut, the bypass hatch was latched and stayed latched, and the pump was attended. Six watches were filled and named. At the top of the nine steps, on the bench at the foot of the stair head, Pell Roke sat with his crutch across his knees and his ear against the stone of the step above the stair head, because the pump was running and the wheel was turning and a person with a good ear in a room with a wheel in it is not a decoration.

At the water mark, Mikka Hale with the lamp, calling the notch out loud every half hour to a person and not to a board, because Ruk Sella was at the bell and a person doing two things is doing neither, and Mikka had said yes and had asked for it to be written down that she had said yes, which Dalla Sen did, in the clerk's own hand, with the time on it.

The gallery had four hours of standing air. Nobody had put that figure on a card in a fortnight, and everybody in the room knew it, and the difference between knowing it and writing it was the entire difference between this work and a suicide, and Nera had said so at the second bell in nine words and had then gone back to her valve without softening any of them.

---

It took her about two minutes to get to the water.

The bay's lip is four feet under the well's lip, and the ladder is green, and the frayed nine-metre line is tied round its stile four times where a dead man's rope has been standing in six weeks of water and has been there seven weeks, and it did not move when she put her weight on the second rung, and it would not have moved for a person either, and the two of them were in the anteroom's eyes all the way down.

Then the shelf.

She was down about three minutes when she called up, and the cord carried it, and Nera held the string and let it out and did not say anything, because the plan said the person reports and the person reports what she sees and does not narrate.

"The shelf is there," Jo said. "The mark is there. The number is on it. It is under about two hands of water and the timber is not under it, it is beside it, it is sitting on the shelf's own bearers, which is the correct place for a length to be stored and whoever stored it knew what they were doing."

"And the rest of the room," said Nera, "which is not on the card, because I have never been in that room, and you are, and I am asking you as an air engineer and not as a plan."

That was the sentence that made the morning into what it became, and Jo answered it, and the cord carried the answer up, and Dalla Sen wrote it down in the ledger at the third bell with the time on it, and Jo Verrick made the clerk say it back before she came up and did not soften one word of it.

"The floor is tiled," Jo said. "Under the silt, at the back, you can see two rows of it. Small ones, about a hand across, in a pattern, and there is more of the pattern under the silt than there is bare, and silt does not lay itself in a pattern.

"And the wall behind the shelf is not a wall. It is a wall with holes in it. Three rows of them, regular, a hand apart, going the whole length of the wall and up above where any shelf has ever been, and the holes are a size you could put a hook in. Somebody hung things off that wall. Not tools — the spacing is wrong for tools. Cords. Coats. Aprons. Pans. There were people living in the room I came out of, and they hung their things up on the wall behind the shelf, and the shelf is in the way of the holes, and somebody bolted a shelf over a wall of pegs because they needed the shelf more than they needed the pegs."

Nobody in the anteroom said anything for a moment.

"Jo," said Ilsa Norr.

"I have said it."

"I have heard it. I want it in the words of the person who saw it and not in mine, and I want it read at the next bell, and I want it on the page next to the work, and I do not want it improved by anybody, including me, and I would like it noted—" she stopped, and corrected herself out loud, in front of the room, the way she had corrected herself out loud twice in nine weeks. "I would like it written. In your words. Because a clerk's line is not the same as a witness and I am a teacher and I have watched a witness be turned into a clerk's line in a school."

"It is in Jo's words or it is not in," Dalla said, and wrote it, and made herself say it twice.

---

The check went flat at the second interval and nobody expected it, and Jo Verrick came up out of the well at a minute and forty seconds with nothing in her hands, which was the plan and which Tams Quill read into the tally that night without anybody having asked him to.

The check had been chattering since the twenty-ninth day, and in the sixth week it had found a note, a thin one, underneath the chatter, and Jo had said at the time that it was the sound of a thing that had a spring in it. What she heard at the second interval was not that.

"It has stopped chattering," she said, standing at the well in a wet apron with her hair down, "and it is hissing. It is hissing flat, and it is hissing all the way, and that is not the check packing. That is the check being *gone*. That is a check that has no face left to chatter with, and the delivery is not stopping because of a seat any more, it is stopping because a thing in there has finished doing its job and is now only doing its other job, which is noise."

"Out," said Nera Oduya, and the word went up the string, and Ruk Sella rang the bell at the threshold and then went up the nine steps and stood at the top of them and did not come down, and everybody on their feet was in the anteroom in a minute and a quarter, and the wheel turned twice under the floor, and Jo Verrick stood at the well with her hands on her knees and did not say a single word for about two minutes, and then she did.

"Two cleanings or fewer," she said. "That is unchanged and I am not moving it. The seat is untouched and it will stay untouched. But the check is a thing we now read by ear, and I want that written in the ledger in my own hand with a time on it, and I want it written as what it is and not as *noted*, because I have watched a word in the language of a duty turn up on that page in the ninth week and get struck out again, and I would rather not have it in the book twice in a season."

"Written," said Dalla Sen, "in your own hand, with a time, and read back before the ledger closes. And I am not writing *noted*, and I am not writing *attention* either, because attention is a thing you pay attention to and then do not."

"A draw that does not work is not a draw that has been stopped," Nera said. "It is a draw that has been spent, and the card on my board is spent, and it is spent in this room and in Tams Quill's book, and it is not going to be used again by anybody, for anything, ever. Write that down as the last line of the entry and read it back, and do not soften *spent* into *finished*, because a finished thing is a thing that got what it wanted."

"Written," said Dalla, "and read back, and *finished* struck out in the clerk's own hand because the air engineer asked for it and gave a reason."

And then Dalla Sen read the card's own words back out loud, which is what she does, and which is the reason this building is the way it is.

"Stop there," she said. "Read the spending line and read it in the air engineer's own hand, and then read the first line of the same card, and let the room have both of them in the same breath."

Nera read it out herself.

"**A card is not a permit for a room. It is a permit for a work. The card is spent when the length is up and received, or when the work is called off in this room, whichever comes first.**"

"Yesterday morning," said Dalla Sen, "nine people in this anteroom argued for an hour and a half about whether a card could be used twice, and the answer came out in Renn Vale's words and the clerk wrote it down and the air engineer put it on her own board. The draw has failed. **The work has not.** The length is on the shelf, and the work is not finished and it has not been called off, and therefore by the air engineer's own term the card is not spent, and I am going to write *used* and not *spent*, and I am going to write the reason under it, and then I am going to read both back."

"You are rewriting a line because the answer suits the room," said Nera.

"I am writing down what you said yesterday morning, in your hand, on a card in your hand, and I am not rewriting it at all," Dalla said. "I am the clerk. I do not get to be the reason. I get to be the mouth."

Nera Oduya stood at her valve for the length of time it takes to boil a pot, and then said: "Then I will hold to the term, because it is my term and I wrote it, and I am not going to be the air engineer who wrote a rule yesterday morning and broke it the day after because the machine made a noise.

"But I want the arithmetic on the card, in my hand, and I want it read out, and it is this. The card has been used twice. It will be used once more and not again, and the number three goes on it now, and the card dies when the work dies, and if the third one fails then the work is called off in this room in front of these people and the five foot nine stays on the shelf with its number on it until somebody writes a fifth card, which will be mine, and which will be a different method, because the third one was written for a machine that was chattering."

"Written in your hand, with the number three on it," said Dalla Sen, "and I am going to say the third line as well, because the third line is the one that is going to kill somebody if it is forgotten: *if the cloth moves, the person is out, the well is closed, the gallery refloods, and the day is over.* Not shortened. Over."

"Over," said Jo Verrick, from the frame. "And I would like the day not to be over, and I have no way to make that true, and I am not going to stand at a frame with nothing in my hands pretending I have."

---

On the fourth interval the length came up, out of about a hand and a half of water with the shelf still under it, the way a five foot nine comes up a ladder a man could not come up with a jack in his hand: on its side, along the ladder's stile, a rope hitch round it, two people hauling at the well head and Jo Verrick underneath it with a hand on it. The hauling was Tams Quill and Leena Orr, which is a thing that had to be arranged for half a day and argued over twice.

Leena Orr hauled, because she had a right arm that took no weight and a left arm that could hold a line and pull with it, standing on the floor with her feet braced, which is not a thing a person with a dislocated-and-reduced shoulder should do on a wet floor and which she had told the room on the Wednesday and to which the room had said nothing, because the room had learned in the ninth week that Leena Orr's refusals are about her body and her acceptances are about the work, and only one of those is negotiable.

Tams Quill hauled because he could hold a rope in one hand and a chalk in the other and he was the only person in the building who could do both, and because nobody in the room was going to be the one to say that a man with two crushed fingers could not hold a rope while his own hand sat open in a sling on a bench.

Jo had said: "He hauls. He is on the count and he is on the board and he hauls. I am not going to be the person who invents a way of doing a job that keeps a man out of it, because in the seventh week I invented a way of drawing a sump that kept a man out of a housing, and it worked, and a man died on a stair a fortnight later and I have had a week to work out whether it is connected, and a week is not long enough, and I have used it."

Nobody in the anteroom had an answer to that, and Jo Verrick had not been asking for one, and the rope went over the well head at the fourth interval, and five foot nine of public-works timber came up out of a flooded room in the Hearthhold boundary and into an anteroom that holds ten.

Then the twenty minutes, which nobody in the room enjoyed and every person in the room understood, and which is the reason the crew is a crew and not a mob.

Sefu Aran stood at the bench and read the register out loud while Dalla Sen wrote, and he read it into the record rather than into the room, because in a hearing the record is the only part anybody will have, and he said so before he started.

*Public-works spare timber, one length, five foot nine, with its inventory number chalked on the end in the air engineer's hand, found on the bottom shelf of the lower pump gallery at the cache mark, condition as found, sound, with a dry face at one end and six weeks of silt on the other, and no decay, and no split, and no saw marks that are not old.*

*Received by the boundary's keeper, at the fifth bell, item, condition, time, and the receiver is the keeper and not a person of the crew, and no person in this room owns this length of timber, and the crew did not take possession of it. The crew moved it. The line it was written against is in the air engineer's inventory and the line is now closed by the air engineer in her own hand, and there is no claim of any kind made by the person who brought it up.*

Sefu stopped on the last of it and looked up.

"Read the next line into the record and read it twice, and it is not mine, it is the air engineer's, and she is going to say it and I am not going to say it for her."

"I am closing the inventory line," Nera Oduya said. "Shelf cleared as to that mark. One length recovered under a named work, received by the keeper, and the number on the end of it stays on the end of it, because when it goes into a wall the number goes with it, and the next person who opens that wall in twenty years is going to want to know whose timber it was and how it got here. It is a mark of a public-works store and this is not a public-works store, and that is a thing the room should be uncomfortable about and I am not going to pretend I am not."

"Write it," Sefu said.

"It is in," said Dalla Sen, "and I have put her name on it and not the crew's, and I have put mine under hers because I am the clerk and I am the one who will be asked in a year."

---

And then, at about half past the fifth bell, with the well shut and the ladder standing in its six weeks of water and the room emptying the way a room empties when something has gone right for once, a woman came down nine steps into the anteroom of the Hearthhold boundary with a satchel over one shoulder and a folded paper in her hand and a leather case at her belt, and she did not shout, because couriers do not shout, and she stood at the bottom of the steps and looked round the room and read it the way a person reads a room they have been told about for eleven weeks and have never seen.

She was twenty-two or thereabouts. She had a courier's satchel and the boots of somebody who walks all day.

Pell Roke was on the bench at the top of the nine steps with his ear against the stone of the step above the stair head, and his crutch across his knees, and he did not see her, because he was listening to a wheel.

Pell Roke looked up about four seconds later, because Dalla Sen read a name out of the register of persons who come to the threshold, quietly, in the voice of a woman who does not read anything at the threshold in any other tone.

"Talla Roke," Dalla said, "courier, out of the middle gate, and there is a clerk with her off the middle passage, and the keeper has not been asked yet and neither has the clerk."

And the boy on the bench at the top of nine steps said, "That is my sister," in a voice with no ornament in it at all, and did not move, and did not stand up, and did not go down the steps, and Dalla Sen wrote it down in the ledger with the time on it, in her own hand, because it was a fact about two people who were in the same building.

And the courier looked up at the top of the steps, and put her hand flat on the satchel's strap, and said, "Pell," and nothing else, and then looked back at the room.

"Which one of you is the keeper," said Talla Roke.
