# Chapter 115 — The Floor Is Dry

**The fifth day of the twenty-second week was a Friday and the corridor was open and the school ran at the far end of it, and the lamp test on the shut school corner's floor was read at the first bell by the woman whose terms it was, with a boy holding a lamp and a rule on the flags, and it came out dry.**

**The condition had been in Renn Vale's hand on a page of her own since the fourth day of the twentieth week,** and nobody had ever expected it to be met, including the woman who wrote it.

"**Dry under a lamp through a night and a morning,**" she said, reading it, "**and I am not going to improve it now that it is about to be true, because a condition you improve when it is met is a condition you were hoping to fail.**"

She went down the nine steps herself and stood in the doorway of a room nobody had been in for three weeks with a boy of fourteen holding a lamp and a woman with a rule on the flags, and the three of them were in there for nine minutes, and the anteroom was full at the same time and nobody was in the anteroom who could see any of it.

When she came back up she said three things in the order she said them, and the clerk wrote all three and read them back.

"**It is dry. The dark at the low corner is a colour and not a wet. The line across the joint is not there. The bed joint under this floor is dry to the touch of a rule laid on it and I have laid it on it in two places and I am not going to say dry to the middle, I am going to say dry at two places, and nobody is to write the middle out of it or into it.**

"**The condition is met. I met it and I am opening the room.**

"**And the school is not going into it.**"

---

**The room got that for about four seconds, and then the room had it for the rest of the week.**

"**Say why,**" said Dalla Sen, with the chalk up, and she said it in the flat voice and not in a hurry.

"**Say the reason,**" said Renn Vale, "**and I am going to say it badly and I would like a better one from somebody before the week is out.**

"**I wrote that condition in the fourteenth week for a wet floor. And the floor is dry. And the floor is dry because a man put grease and chalk on a jamb on the second day of this week, and that is the whole of the reason, and the whole of the reason is a thing two women can take off a wall.**

"**In a month the mark will come off, because I have written the condition that takes it off and I intend to be the one who takes it off. And when it comes off that head will start going out again, and the joint will start feeding that floor again, and nine children will be standing on it. And I would have opened the room to nine children on a grease mark, and I wrote a condition about a floor and not about a wall, and the fault is mine and I am not going to fix it by not opening the room.**"

"Then the room stays shut," said Bel Ordry.

"**The room opens,**" said Renn Vale. "**The condition is met and I met it and I am not going to have a condition of mine sitting on a page looking met while it is not, because the day this building starts leaving a condition on a page that is true on paper and false in the room is the day every condition in this building is worth nothing.**

"**So the room opens, and the floor is dry, and the school is not in it, and I am going to write both of those on the same line.**"

Marek Venn heard it and did not say anything, and what he did not say was *I did that*. He had put a line of chalk and a smear of gland grease on a doorframe on the second day of this week, and eight days later a room with a wet floor in it was dry, and the room was open, and nine children were two hundred feet away in the worst room in this building, and both of those sentences were true and the second one was going to be true on Friday as well.

He had spent twenty-two weeks learning that a boundary could make a room safe by making it a room nobody was in, and he had said so out loud in this room ten days before he did the other thing, and now the other thing had produced a dry room nobody was in, and he could not tell which of the two sentences frightened him more, and the honest answer was the first one.

---

**Then Ilsa Norr said it, from the far end of the corridor with nine children behind her in a room with a cold hearth in it,** and she was not consoled and nobody tried her.

"**We were moved out of a wet room and we are not being moved back into a dry one.**

"**And I want to be careful, because I have watched this room argue for three weeks and I am the one who has been losing. So: if you had asked me on Monday whether I would take a room held up by something a person can take off a wall, I would have said yes. I would have said yes and I would have been wrong, and I would have had nine children on a floor that a woman sixty miles away decides to take a cloth off, and I would not have been able to see her, and I would not have been able to be angry at her in a way that did anything.**

"**You did not ask me. That is not a favour and I am not thanking anybody for it, because the reason you did not ask me is that a change to a room people use is written by the person who keeps it, and that is a rule in this building that is older than me, and it happened to work out.**

"**So put this in the book in this order. A room was shut. A room was opened. A school day was not moved. The reason is beside them and there is nothing between them and I am not going to be the one who says which of the three is the important one.**"

Dalla Sen wrote it and read it out and Ilsa Norr said yes, and the clerk entered underneath it, in her own hand, in the shape she has used once already:

"**I am putting three facts next to each other with a reason beside them and nothing between them. The reason is not a reason. The reason is that I am putting them near each other so that a reader in a year cannot make them into a finding.**"

---

**And at the fifth bell a boy of fourteen read the numbers out loud in a room, because that is what he is for, and the anteroom was full and the corridor was open and the school had run all day at the far end of it.**

Pell Roke was on the bench at the top of the nine steps with his crutch across his knees and his ear against the stone, and he did not go down the steps and he did not go into the corridor and he did not go over the waist, and Dalla Sen handed him the kitchen part of the plan from the top of the steps the way she has handed it to him twice.

"**First column.**"

"**Ten. Four. Eight. Ten. Six. Nine. Five. Two. And a dash.**"

"**Second column.**"

He read the nine conditions with no number and no day of the week on any of them, and the fourth of them was the word *wet*, and the eighth was empty, and the ninth was a dash.

"**Third column.**"

"**Eight lines and one empty,**" he said, "**and I am not going to say which one is empty because the man who keeps it has said in a room that he is not going to say, and I read numbers and that is the end of what I do.**"

"**Fourth column.**"

He looked at it for a while. He had it in his hand and he had it the right way up and he is fourteen and he has been in this building five months and he has an ear for a page.

"**There is nothing in it,**" he said.

He had never been asked to read that column before, and it had never once been read out loud in a room, and it has been empty for weeks with a reason on the page in two hands — Renn Vale's with a modal struck out of it and Nera Oduya's in its place — and a boy of fourteen had just said out loud, in a room, in the ordinary voice, that there was nothing in it.

"**Enter it,**" said Dalla Sen. "**Enter that a boy read an empty column out loud and said there was nothing in it, and enter that it is the fourth time it has been read and it has always been nothing, and enter that this is the first time it has been heard out loud as nothing, and enter that nobody had asked him to and I asked him to.**"

"**Why did you ask him,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**Because a column that is empty and has a reason on it is the only thing in this building that is a decision on purpose,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and I have read it out in the air engineer's hand and the cook's hand and I have read the reason and it never once occurred to me to have a person with an ear say the thing the column says, and he said it in four words and it took a fortnight of pages to say what he said.**"

---

**At the sixth bell the board was walked, and the column with nothing over the top of it was referred to and not read out,** because the count had begun refusing to itemise it and the clerk had begun refusing to read it out in full, and both refusals were entered and the room let them stand.

"**The crew are at fifty-three and a half and they moved on Monday and I am not giving the reason because a clerk gave it in a room and it is in the book in better words than mine. A man of twenty-two is fifty days and fifty nights into a count that is his own, and it is on this board, and it is not a debt and it is not a wage, and it is a man's.**

"**The boundary is at a hundred and seventy-two and a quarter, and that is four on the first day of this week and three and five and four and three, and it adds, and both columns reconcile and they have reconciled for seven weeks.**"

Then he read the thirteen entries, which is one more than he read a week ago, and he read them in the way he reads them, and the thirteenth was the mark, and the twelfth was a floor, and the eleventh and the one before it were nulls in a man's own left hand in a room where a woman had told him where to put his hand.

"**Eleven nulls, one claim, and one mark. Nine in the ordinary column and four in the one with nothing over the top of it. The claim is still a claim and its condition is still not met. The mark is not a null and I am not going to pretend it is and I am not going to call it a reading, because a man put grease on a jamb and came away with a hand that will not shut and a sound in a room he already had.**"

What he did not do was read out the column with nothing over the top of it, and Dalla Sen did not read it out either, and it was referred to, and the reason was entered by him and it was: *the clerk and the count have both begun to refuse to itemise it, and the reason given is that a thing read in full four times is a thing with a shape, and we do not have its shape yet and we are not going to make one up.* And the column is still unheaded, for the fifth batch running, and the count gave no reason for that on Friday and was not asked.

---

**And the last thing in the week was that the elected keeper of this boundary was not in it, and nobody in it knew when he would be, and the room had to be told what to write down about that, and it was Renn Vale who told it.**

"**We are not writing how long he has been gone,**" she said. "**I want that in the book as a decision and not as an oversight. A number of days is a thing a room can be wrong about, and this room has been wrong about a number before, and it took a week to find out and the finding was that we had been writing down somebody's permission instead of everybody.**

"**So the book says: the keeper is on a road with two true sentences in his coat, and he will say them at the first of the month and not one day before, and we do not know when he arrives and we are not going to guess and we are not going to write a figure. And if he is not back next week then next week it is, and the sentence does not change, and that is how a boundary waits.**"

"**And if he comes back on a day we have written nothing about?**" said Wen Pask, from the fourth step, who has no standing in that room and has had it anyway for five months.

"**Then it is a day,**" said Renn Vale, "**and it is not on a page until one of us is sitting down.**"

**At the end of the corridor the school corner's door was standing open,** with a dry floor in it and nobody in it and the lamp still on the flags where a boy and a woman with a rule had left it, and **nobody in this building looked up at the four marks and the groove on the lintel above it, and they were still there, and they were still read two ways, and they still answered nothing.**
