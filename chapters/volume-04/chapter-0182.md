# Chapter 182 — The Room Is Emptied And Filled

The third day of the thirty-fifth week is a Wednesday and the corridor is open, and the school ran for the thirty-third morning in the room at the kitchen-line end, which is the room a printed sheet has in it and has not struck. Nine went up the nine steps at the first bell with a woman who keeps that room ahead of them, and nine children crossed the anteroom twice, and the light came off the flags at the slant it comes at on open mornings and there is no weather in this building and no other thing to look at.

At the second bell the room at the kitchen-line end was emptied.

That is a document being obeyed and not a room being shut, and the difference took the morning. Ilsa Norr walked all nine out of it in the order she walks out of it, which is last one first. She stood them along the corridor wall, the way you stand nine children along a wall when you are not going to move them again until somebody tells you where. A woman with a slate stood at the end of the line and said nothing. The four rooms of this boundary were held as one space, and nothing was written down about that this week, because it has been true for thirty-five weeks and has not stopped being true. It is the cheapest standing anybody in this building has ever been given and the only one that has never cost anybody anything, and the clerk has entered that she has been suspicious of it for six weeks and has not found the thing to be suspicious of.

Dalla Sen entered that the first numbered thing on a Warden-General's instruction was complied with on the next open morning after the day it was read in, at the second bell, and that the compliance was entered at the hour and not at the day, and entered the reason: a clerk who enters a thing as done without the hour has entered a thing she did not watch.

"**Then take the other half of that entry,**" said Bel Ordry, from the step.

"**That a sheet says *on the next open morning* and a clerk answers it with *at the second bell*, and that a second bell is this boundary's answer and not the sheet's,**" she said. "**Then the sheet gets a better answer than it asked for and everybody in it is pleased, and a sheet that is better answered than it was written is a sheet that will be written shorter next time.**"

She entered that too.

Then at the third bell the woman who keeps that room said the thing she had come up the nine steps to say, and she said it standing in the kitchen-line doorway with her slate against her hip and nine children in a corridor behind her.

"**The nine go back in at the fourth bell,**" Ilsa Norr said. "**And I am going to say the reason out loud before anybody puts it in a book, because I have been in that room since the first bell with my hand on nine hearth stones in order, and I knelt at the ninth one longer than the other eight took together, and this morning it told me nothing, and I have decided that a stone which has told me nothing today gets nine children in the room with it.**"

"**That is not a reason,**" said Nera Oduya, from the third step, and she came down two of them, which is all she came down.

"**It is the reason and I know it is thin,**" Ilsa Norr said. "**The reason that is not thin is this. That room has been emptied twice in ten days. Once by a woman striking it out of a printed sheet in nine seconds in the twenty-ninth week, and once this morning by a number in a numbered list that came down a passage with six pieces of timber. And a thing that can be emptied twice in ten days by two documents is a thing that is being emptied, and the woman who empties it is not in either document, and she is the only part of the emptying I have ever been able to trust.**"

Nobody in the anteroom had heard her put it that way before, and the clerk entered it in her words.

"**And I am going to say the other half and I would like it entered in the space where a reason goes and not in a column,**" Ilsa Norr said. "**The half against it is that nine children in a room with a wall behind it that has a gap in it the width of two fingers is nine children next to a thing that four people in this building cannot describe and I am one of the four. I have not said no to that room in front of anybody this week. I have said the room is not to be used, and I have said it every day, and today I am saying the opposite with the same mouth, and if a room ever wants to know how I decide things, that is how I decide them, and I would like it written down where somebody can find it.**"

The air engineer came down the last of the three steps then, all of them, which she does for air and for nothing else, and stood in front of the door of the room at the kitchen-line end with her dyed cloth out and did not go in.

"**I certify the air of that room and I am not going to stand in this doorway and say nothing so that a woman can have her morning,**" she said. "**Air comes out of that wall. It has done since the seventh day of last week and it comes about four times in the time it takes to say a sentence and stops for a count, and a room with a draught out of a wall in it has an air that is not the air I certified on the fourth day of the last month, and the limit I set a fortnight ago has no number and no day on it, and what the limit says is that the air stays what it is now.**"

"**Which it has not,**" said Renn Vale.

"**Which it has not,**" Nera Oduya said, "**and I am not going to soften that by standing here. I have said in this room that I certify nothing and refuse nothing, and I am not going to do either of those things on a Wednesday morning to make a keeper's decision easier for her. A boundary that has a room and an air in it and a person who cannot be in both places is a boundary that has a decision, and the decision is hers, and I am the person who has to live in the air afterwards.**"

She went in. She was in there about a minute and a half. The pump ran its whole length while she was in there. She came out and said four numbers, and did not say a fifth. Dalla Sen wrote the four down and did not ask for the fifth. The clerk entered that she had not asked, and entered that this was the third time in a fortnight she had noticed herself not asking, which is a change of kind and not a change of number.

Ilsa Norr stood in the doorway for a while with the four numbers in front of her that she had been handed without being asked for them.

"**Then the nine go back in,**" she said. "**I have said it once and I am not going to say it a second time, and nobody in this room is going to be thanked. Dalla, write the reason down the way I said it and not the way you would say it.**"

Marek Venn had been at the end of the trestle for the whole of it with his book shut, and he had wanted, for about four minutes, to say that he could go and look at the wall behind that room with a lamp and a line and come back and tell them what was in it. He had wanted it the way he wants things, which is the way a man wants a thing he has not been asked for. He had asked to be asked and not to be let in, nine days ago, and had discovered the difference between the two at a cost, and the cost was that this morning a woman was putting nine children back into a room with a gap in its wall on the strength of a stone that told her nothing and four numbers out of a doorway, and there was nothing at all in that sentence he could improve on with a lamp.

So he shut his book and said nothing, and the clerk entered that he said nothing, and entered that a man who drew a hole in that wall three days running did not offer to put a lamp in it on the morning a keeper decided about nine children.

"**The way you said it,**" Dalla Sen said.

She read back out of her own book what she had written. Then the woman who wrote it read the same line out of the book against what she had said with her own mouth, and the two were the same. The clerk entered that the only writing in any document in this building this week was written by the hand of the person who keeps the row, and that a second person compared it. She entered that a clerk who corrects a keeper's line has stopped being a clerk and started being an office.

Then the sixth person in that room was the man who has a question he thinks is not fair, and Bel Ordry put it, and it was not about the four numbers.

"**The room is to be used again on the fourth day of a week,**" he said. "**And a printed sheet in this building says the inspection is on a fifth day. So the room will be full of nine children on the day a man from an office is scheduled to look at it, and the room's own keeper decided that this morning, and the sheet did not put her there.**"

"**Then say what you want,**" said Nella Bray from the kitchen-line doorway, where she had been standing for an hour without anybody asking her to stand anywhere.

"**I want the room not to be full on a fifth day,**" Bel Ordry said, "**and I am not going to be the man who says it, because a man who keeps a page is a man who has spent thirty-five weeks asking whether a page in a coat is better than a printed one, and the honest answer to that is a man who will take every cheap way out of a morning once he has said he does not know the expensive one.**"

"**The reason there is not one,**" Renn Vale said, "**is that the school runs on the three open mornings because it has run on the three open mornings since before any of us were in this building, and a keeper who empties a room of nine children on the fourth day because of a sheet on it has let the sheet pick the day, and a sheet that picks the day has this room.**"

Nobody improved on that either, and Mero Senn said the sentence from the doorway, once, and then said the other half of it himself.

"**A plan that depends on a day of the week for the safety of nine children is not a plan,**" he said. "**I said that in this room on the seventh day of last week. I have had a week to think about whether I was right and I have not changed, and I am not going to be gentle about it. So here is the other half, and I have been wrong in this room before and this is what it costs to say it. A day of the week is not a fact about a wall. It is a fact about a bell, and a bell is the only thing in this building that has never once let anybody down, and I have spent nine weeks telling this room to stop believing me and start believing the stone.**"

Dalla Sen entered the sentence and the other half, in that order, and entered that a physician who had argued in this room that a day of the week is not a plan had also said in this room that a bell is a thing to believe, and that the two are the same argument pointed at different objects, and that the clerk can see the shape of it and is not going to write the shape down.

By the fourth bell the anteroom's terms were said, and the count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change, and the outer service door was the one a body went out of, and the fifth term was in a room with both the people it names in it, and the pump was attended and stopped on a rising water line that has not moved.

Renn Vale said one thing about the six strangers while she was saying it.

"**They have been on the common landing since the first bell and they have not been in this room once,**" she said. "**And that is my decision and not the clerk's, because this room is a crossing and a crossing that takes nine people twice in a morning does not need a seventh pair of eyes standing in it holding a tally, and I have kept six things for thirty-five weeks and one of them is that a room is not the place to count people in.**"

"**You always give the other half and I have stopped waiting for it,**" Bel Ordry said, "**so here is mine first, which is new, and I want you to know it is new.**"

"**What I have actually done is move the arithmetic,**" Renn Vale said. "**The common landing has carried no number on any page in this building since the first week and I have just put six strangers on it for four days. So I have not kept a room empty. I have moved a count to a floor that was never on the sheet, and a floor that was never on the sheet is the only kind of floor in this building a person can be illegal on.**"

Down on the common landing side of the party wall, five people who had come down a passage on Monday had spent the morning doing the only thing anybody can do to a wall from that side of it, which is to stand in front of it for hours and be wrong about nothing. Emrys Callow had set out his own lengths on the flags, chalked, by measurement and not by eye, and had not put a mark in the stone, and had said out loud, twice, to make sure that the second man heard it, that nothing in this building gets a mark cut in it.

The wall from that side is nine hundred years of stone. The line where the lime repair stops and the old stone starts runs at the height of a man's shoulder. It is the same line the anteroom has been reading with a string for thirty-five weeks, and nobody had ever stood on this side of it and looked at it before. Emrys Callow stood on this side of it for a full morning and looked at it, and came up the nine steps at the fourth bell with the first of the arguments he and the woman who keeps a corridor were going to have over three days.

"**That wall is nine hundred years of good stone,**" he said, "**and the two hundred years of bad repair is in it, and a man cannot shore a good wall, and a man cannot shore a bad repair either because a bad repair is not a shape, it is a story about a man in a hurry. So I have six shores on a good wall and I have no idea what they are doing, and I would like somebody in this building to tell me what they are doing, and I have asked twice and both times I was told it was not the morning.**"

"**And the answer,**" said Renn Vale, "**is that the answer is that the six are on that wall for a reason that is written on a sheet that came down the passage on Monday, and the reason on the sheet is a decision without a responsible operator, and a decision without a responsible operator becomes an accident, and a Warden-General with a good hand wrote that in his own unnumbered sentence and he was right.**"

"**Then I have been working nine days for an accident,**" Callow said, "**and I would like to know the hours, and I would like to know them before I go back down the passage, because I have five men and a rate and no district work and the boundary has a pot at eight days, and I would rather be told a number than be told to be patient.**"

"**Then that is the number,**" Dalla Sen said, "**and the number is a clerk's number and not a keeper's, and I am going to give it to him in a room with two strangers in it, which I have never done, and here it is. The boundary's own hours are on a page and the days are written on it by the day the work was done, and the two of them reconcile, and I read them out loud at the sixth bell on a morning when the room is ready to hear them and I do not read them on a morning when it is not.**"

She did not read them at the sixth bell on this one.

The count was read at one hundred and seventeen, and the reason was entered, and the reason was that nine children had gone back through a door in this building for the first time in a fortnight by a keeper's own decision, and that a clerk does not stand up and read a total on the morning a room got its people back. Two today and two hundred and forty-eight since the keeper signed. The boundary is at three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter. It adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.

Tams Quill read the eighteen and the column with nothing over the top of it stayed without a heading, and he gave no reason for it and nobody asked, and the six names from yesterday did not go on the board, and Dalla Sen entered that a man who reads numbers was asked nothing by anybody and refused two things to anybody.

The crew are at seventy-one and there has been no sixth asking, and the reason is in the book. The reason is that a room this morning was deciding whether nine children go back into a room. A clerk who asks five people to carry a piece of work on the morning a room is deciding a thing like that is asking them to carry the mood of a morning that does not belong to them.

The pot is at eight days and the seam gave nothing on this morning either, and the four households at a second gate have nothing, and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody. And the count. Wen Pask is one hundred and seventeen days and one hundred and seventeen nights into a count that is his own, and it is not going to be one hundred and eighteen, and she has said in twenty-six weeks running that she is going to keep writing it, and nobody has thanked her.
