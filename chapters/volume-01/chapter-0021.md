# Chapter 21 — The Next Work-Bell

The pressure turn came on the second morning, and nobody had to be told about it.

The wheel below the floor turned twice in the space of a breath, the way it had during the review that produced the sheet, and the anteroom ran without a single instruction being given aloud. Nera was already at the air valve with the dyed cloth lifted. Sefu had both hands on the civic wheel. Renn moved the enclosed lantern to the landing bracket. Mikka went out through the clinic gate. Dalla took her place at the threshold with the bell rope in one hand and the ledger closed under her arm, because a person holding an open book cannot hear a bell.

The anteroom emptied. Oren and Tams took Leena's litter down the outer step without putting weight through her right arm; Iven carried the pump-parts box clear of the escape line; Jo was already out with her hands empty. Pell went last on his crutch, keeping his splinted ankle off the sill lip, and he did not stop to look back at the pump.

Marek stayed a moment longer than he had any right to.

The floor answered the stair with a shallow ripple that travelled sideways and died at the wall. It told him nothing he could use. It told him, precisely, that the room was connected to something below and that the connection was not finished with them. He had felt four such things since the collapse. Four brief, relative impressions that had never once turned into a measurement.

He left with the others.

They stood on the upper landing for a count of two hundred. The lantern flame leaned and came back. Dalla rang once for the clinic and twice for the outer valve, waited, and called the all-clear from the threshold. Then Sefu turned the wheel and the anteroom opened again for one attended interval.

That was the whole turn. It took four minutes and cost five people the middle of a morning, and by the time it was over the crew had learned nothing new about the stair and everything new about how quickly a room can be emptied by people who have practiced it.

The service bypass hatch had stayed latched through all four minutes, because Iven had put his hand on the lever twice and taken it off twice, and neither time had he said anything about it, and both times it had been Nera who said it instead. The hatch stayed latched because the stair was pushing through that shaft, and opening it would have given the pressure somewhere shorter to go, and the whole argument had already been had once and had ended with a line in a record that everybody could now recite without reading.

"Then we review," Sefu said.

The board hung where the pump crew could see it from the grate. Marek read it standing, because sitting would have made it a meeting and he did not want it to be one. The sentence he had rewritten the day before still sat at the top: *the anteroom and service bypass are conditionally open for attended maintenance and limited boundary work only.* Beneath it, Nera's five watches. Beneath that, Jo's hand on the pump line: short attended intervals, no overnight run, delivery seat scored, no spare released. And at the foot of the page, in Dalla's careful hand, the review line: *the second work-bell after the next complete pressure turn.*

Dalla counted the bells from the shelf. "Two. It is due."

"Read the sheet," Sefu said.

She read it. That was the practice, and the practice had survived the argument that created it. She read the air conditions in Nera's order, the boundary conditions in Sefu's order, the pump in Jo's order, and the corrections in Leena's, and when she reached the words *temporary maintainers* she did not slow down and she did not speed up.

Then the pump wanted its interval, because the wet seam had put a finger of water into the service well overnight and the lower mark had moved with it.

Jo took the lever. Tams counted the strokes aloud. Oren took the relief at the frame, and put his weight where he had learned to put it, over the plinth rather than against the housing. Pell called from the bench with his slate on his knees. On the ninth stroke the delivery check gave its ragged double knock, and Jo stopped it before the tenth, wiped the seat, and started the count again from one. The bright scrape along the bronze lip had not widened. That was the news, and it was small, and Dalla wrote it down as small.

Tams marked the water skins on the case board. Five liters from the collapse, and the separately sealed condensation liter in its stoppered jar where it had been since the sixth day. He drew the line between the Hearthhold vessels and the crew's own supply, and beneath it the standing rule: during a named work period the kitchen's provision is the crew's drinking supply; the sealed liter opens only if a shift overruns or a person is unwell. Mikka's tally board hung three feet away with forty-one strokes on it, and the two numbers had never been merged and never would be.

Marek had come to the review with an argument, and the argument was this.

The anteroom held ten people including one litter, for two hours, with the pump attended and the outer door open. That was a workshop with a roof. Every Hearthhold resident in the rooms behind the gate had a worse answer than that for a bad afternoon, and a much worse answer for a bad afternoon that happened while a pump was down or a stair was turning or a child was feverish across the corridor from the only adult who knew where the cloth was kept.

He waited for the interval to close before he said it, because saying it during the pump stroke would have made it look like a reason to keep the water down.

"You are describing a room for six outsiders and one stretcher," Leena said.

"I am describing a room the six of us happen to be in."

"No." She shifted on the cot, keeping the sling tight. "You are describing a room for six outsiders, and you have already decided that the six of us are the people who need it. That is the same error as the old charter, in a smaller room."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked at the board, not at him. "The rating is ten including one litter. If that were a neighborhood room, it would hold a class and half a family, and nothing in this sheet was ever tested above ten."

Which was the objection he had expected, and the answer to it was the reason he had waited for the interval to close.

"Then test a larger room," he said. "That is the ordinary next step. We have five people, a pump record, a room that passed a full-occupancy test once, and a sheet that says the next review is due. A room for six is not a refuge. It is a shed we happen to be permitted to sleep in."

Jo wiped her hands on her cloth. "Say what it costs."

"It costs a design, a test, and material we do not have yet."

"It costs me more than that." She did not raise her voice. "Every cleaning of that check is half a day of my shutter route. Two cleanings since the collapse. The kitchen brace and the wash-room shutter are still standing open on my list, and now there is a third thing that stops me when I walk past it. You want a bigger room. A bigger room has a bigger pump, or a longer duty, or a second throat that somebody has to watch, and that somebody is standing in this circle with a basket she cannot put down."

"I am not asking you to stop your route."

"You are asking for a reason to make stopping it easier."

The words landed because they were accurate. Marek felt the shape of what he had actually proposed: not a room, but a project. A project has a beginning that everyone can agree to and an end that arrives on somebody else's schedule, four months later, when the person who said yes has spent the winter paying for it.

Iven turned the flat file over in his hands. He had been listening with the file held but not used, the way he did when he was thinking about grain.

"You are asking the wrong question," Iven said. "You are asking whether we should keep the small conditional arrangement or build a large refuge. Those are not the two options. The second option is whether a refuge built here is a room this neighborhood can keep. And look at what keeps it." He ticked them on his fingers. "Water down, which means a seat I cannot replace. Air in, which means Nera and five watches and every one of those watches a person who has to be somewhere else at a certain hour. A way out, which means a throat that stays clear. That is not a room. That is a shift that never ends, sold as walls."

"We would train people," Marek said.

"You would," Iven agreed. "We would all be here. The guild is not. The stores have what they have. In nine months, when the people who agreed to this are doing their own work, who is at the lever?"

Jo made a small sound that was almost a laugh. "Write that question down. Not his answer. The question."

Dalla wrote it.

Sefu had not moved from the wheel. "There is a second thing you have not said out loud, and it is mine to say for this boundary. If you build a room that Hearthhold residents use, it does not become theirs because they use it. It becomes a room with a keeper, and the keeper is the person who signed for it. You are six people with an unsigned sheet and a suspended bond between them." He put his thumb on the wheel. "I can refuse admission at this boundary. I cannot hand you the rooms behind it, and I cannot stop a room keeper from saying yes either. If Ilsa Norr teaches nine children in the upper gallery and says the room is hers, that is her answer to make. If the kitchen says no, the kitchen says no. Nobody in this room can take either of those by agreeing among ourselves."

"Then I am asking," Marek said, "whether the crew will help design a room that ordinary residents can use. Not that we will own it. That we will do the work of turning what you need into something that bears weight, breathes, drains, empties, and survives an ordinary bad day."

The word *ordinary* did the work. He had learned it from a room that had failed him.

For a while the only sound was the pump settling and the wet seam ticking as it cooled.

Renn spoke from the landing, where she had not come in because coming in would have made her a counted occupant and she had not agreed to be counted. "My mother kept the school register for thirty years. When the room above the east stair is empty it is the biggest dry floor in this part of the Hearthhold. It is also the room where nine children sit when the lessons are running, and it is the room I have been told by three different people to leave alone. If you want it, you will have to hear why they say that."

"Then hear it," Marek said.

Dalla closed the ledger on her thumb and looked at the review line at the foot of the board, which was now due, which was now a real decision, and not a promise that anybody would look again.

"Write the decision first," she said. "The room we have, or the room they are asking for. Not both at once, and not neither with nothing on the page."

Marek had the chalk in his left hand. His right rested open against the bench, where anyone could see it and where it stayed.

He had spent three days learning that the hardest sentence to write was the one that left a blank in it. Now he had to write the first sentence of something that had no precedent in this room at all, and every person standing in it would have to live in whatever came out.

"The anteroom and service bypass continue for the next named work period under the marked conditions," he said. "That is decided and it is not the answer to the question. Second line."

He wrote it with his left hand, the letters uneven and legible.

*A larger room for ordinary residents is proposed, not agreed. Design work is invited. No admission changes, no ownership is created, and no room is opened until the people who keep it have been asked by name.*

Renn read it from the landing and did not come in.

Then she went to fetch Ilsa Norr, because a schoolroom was not going to be discussed by people who had never been inside it.
