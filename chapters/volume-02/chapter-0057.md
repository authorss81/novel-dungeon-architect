# Chapter 57 — Reading It by Ear

It was the fourth morning, and Jo Verrick said the thing about the check that nobody had asked her to say, flat, at the frame, with her hand on the delivery valve and the tally board behind her. She said it in the anteroom and not at the frame, because she wanted it in the room.

"I want that said plainly because I know what a room does with a thing that is not getting worse. A room decides it is stable. A room decides a hiss is a hiss and a hiss is the new normal and the new normal is fine. The check is not getting worse because there is nothing left of it to get worse. It is a hole with a shape and it is hissing because that is what air does coming through a hole of that shape at that pressure, and it will hiss the same way in the cold and the warm and the wet and the dry, and that is the only good news I have had about it this month.

"So we read it by ear. All of us. Every time somebody stands at that frame. And the rule is: if the note changes, the draw stops and the work stops and nobody argues with me about it, and I do not care whose work it is or whose day it is. And when it does change, and it will, then the change is not a bad morning. It is the seat. The seat is not a thing I can clean. It is a thing I can only say out loud, and then the room decides what to do about a pump that is going."

"Read out the note," said Ilsa Norr, "so that a person who has not been standing at the frame knows what they are listening for."

"Two things and no more," Jo said. "A hiss that stays a hiss. And a hiss that gets *thinner*. That is the whole of the vocabulary. A thicker hiss is the water coming up. A thinner hiss is the seat, and I have two cleanings on the seat or fewer, and if the hiss goes thin I am not going to be able to do anything about it in this room, because there is no bronze in this building and the seat is a thing you cut in a housing with your hands."

"So we are listening for two notes on a machine that used to have a whole vocabulary," said Sefu Aran.

"Yes," said Jo. "And I would like that written in the ledger in a room that can be frightened, and not on a board, and not with *noted* on it."

---

A Thursday. The school ran, because the school always ran — Mikka Hale had put that in the anteroom on the Sunday and Ilsa Norr had repeated it on the Monday, and neither of them had been asked to and both of them had said it in the flat voice.

"Nine children come at the first bell and they go at the fourth," Ilsa said, "and a day lost is a day they do not get back, because there are not eleven more Mondays in this year, and the corridor is open on a day the school runs and the waist is a post, and that is the deal, and the deal was struck in the ninth week and it is not being re-struck because a pump has started making a noise."

So the Thursday was: a school day, the corridor open, six watches filled, the waist a live post held by a person, and the stair used twice in the pump's window for people who had asked to be let over and were counted by Ilsa at the head of the crossing.

Renn Vale took the waist. She said the whole of it out loud before she took it, the way she had in the ninth week, and this time she said one thing more.

"I am going to say the thing that has been underneath every stair period for nine weeks," she said, "and I am saying it because the corridor is open today and I am on the waist today and the waist is a post, and if I do not say it then in a month somebody will be able to say that Renn Vale stood on a stair.

"I do not stand on a stair. I said it in the thirty-eighth day and I said it was wider than the wet seam, and it is wider than the wet seam, and the wet seam is the waist, and I hold the waist four days in seven and today is one of them. I am on the waist. I am not on the stair. If a person this room needs me to be on the stair, they are going to have to come to the upper landing and ask me and hear me say no, and I would rather they heard it from me than worked it out."

Which was in the ledger, in Renn Vale's own hand, with a time on it, and which the room accepted without one word of argument, because a thing that is refused in advance and in writing is the cheapest thing in a building to keep.

---

Four minutes early, and no warning, and this time everybody was off.

Not on the twenty-fourth stroke this time. On the nineteenth, four intervals in, with the check hissing exactly the way it has hissed every day since the draw, and Jo Verrick's own hand on the frame and no warning from anybody because there was nobody to warn her, because the whole of the arrangement in this building is that the plan is a plan for stopping and not a plan for timing, and a plan for stopping is a plan that requires somebody to shout.

The interval came off. The six minutes began. The six minutes are the part with the water up and the air in the boundary going the wrong way and the stair pushing through the shaft under the anteroom, and the route's own terms say that nobody is on the stair in those six minutes, and there was nobody on the stair, because the party had been back on the near side for a count and a bit and the woman on the string had the word out and the bearer had them off the lip and did not leave it.

Then Jo said, "Now," in a voice nobody in the anteroom had heard her use, and everybody on their feet was out to the outer step in a minute and a quarter, and the school came with them, nine children and their teacher at a walk and not at a run, because Ilsa Norr's order had been the same for nine weeks, and the last child came through the school door with an adult either side of him because no child in this building goes on a slab on her own, and the anteroom was empty.

The litter was still coming down the corridor on four bearers.

"A minute and a quarter for the ones who were on their feet, and the litter set the number and the litter has not come," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel, "and that is the same as the ninth week, and the room to the kitchen line was a minute and a half then and is a minute and a half now, and I am recording that the system worked and the pump did not, and those are two different sentences and only one of them is a reason to be pleased."

---

Ruk Sella came off the plinth about four seconds after the interval, and Pell Roke called it eight seconds before that from the top of the nine steps, in a voice that did not carry and did not need to.

And the whole of the apparatus worked, and the record says so, and the record is the reason it worked: the terms were read at the school door by Dalla Sen in her own hand before the first crossing, the bearer at the near lip was named on the day's board and was Isel Ferrow, and the woman from the wash rooms had been asked at the top of the three steps by Renn Vale on the morning and had said yes and had had the asking and the answer written down in Renn's hand with the time on it, and the plinth was read by Ruk Sella, and the bell was rung by Bel Ordry, who was asked in person and told what he was being given and repeated the whole of it back.

Nobody died. There is one sentence in this record for that and it is: the plan was a plan for stopping, and the pump stopped, and everybody stopped.

And then the corridor was shut and the stair was corded and the day was over, and the pump came back on at the fifth interval and ran nineteen strokes and stopped, and the anteroom got its ten back, and nobody in that room was pleased about anything.

---

The closure went on the clerk's page, in the clerk's hand, with a name and a date and a reason, because that is what the clause says, and the clause is the woman from the wash rooms' own eleven words and it is in her hand and not Renn's, and Dalla Sen has never once let it be anything other than what it is.

"Shut by me," the woman from the wash rooms said, when Sefu asked her, at the top of the three steps, in front of the room, and she did not have to be asked twice. "And the reason is mine and I am going to say it out loud instead of writing it and having you read it, because I can say it and write it, so I am going to do the harder one.

"Jo's hiss has gone thin and I heard it go thin, and I am not a pump hand and I do not know what a thin hiss is, and that is exactly why I am shutting it. I am not going to stand four people on that stone on the word of a man who has told me what a thin hiss is. I have been on that slab once when nobody asked me and I did not know what was happening and that is how Oren Sill's Thursday happened, and I have had a week to think about it, and a week is not long enough, and the difference between that Thursday and this morning is that this morning I know something I did not know then, and it is enough."

Dalla Sen wrote the reason in the woman's own hand as she spoke it, which she has done before, and said it back once, and then said it a second time because the woman asked her to.

By the fourth bell it was gone, the pump came on, and the anteroom cleared, and the school went home along a corridor that is open because the school runs, with a cord across the top of three steps at the end of it and two people standing at that cord, and Renn Vale said the word and Sefu Aran said the word, in that order, and the cord was tied after them and the stair was shut for the rest of the week, which meant the waist was not a post for the rest of the week, and which Renn said out loud at the fifth bell so that nobody could say afterwards that a watch had quietly stopped.

---

The second cost of the Thursday did not come from the pump.

It came from a bar.

Somebody — Isel Ferrow, in all likelihood, and he said so himself, twice, and asked for both of them to be written down — put a length of iron in Tams Quill's left hand on the Sunday evening and said, "You are the count and you are the reading-back and you are going to be standing at the frame with Jo every time that hiss is read, and I cannot do the reading-back *and* the frame, and I am asking you to take the bar so that I do not have to."

And Tams Quill held it, and looked at it for a moment, and put it down on the table, and said the thing that he had said in the anteroom in the seventh week and had said twice since, and which Dalla Sen had written down on the first occasion and which the room had heard twice and had never once got used to.

"No," he said. "Not the bar and not the lever and not the chisel and not a face to pack, and I am not saying it because it hurts, because it does not hurt, it is a hand that does two things and one of them is a finger and a half. I am saying it because of what happened on the Thursday.

"When the interval came off early, the first thing that went was the hiss. Not the water, not the wheel, not a person. The *hiss*. And a man at a frame with a bar in his hand does not hear a hiss, and a man at a frame with nothing in his hand does, and if you want to know the first thing that has changed in a machine then you have to have a pair of empty hands.

"I am the one in this building with an empty hand and an ear for a machine, and I am not going to spend that on a bar so that a boy of twenty does not have to do two things. Put the bar down and go and read the plinth, and tell Jo what I tell you, and I will tell you, and then it is the same as if you had heard it and better, because you will have heard it twice."

Isel Ferrow put the bar down. Dalla Sen wrote the whole of it down, in Tams's words, including the part about Isel asking twice, and Tams made her say the part about the bar twice as well, and then asked her to read the whole of it out to the room, which she did.

"It is the eleventh week," Tams said, to nobody in particular, and put his sling back on himself, which nobody offered to do. "It is not this week and I have said it four times in three days and I am going to keep saying it until somebody stops needing to ask."

---

The tally that evening gave the boundary's figure as fifty-eight and the crew's as thirty-eight and a half, and Tams read both and read the finding, which had not changed in three weeks and had got one item longer.

"Fifty-eight and thirty-eight and a half," he said, "and I have stopped dividing it, and I will say the sentence again because it is the sentence: the half that gets counted is the half that is ours.

"And I have got one more item for the *held* column and it is not an object. It is a shut. It is one shut, by the woman from the wash rooms, in her own hand, with the reason, on the clerk's page, in the tenth week, and it is the first time that clause has ever been used for a pump and not for a school bell.

"And I have got one more for the list, and it is the eleventh, and it is not the post, because the post is the tenth and the post knows what it is.

"**The check is a hiss and there is no spare and there is no bronze, and if it goes thin on a morning when the school is running then the boundary shuts its own route and its own school, and neither of those things is anybody's fault, and both of them are going to happen before the cold.**"

Nobody in the anteroom had a better number, and Nera Oduya said so, in her own words, from the valve, and then said the other half.

"And I am going to say the thing that is underneath it, because it has been underneath it for three weeks and nobody has asked me and I would rather somebody asked.

"I took the stair's air out of the certification list. I put a watch on it instead — read at the second bell and the fifth, by a person, out loud, to a person, and not to a page. And I did that because I would not publish a rate for a turn I had seen twice during pump movement, and Oren Sill died inside a plan built on that refusal, and I have not revoked it and nobody has asked me to and I am not going to be asked, because I was right.

"But a watch is the cheapest thing there is, and I chose it, and I have been sitting here for a week noticing what a watch costs when the thing it is watching starts making a noise. It costs a day of school. It costs a shut route. It costs a woman standing at the top of three steps with a reason in her own hand, which is the best thing that happened this week and it cost a day anyway.

"**A watch is not a smaller certificate. A watch is a debt that gets paid in somebody's day.** And I will take that as my own, and I will not have anybody else carry it, and I am not going to change the watch, because the alternative is publishing a number I cannot stand behind and a man going under a lip because of it."
