# Chapter 1 — The Inspection

The changeover bell rang above the East Spur, two notes followed by a hard strike that shook dust from the conduit brackets.

Marek Venn looked up from the lining instead of answering it. The dry service tunnel ran straight ahead for another forty meters, then bent toward the surface under a crown of pale limestone. The public survey said the bend was stable. The stone beneath his palm said otherwise.

He tapped the wall twice with the knuckle of his left hand. The first tap returned flat and dead. The second came back with a hollow note, faint enough that anyone breathing hard might have missed it. Marek shifted his thumb across the brass rule in his other hand. It was a narrow instrument, worn smooth along one edge and split by an old impact near the hinge. His father had carried it through the Sable Cut fourteen years ago. It had come back without him.

The rule tapped against Marek's palm, three uneven beats.

He put it away before he could pretend the tapping meant anything.

“Inspection isn't finished,” he called down the tunnel. “Nobody moves toward the bend.”

Leena Orr stopped beside the survey case and looked at him over the rim of her lamp. She had the compact, unhurried expression she wore whenever an official wanted a number and she intended to give him the number that would make him angry. “The desk says changeover is in four minutes.”

“The desk can wait four minutes.”

“The desk can also make our pay disappear.”

“Then it will have to learn to carry a load.”

That got a short laugh from Pell Roke, the junior route messenger, who was pretending not to listen while he checked the buckle on his courier satchel. He was fourteen and had been told twice that he was not to touch anything. Both instructions had been delivered with the confidence of people who had never watched a fourteen-year-old work.

Iven Tallo crouched at the timber cart, running a thumb along the edge of a wedge. He was the only person in the shift wearing a Cartwright badge, though he had removed the guild's blue cord from his hat when the work turned muddy. “If your wall has a complaint, make it to the survey office. I have a cart to get up the surface grade before the next shift.”

The shift had brought four six-liter skins, a twenty-meter rescue line, two hand lamps, a survey case, timber wedges, grease pencil, chalk, and Marek's folding rule. The water was a working supply, not a luxury. Everyone had been told to bring enough for the full shift because the East Spur had no drinking tap.

“Your cart can wait. The lining is taking load out of sequence.”

Marek pointed to the grease-pencil mark he had made beside the suspicious joint. “There is a mineral seam here. It isn't on the surface plan.”

“There is a mineral seam here,” Iven said. “That doesn't mean it's doing anything.”

“The return is late.”

Iven rose, wiping his hands on his trousers. “The return is always late when the rock is wet. Or when you're listening with your fingers.”

Oren Sill and Tams Quill stood farther down the tunnel with the second chock line stretched between them. Oren had the heavy shoulders of a man who had spent twenty years lifting things other people called temporary. Tams kept one hand on the line and watched the rear of the tunnel, as if the surface might send help down the moment they asked for it.

No one did. The line was their own work, and the tunnel belonged to the Public Works Department until the paperwork said otherwise.

Marek opened the clipboard. Form 7-B was clipped beneath the inspection sheet. The form authorized a partial closure of the unused North Relief Branch. In the small print, the closure would leave the branch's maintenance burden unassigned. A second paragraph allowed the East Spur to reopen under a one-shift clearance.

He had been asked to sign it before the inspection.

The shift supervisor's message had come through the tube at the entrance, compressed into a clerk's neat instructions: *East Spur clear for changeover. Sign 7-B and bring the men up.*

Marek had read it twice. The phrase bring the men up had made the request sound less like an order and more like a favor, which was how supervisors made dangerous paperwork sound whenever they needed a signature.

“Where is the branch problem?” Leena asked.

“Unknown.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It is the answer we have.”

He turned the form so she could see it. “If I sign, the branch stays closed on paper. Nobody owns the repair. Then the East Spur gets a one-shift clearance, which means one shift of people can be sent through a tunnel with an unresolved pressure path.”

Leena's eyes moved over the paragraph. “And if you don't sign?”

“The East Spur stays closed until we can prove the lining is stable.”

“The changeover supervisor will call it delay.”

“The changeover supervisor can call it delay after he explains why he wants a signature.”

“I won't sign 7-B,” Marek said. He set the form back on the clipboard, signature line upward, and looked at the unassigned maintenance clause. “The branch stays closed until someone owns the repair. That is the part I can defend.”

For a moment, the only sound was the faint hiss of the ventilation duct overhead. It pushed warm, iron-tasting air toward them in a steady stream. Marek watched a thread of grit tremble beside the vent collar. The air moved, but the seam in the wall answered with a different rhythm.

He set down the clipboard.

The brass rule was in his left hand now. Its edge caught the lamp light. He could have used it as a level, a scratcher, or a way to make a line on stone. Instead, he held it against the seam and waited. No light appeared in the brass. It gave him only a hard tap beneath his fingers.

Marek knew better than to trust a private sensation in a place built to punish assumptions, but he also knew not to ignore a warning merely because it lacked a certificate.

“All six,” he said. “Back to the inspected shaft. Leave the cart.”

Pell looked toward the bend. “We didn’t even see the bad part.”

“The bad part is enough.”

Iven’s mouth tightened. He did not argue. That was more worrying than an argument. He began pulling the timber cart back, and Oren took the opposite end without being asked. Tams wound the second chock line around his forearm and backed toward the entrance.

Marek went last.

The changeover bell rang again.

This time the sound came from inside the wall.

He stopped. The rule tapped three times, then once, hard enough to make his teeth ache. Dust leaked from the joint above the lining in a thin gray line. The ventilation duct shuddered. Somewhere behind them, the clay insulators on the lamp cable began to pop softly.

“Marek?” Leena said.

He turned.

The floor rose.

It was not a dramatic heave. The stone simply lost its agreement with the horizontal. A joint opened beneath his boot, a cold breath came up through it, and the East Spur tilted by a fraction before the whole tunnel heaved again. The lamp in his hand struck the wall. Glass broke. Someone shouted. The timber cart broke free from Oren and Tams and went down sideways, its wheels spinning against the floor as the slope came alive.

Marek threw himself toward the wall, then away from it when the wall folded inward.

The ceiling came down in pieces.

A slab caught Iven across the shoulders and drove him toward the cart. Oren caught the man’s coat and Tams caught his belt, and the three of them went down together beneath a wave of limestone dust. Marek heard wood split, then Iven’s breath hitch. He had a hand on the nearest upright, trying to keep his footing, when a second slab struck his left side and threw him against the inspection case.

The brass rule was in his hand. He could not remember putting it there.

Leena was shouting Pell’s name.

The boy had stumbled into a coil of the second chock line. One of the lamp brackets had fallen across the passage, and the line had taken his ankle. Pell was trying to pull free while the floor tilted under him. Leena reached him without her lamp, caught the back of his satchel, and hauled.

The movement wrenched through her right shoulder.

The joint made a wet, hard sound. Leena’s face went white. She did not let go of Pell. She pulled him clear as a stone plate came down where his legs had been, then dropped onto one knee with her right arm held against her chest.

“Marek!” she cried.

He got to her by crawling over broken survey boards. The air was thick enough to chew. His left hand found her cheek. Her skin was cold, her breath quick.

“Your shoulder.”

“Later.” She grabbed his sleeve with her left hand. “Pell.”

“I’m here,” Pell choked. “My ankle—”

“Leave it.” Marek’s voice came out harsher than he intended. “Leave the line and crawl toward the lamp.”

Oren and Tams had pulled Iven clear, but the fallen slab had pinned one of Iven’s legs. Iven’s hands clawed at the stone, then at the cart’s broken axle. “Do not put your back under that,” he said through dust. “The lintel is still carrying.”

Marek turned toward the sound. The lintel was not visible, but the pressure had changed. A thin line of dust ran along the floor where the stone had split. The brass rule in his palm jumped, and for an instant the whole tunnel seemed to lean toward a point behind the wall.

Then the seam opened.

Water and fine black grit came through the crack in a hard horizontal jet. It struck the far lining, peeled away a sheet of stone, and left a hole where the wall had been. Beyond the hole was not earth. It was a deep, sloping space, lit by a gray light that had no source Marek could see.

He had time to think that the survey had been wrong.

Then the floor gave way.

He grabbed Leena with his left arm and felt her fingers close around his coat. They went down together through broken stone and old timber. Oren shouted Tams’s name. Tams shouted back. Iven’s voice came from somewhere below, followed by the heavy impact of the cart. Pell’s cry was the last clear sound Marek heard before the tunnel folded shut over them.
