# Chapter 58 — Eleven Feet Down

*On the Sunday morning she was two floors down with a cloth in her left hand and a board under her arm and nothing in the world between her and a hole eleven feet deep, and the four terms of this boundary are written for rooms people argue about, and this is not one. Everything she decided in the first ten minutes, she decided alone, at the top of three steps, with her right hand on a rail.*

---

She had been asked, which is the part she would remember afterwards.

Not volunteered. Asked — by Renn Vale, at the top of the three steps, on the Sunday morning, in the anteroom, in front of Dalla Sen and Bel Ordry, who had the bell that day and who had asked for nothing. And the asking had been in the flat voice, and it had named the day and the hours and the post, and it had said out loud what the post was, and it had said *you may say no, and if you say no I will not ask you again this month*, and Nera had said yes.

Then Dalla Sen had made her answer a second question, which was the valve.

"Because you are the air valve watch," the clerk said, "and the air valve watch does not go to a lip, and if the air valve watch is empty then the work stops and it does not shorten."

"I am taking the board and the cloth with me," Nera said. "The valve is shut and it stays shut and the string is in my left hand, and I will be two floors down with my own watch on me, and I am telling you that now, out loud, in this room, before I go, because it is the first time in eleven weeks that the air valve watch has been anywhere but the anteroom and I am not going to do it in a way that a person finds out at the fourth bell."

"It is written," Dalla Sen said, "in your words, with the time on it, and read back. And I am putting on it, in my own hand, that the air engineer has said this is not to be done again without the air engineer saying so in writing, and that the valve is shut and the stair-side grille is shut and the bypass hatch is latched, and that the water is where it is going to be on a Sunday morning."

Then Renn had written the asking and the answer down in her own hand with the time on it, and had read it back, and had then said: "It is not the stair. It is the lip. You will be at the lip and not on the stone."

Which was the distinction Renn Vale had been making out loud, week after week, in every room that would let her, and which Nera Oduya had privately thought was pedantry until the seventh week, when a turn came two minutes into the nineteenth strokes and a man went under a lip with a bearing hand on it.

So on the Sunday she went down the three steps to the service bend at the first bell with the cloth in her left hand and the board under her arm, and Bel Ordry went with her as the bearer because a bearer at the near lip is a person of this boundary and not a person doing something else, and they stood on the fixed first landing, and Bel Ordry put himself at the lip and did not put a hand on her or on anything, and Nera Oduya put her left boot on the stone and her right hand on the rail.

The rail was on the far side. The rail was four feet away over the throat and she was not going near it, and the near landing's lip was on her right and the throat was under her and the wall she could reach with her free hand was the dressed stone of the throat's near cheek, which had been dry for as long as anyone in this boundary had been alive.

Nera Oduya has been listening to this building for eleven weeks.

That is not a figure of speech and it is not a talent. It is a set of ears and a board and a set of habits she has built because she came out of a place where people died of the air and she has not been able to stop doing it since. She knows the sound the wheel makes at the twenty-sixth stroke and the sound it makes when it is labouring. She knows the sound a check makes at a quarter of pressure and she knows it flat, and she knows the sound of the shaft when the pump is drawing and the sound of it when the pump is off, and the two are not the same sound and she has never written that down on the board because the board is for things other people have to read.

At eleven minutes past the second bell the pump came off the lever, and the shaft changed.

She had heard it change forty-one times before the shore went in, and more than that since, and she had counted them in a column that is not on the board and never will be, because a count of turns is a working number and a working number on a page is a number people plan school days against. Thirty-four of forty-one came between one and nine minutes after the lever came off. She had said that once, out loud, in a full room, in the thirty-sixth day, and had then refused three times to say it again.

She stood at the lip with her hand on the stone and let the shaft change, and the six minutes went by, and the air came up the throat and went past her and out through the school corner's shut mouth, or did not, and then the wheel started again and the shaft went the other way and pulled, and everything below her began, very slightly, to come up toward the anteroom.

And at about the second minute of the nineteen strokes, from eleven feet down, something in the shaft made a sound.

---

It was not a drip and it was not a shift of stone and it was not the water.

It was a flat, dry, wooden knock with a slide on the end of it, the sound a bar of iron makes in a staple, or a finger makes on the edge of a cupboard, or a hand makes on a door frame when somebody in the next room is leaning on a door and lets go of it.

Nera Oduya did not move. She stood on the fixed first landing with her right hand on the rail and her heart going like a bird's and she counted, because counting is what she has instead of fear, and she counted the time from the sound to the moment she was certain she had not imagined it: about four seconds of breath.

Then the shaft did the thing it does when something has moved in it. The air came up past her in a slow, cold push that had a shape to it, like a door opening somewhere below and the room behind it breathing out.

And then nothing, for the rest of the nineteen strokes, and then the interval, and then the wheel.

And then, on the second minute of the six, the turn.

She knew that sound. It is the sound of a stair coming back on a line, and she heard it, and Bel Ordry heard it too and put his hand flat on the cord because he is a person who has been told what a cord is for, and Nera Oduya said the word that the terms give her, which is *out*, and the two of them were up the three steps in under a minute with the board and the cloth and nothing else. Nobody was on the stair in the six minutes after the lever came off, and they were not on it, and the terms held, and the terms are the only reason that was a sentence instead of a scramble.

And the other half of it, the part she did not understand until the Tuesday, was that a person standing at the lip of an eleven-foot shaft is standing a long way closer to a turn than a person standing at the top of three steps is, and the thing that made the sound in the first place was eleven feet down and not here, and she got both of those free on the Sunday morning and did not know she was being paid in them.

She stood at the top of the three steps for the rest of the interval and then went up to the anteroom and washed her hands for a long time.

---

The two things on her board that she could not account for had both got a little worse that morning, and she looked at them in front of eleven people, which she had not done in nine weeks and had thought about doing every day of the nine.

The board is small and it is hers and it hangs behind the valve, and half of it is the anteroom's rating, which has not moved, and the other half is four gallery cards in a row and the stair's watch, and in a corner of it there is a column with no heading at all, and in that column she has been keeping two marks for a fortnight, and one of the two is the same shape as the reading that came up under the school corner floor in the thirty-seventh day, and the other one is a shape she has never seen, and it is on a Thursday.

"Two things," she said, "and I am going to say them in the order I have believed them.

"The first is a sound. At the second minute of the nineteen strokes on the Sunday morning, at the top of three steps, from the shaft, about eleven feet down, I heard a wooden knock with a slide on the end of it. A bar in a staple. A hand leaving a door frame. I have been in this building eleven weeks and I have heard that shaft empty for eleven weeks, and this is the first time anything in it has made a sound that was not water and not stone.

"The second is the air. When it came, it came up past me in a push, and a push has a direction and a width, and this one came out of the north-east cheek of the throat at about the height of my knee, which is where the air would come out of a doorway and not out of a wall. I have no instrument for the width. I have my hands and I have eleven weeks and I am telling you that I do not have an instrument for the width."

Nobody spoke, which is what she had wanted and also what frightened her.

"So here is what I am going to say, and I am saying it before anybody asks me, because I have watched this room spend nine weeks finding out what I think at the same time as it finds out that it is true.

"**I am not going down that shaft. Not this month, not for a sound, not for a door. And I am not publishing the number of turns, and I am not taking the watch off the stair, and I am not certifying the air in that shaft or anywhere under this boundary, and the reason for all four of those is the same one and it is not caution.**

"There is a door down there. I cannot see it, I cannot measure it, and I cannot get to it. Everything I have ever certified in this building, I certified by standing in the room. That is the whole of my method. I did not learn it in a school, I learned it in a place where a room that nobody stood in killed thirty people, and I have never once certified a room I have not stood in, and I am not starting at the bottom of a hole because a boy with a good ear and I both heard something in the dark.

"And if there is a door down there — and I have said *if*, and I am going to keep saying *if* — then the last thing this boundary needs before the cold is a person on a rope going to look at a door, and the first thing it needs is somebody in a room with a bell, a shelf, and a plan of what the air is doing."

She took the cloth off its hook and hung it up again.

"So the terms of what I have just said are these, and I want them on the page and I want them read.

"One: the post under the near landing's lip is scoped to what can be seen from the fixed first landing. Nobody goes under that stone. There is a shore in it and the shore is packed with a knee and the packing is a person and that person stands on the fixed landing, not under the lip, and I will not have a plan that puts anybody's head under that stone.

"Two: the four treads behind the rail on the far side are not walked this month. They were found in the ninth week by a person who was not asked, and they are dry, and the receiving landing's floor is gone, and there is no rail on them and no light and no second ladder in this building, and I have read the air for eleven weeks and I will not put a person on them with what I have.

"Three: the throat's north-east cheek is to be sounded, not entered. Once a day, at the second bell, by a person who is not going down anything, with a bar, by hand, and the answer is written down whatever it is, including *nothing*, and I will hold the bar myself if nobody else will do it, and I would like somebody else to do it, and I am asking for that out loud and in front of these people so that it is an asking and not a preference.

"Four: the review line is brought forward. It was the ordinary one. It is now the first work-bell after any one of three things — a sound from the throat, a thin hiss, or anybody in this boundary writing the word *out* on that slate. I am not asking for the long review. I am asking for it sooner, and I have given the room the three words so that nobody has to be brave about it at two in the morning."

---

The room took it apart for two days, which is what the room does, and which is why she had said it in that order.

Renn Vale asked the practical question at about the fourth bell on the Monday, standing on the common landing, and it was the best question anybody asked and it was not about doors.

"Nera," she said, "what would a door be *doing* down there."

And Nera said, "What would any of us be doing down there. It is eleven feet and it is a room under a boundary that has been lived in, and there are pegs in a wall in a flooded gallery and a shaft with a chamber at the bottom of it, and I do not know what the room is for, and a door in a room is there because people walk in and out of it."

"Then somebody walks in and out of it," Renn said, "and there is nobody down there."

"No," Nera said. "There is no one down there. I want to be exact, because this room will get this wrong by accident. There is nobody down there. I have no way to know that, and I have no way to know it in the way I can know it in a room, and I am not going to say the sentence that would make people stop asking, and I am not going to say the other sentence either. What I am going to say is that in eleven weeks, with a pump running twenty-six strokes out of every forty and a bell at the threshold and a person on the landing, there has not been one sign of a person. And I would like somebody to notice how long that sentence is, and to notice that I am the one who can only say it in eleven weeks."

---

Talla Roke said the other thing, and she said it on the second afternoon, from the bench end with her back to the wall where a courier sat, and she had been in this building five days and had slept four times in the school store and had already begun, without anybody noticing, to be a person who was told things.

"A latch," she said. "That is a door with a bar on it. And a bar on a door is a door somebody comes and goes through, and the only reason anybody builds a door in a plan is that somebody walks in and out of the room it is in.

"So here is the thing and I am the only person in this room who has carried plans. The plans I have carried off the middle gate's shelf are drawn like houses. A stair. A room. A thing marked with a line that means a level. **Doors drawn on hinges, with the swing marked.** Every one of them. And a plan with a door drawn on hinges is a plan somebody drew while the door still worked, which means the plan was drawn by somebody who was *standing in a building like that and looking at it*, and not by somebody who was guessing where to put a store.

"And this boundary has a door that still works, eleven feet down, with a bar on it, and nobody knows what it opens into. And a school store with nine boxes in it and a plan on a dowel. And the whole of that is on the same list of things nobody has opened, and I have been in this building seven days and I have counted four of them, and I have been a courier for four years and I have never once been in a room where four things nobody had opened were all about the same subject."

Dalla Sen wrote it down and read it back, and Talla Roke made her read the last sentence twice, and then said: "Now put a line under it that says who wants the boxes open, and put a line under that that says who wants the door opened, and then somebody in this room is going to have to notice that those are two different people and one of them is in this room and one of them is forty days away."

---

And in the anteroom on the Wednesday morning, Marek Venn stood at the bench with the tally board in his left hand, and on the shelf above the bench, where the bench was needed and the board was the record, there was a knotted bundle of work cloth with the corners tied off by somebody else's hand, and it had been on that shelf for eleven weeks and it had not said one word about a door eleven feet below the floor he was standing on.

He did not take it out. He put his hands flat on the bench, one on either side of where the shelf was, and he waited for about as long as he waited the first time in the seventh week, and nothing came, and he took his hands off the bench, and did not write *null* on anything, because there was nothing to write it on and he knew what it would say.

The brass survey rule was in the other pocket and had not said a word since the sixth week and did not say one now, and there was nobody left in the building who would ever be able to tell him whether that was restraint or luck.

At the top of nine steps, a boy with a splinted ankle and a crutch across his knees put his ear against the stone of the step above the stair head, and heard the wheel, and called the interval eight seconds early in a voice that did not carry and did not need to, and did not know anything at all about a door, and would not have cared, because he was the count, and a count was the only thing in that room that was never wrong.
