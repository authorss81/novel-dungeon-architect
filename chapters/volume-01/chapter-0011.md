# Chapter 11 — The Room That Holds the Terms

Tams kept the timber lever moving, but the delivery check no longer sounded like a single hard report. It chattered in a ragged double beat, each knock throwing a little vibration through the grate. Nera watched the dyed cloth over the air valve. Sefu watched the water mark beside the sill. The sound was small enough that a tired person could ignore it and large enough to change what everyone else did.

“Stop there,” Nera called.

Tams eased the lever back. Oren took the weight without being asked, and the pump settled into its frame with a final knock from below. The anteroom became quiet except for twelve sets of breathing and the faint hiss of air through the outer door.

Marek had thought the test would give them time. Instead, it had given them a pump that could not be left alone and a room whose two-hour rating had begun with the first person who entered it.

The twelve-person test was over. Sefu pointed to the upper landing and asked Renn and Mikka to step there while Nera reset the air record. A fresh working interval began with the six-person crew, Nera, Sefu, Dalla, and Jo: ten occupants, one of them on a litter. Renn and Mikka could speak from the landing, but they did not count toward the room until a named return was recorded. The courier stayed on the inspection side of the incline, outside the rated anteroom. Dalla marked the interval’s start and its two-hour limit on the route sheet.

Leena was still on the litter. Her shoulder had held through the test, but the rest of her had not. Color had drained from her face, and she kept her left hand braced beneath the sling as though the weight of the cloth had grown.

“Where do I recover?” she asked.

Sefu looked toward the bench. “There is dry floor.”

“A bench is not a bed,” Leena said. “And I do not intend to spend the night learning whether that distinction matters.”

Renn Vale set her lantern on the landing floor and inspected the anteroom threshold. Jo Verrick brought over a low canvas camp cot, its legs bound with waxed cord. Mikka Hale followed with a folded blanket and a stoppered cup of dark water from somewhere beyond the gate, then stopped at the threshold. The cup was not from the crew’s reserve. Its rim had the pale mineral line of a Hearthhold vessel.

“May I?” Jo asked Leena.

“Help me sit up, then ask me where the cot goes.”

Jo and Oren took opposite sides of the litter. Iven guided Leena’s legs while she kept her right arm tight against her chest. With her left hand, she pointed to a strip of floor between the bench and the wall, away from the service well and the outer-door swing. They lowered the litter, then shifted her onto the cot without putting weight through the right shoulder.

Marek had a dozen instructions ready. Leena looked at him until he put them away.

“Thank you,” she said.

Mikka placed the cup beside the cot. “Small sips. That water is for recovery, not a full ration.”

“We will not take from the crew’s reserve without record,” Marek said.

“The reserve stays yours,” Mikka replied. “This is one cup from a kitchen that has other water. Do not turn gratitude into a debt.”

He nodded, surprised by how quickly she had named the thing he had not yet considered.

The courier moved beside the inspection incline. He had remained quiet after the test, but the locked surface door was only a few steps away, and Dael Harrow’s order still lay open on the bench between the old seal and the unsigned custody line.

“There is one further consequence,” the courier said. “The order freezes guild-financed equipment and restricts use of the licensed line pending hearing. Iven Tallo’s maintenance signature does not turn continuing pump work into private custody.”

Iven looked up from the service well. “No one has claimed the line.”

“The pump is linked to it through the service works.”

“Water and stone are linked. That does not make either a Cartwright door.”

“The order reaches associated works where guild material has been used.”

Iven wiped his hands on his trousers. “The gland seal and key came from the Hearthhold service canister. The lever timber is the same. No guild-funded part passed that gate.”

“The hearing may ask how an unlicensed repair crew came to occupy a maintained route.”

Marek felt the sentence tighten around the room. It was vague enough to be useful to Dael and narrow enough not to state a fact. He could answer it, but the answer would put Iven’s signature at the center again.

“If the order needs a charge, let it name the charge,” Marek said. “It locks your door. It does not lock this pump.”

Sefu placed one hand on the civic wheel. “Nor does a physical connection give Dael Harrow authority inside this boundary.”

Nera added, “And it does not change who may operate the air gate.”

The courier inclined his head. “I am not saying the order reaches the gate. I am saying the hearing will examine the relationship between the line, the repair, and the people remaining here.”

“Then it can examine the maintenance sheet,” Iven said. “That is what I signed. Not custody.”

Marek watched Iven square his shoulders. The answer had come quickly, but his injured pride was not the only thing behind it. If the guild called his labor guild custody, the work would survive and he might not. Marek thought he was choosing a cost he could name over a danger that would arrive wearing his name.

Dalla Sen closed the repair ledger on her thumb. “The test observers are finished.”

Everyone looked at her.

Renn lowered the lantern. Jo stopped fussing with the camp cot. Mikka had one hand on the folded blanket.

Marek had been thinking of the four residents as a single consent. The word *volunteers* had kept them distinct in his notes, but the room had blurred them into one useful body whenever the pump made a noise.

“Which part?” he asked.

“Standing here,” Dalla said. “Renn and I entered to test the room. Jo and Mikka carried tools and canvas because we needed them at the time. None of us agreed to keep a shift.”

“No one asked you to,” Marek said.

“You asked Sefu how long the test gave you. You did not ask what happens after the test.”

“No. I should have.”

The admission did not repair the omission, but it gave the conversation a true place to begin.

Sefu released the civic wheel and stood beside it. “My authority is at this boundary. I can keep the deeper gate shut. I can refuse passage across it. I cannot make Renn, Dalla, Jo, or Mikka work a pump, sleep beside a stranger, or give their rooms to a route charter. I also cannot speak for every Hearthhold resident.”

Dalla looked up from the ledger. “And if the four of us agree?”

Sefu turned the wheel a quarter turn and let it come to its stop against the pawl. “Then it binds your work and your rooms. It does not bind the people who did not volunteer, and it does not bind me out of the right to shut a gate I believe will fail.”

Nera stood beside the air valve. “And I can say when the mechanism is safe for the conditions I test. I cannot turn that answer into permission from the residents.”

Iven looked down at the pump. “The guild can lock its own door, deny its parts, or challenge my bond. It cannot write the local shift for you.”

The distinctions had been made before. Now the room was forcing them to act like distinctions.

Leena shifted on the cot. “What do you need from us while the pump is running?”

Dalla answered first. “A name beside every task. Not ‘the crew.’ A person who starts the pump, a person who watches the water, a person who watches the air, and the person allowed to stop it.”

“I can work the lever,” Tams said.

“I can relieve him,” Oren added. “Pell can call the level from the bench. Iven can say when the check has changed.”

“I cannot go below,” Pell said. “I can call the marks, though. I can keep the time.”

Jo turned the flat file over in her hands. “Someone has to open the delivery check for sediment. That is not a thirty-second job. The housing pinches the wrench, and the retaining bolt is crooked. Whoever does it needs both hands and a third person on the frame.”

“Iven can teach that work,” Marek said.

“To whom?”

He had no answer ready. He had assumed that Iven’s knowledge would pass to whoever inherited the pump. Inheritance was not a shift roster.

Jo folded her arms. “I carried tools into the test. That does not make me a pump keeper. Mikka has the next kitchen work shift. Renn cannot leave the door lantern all day. I have a repair route of my own.”

Dalla wrote in the ledger, making each refusal visible rather than folding it into consent. “Then we do not call a resident shift agreed until a resident maintainer puts a name down.”

Marek turned toward Sefu. “How is deeper admission actually decided?”

Sefu rested both hands on the wheel again. “Room by room and work by work, with the people affected. The boundary keeper may refuse passage. A resident may refuse a stranger in a sleeping room, kitchen, clinic corner, store, or workshop. Air approval remains Nera’s. A useful repair does not purchase the next door.”

“And if every person affected agrees?”

“Then Sefu still has to keep the boundary usable,” Sefu said. “And I can still refuse if the route cannot be kept. Agreement is not a transfer of veto.”

Marek wanted a single procedure, a clean sequence from test to admission that he could follow until the route opened. The Hearthholds offered something less satisfying and more real: a different decision in every room, with the burden placed on the people who would live with it.

Dalla turned the repair ledger so everyone could see a blank page divided into four columns: *work*, *person*, *limit*, *stop authority*. Beside it lay the public maintenance sheet from the test. Marek laid his hand on the old page.

“Record the current route as well as the work,” he said. “Ten people in this working interval: the six of us, Nera, Sefu, Dalla, and Jo. One stretcher. Renn and Mikka are at the landing until their return is recorded. The service bypass from the Cinder Steps. The pump temporary. The air route conditional. The returning stair closed. If we are going to work beside you, the record should show what we believe we are doing.”

“And if your belief is wrong?” Dalla asked.

“Then the next person can correct it.”

Leena lifted her cup with her left hand. “Make room for the corrections.”

Marek moved the old clearance seal away from the maintenance sheet. The documents were easier to confuse when placed together. Seal, lien, insurance notice, custody order: each had its own reach. The Hearthhold shift could not be hidden beneath any of them.

Dalla began a new route record on the back of a spare delivery sheet. Renn checked the outer-door swing and wrote down the landing as the evacuation place. Jo listed the tools above the grate. Mikka added the one recovery cup and the fact that it was Hearthhold water, not crew reserve. Nera wrote the air limits and her independent stop authority. Iven entered the pump’s temporary condition and the fact that the lower cache remained under the Hearthhold inventory.

Sefu did not sign the page as owner of the route. He wrote only the boundary terms: no deeper passage, no unlisted occupancy, no night use of the tested room until another review.

Marek was the last to add anything.

He wrote the six outsider names. Below them, he added: *present for attended maintenance only; no civic admission and no claim to the route.*

“The start time is in the margin,” Leena said. “Add the review time.”

Marek looked down.

“And the person who can stop a shift,” she added.

He left the lines open. They were not a clean agreement, but the gaps showed where people had refused to be used as assumptions.

Dalla turned the ledger so the residents could see it. “Before anyone asks about the rooms beyond the gate, give us a named shift and a route record we can correct. Then we will decide whether staying long enough to keep that shift belongs in the rooms.”

The pump ticked below the grate, waiting.

For the first time since the anteroom test, the room’s next permission was not a number.

It was a list of people who had agreed to be there.
