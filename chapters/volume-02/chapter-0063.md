# Chapter 63 — Thinner

The cold came into the anteroom on a Monday and by the Wednesday the check was hissing at a pitch that made everybody in the room look at Jo Verrick, and Jo Verrick put her hand flat on the frame and held it there and let them all wait.

"Hear that," she said.

The room heard it. It was the same hole it had always been, and the sound came out of it, and it was thinner than it had been on Friday.

"Thin," said Ilsa Norr.

"No," Jo said. "Say what you are hearing and do not put a word on it. I have got one word and I do not want it used in a room that has not earned it."

She kept her hand on the frame for another eight seconds, and then she took it off and wiped it down the front of her apron and stood looking at the middle of the floor the way she looked at things that had stopped being a problem and become a fact.

"Right," she said. "Here it is, and I am saying it at the second bell with a room full and I am not saying it at the frame where it would be smaller.

"**I cannot tell any more.** That is the whole of it. I can no longer tell a thin hiss from a full hiss, and I have been telling this room to listen for exactly that difference since the tenth week, and I have been doing it out loud, and I did it in front of eleven people, and I did not say — because I did not know, because nobody in this building has ever been here in the cold — that **a hole hisses thinner in cold air.** Of course it does. The air is heavier. It comes through the same hole under more pressure and it makes a smaller noise. That is not the seat. That is December.

"So the note I gave this room is dead, and it died the way an instrument dies, which is not suddenly. It died on Saturday and I did not notice until Wednesday morning and I have been standing at this frame every day since Saturday and I heard it change three times and I told myself it was the seat, because the seat is what I have been telling them, and the seat is not what I was hearing."

Somebody said something at the back and it was not a question and it did not need to be.

"I am not taking the pump out of service," Jo said. "I want that said before anybody offers it, because somebody will offer it in about an hour and it will be a well-meant offer and it will be wrong. If the pump is off, that dip fills, and I have said that sentence in this room before, and the corridor's low end is under it, and the corridor's low end is two foot eight and stands on the shaft. So the pump runs.

"And from today the pump runs **attended.** Not the usual. Two people at the frame and one of them with nothing in their hands, and the draw is not taken on the note, because the note is nothing, and it is taken on the water in the dip, which I can see with my own eyes from the frame and which I have been seeing with my own eyes for eleven weeks and which I stopped looking at the moment I got a check."

---

"Attended means attended," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel, "and I am not going to have the word used loosely in a room where a woman has just lost an instrument. Isel."

"Isel Ferrow, at the frame, from the second bell to the fifth, and I will say the same thing every morning and I will say what Jo says and not what I think."

"He has the shutter route," said Mikka Hale, from the door.

"He has the shutter route," Jo agreed, "which pays him, and he is going to lose a day of it standing here with his hands empty watching a dip, and I am telling him now and I am telling him in front of Dalla so that it is in the book in the same line as the estimate and not three weeks later in his mouth."

"Then somebody else has the shutter route today," said Mikka, "and I will do it, and it is a half-day and I will do the half, and Jo, you are not going to argue with me about it, because I am not doing it for you and I would like that on the page too."

---

The air engineer arrived at it the way she always does, from a completely different direction, and made it look as though she had been walking toward it the whole time.

"You are all looking at the pump," said Nera Oduya, "and you should be looking at the shaft, and I want to say what I found on Monday and have not said since because I was waiting to see whether it was still true on Wednesday, and it is."

She had the board under her arm and she did not put it on the bench. She held it the way she held it when she was about to say something she had checked twice.

"Everything I taught this room about that stair, I taught in September, in the warm, and I taught it as though it were a law and it is not a law, it is a description of a warm month. In the warm, the shaft is a chimney. The pump comes off the lever, the air in the shaft is warm and thin, and it goes *up*, and it comes up past the top of three steps and out into the anteroom, and everybody in this room has felt it and called it the stair breathing out, and it has been true every day for nine weeks.

"It is not true now. Cold air is heavy. The shaft is eleven feet deep and it is four degrees and the air in the bottom of it wants to stay in the bottom of it. On Monday morning at the first bell I put the dyed cloth on a stick at the top of three steps and it did not lift. It **sank.** It went down the stair and it lay down against the wall of the throat and it stayed there for a count and a half, and then it came back up, slowly, like something being let go of.

"So: for six minutes out of every forty, that shaft is not a vent. It is a **sink.** And what is in the bottom of it is the same wet mineral the pump has been drawing out of the sump since the seventh week, and the pump holding the dip down is the same pump Jo cannot hear."

"Then what has changed for the stair," said Renn Vale, from the upper landing, and there was something in her voice that had not been in it on Monday.

"Nothing has changed for the stair," Nera said. "That is the sentence I have come down here to say and I want it said clearly, because in about an hour this room is going to get frightened and frightened rooms invent rules. **The stair's terms are unchanged, and the terms were written by a keeper who wrote them to be the worst case.** Four at a time, four minutes, nobody on the stair in the six minutes after the lever comes off. I have watched that six minutes for eleven weeks and it has been the quiet part. It was never a safe part. It was the part where nothing moves because the shaft is breathing out. And now nothing moves because the shaft has stopped, and those are not the same nothing.

"So the terms hold. And I am not publishing a rate, and I am not taking the watch off, and I am not certifying that shaft in this cold, and I have not been asked to and I would have said no."

"Then what do we do differently," said Isel Ferrow, "at the second minute of the six, that we did not do in the warm?"

And Nera Oduya, who has refused to publish a rate for eleven weeks and has a debt on her own board that she claims out loud, said the answer in one sentence and it was the worst sentence anybody in that anteroom had heard since a man went under a lip.

"**Somebody stands at the low end of the corridor and watches the water, because the water is the only instrument left and I do not have a person for it.**"

---

The room did what rooms do, which is to go quiet and then to become extremely interested in a different question.

Nera did not let it.

"Before anybody says *whose*, I will say what I know, and then I will not say it again this week. It is the woman from the wash rooms or it is nobody. It is not Mikka, because Mikka has the clinic return and the frame and a pot and a flue wall she chose to watch, and a person doing three things is doing none of them. It is not Ilsa, because Ilsa is counting nine children at the head of a crossing. It is not Renn, and I am not going to explain why and nobody is going to ask me.

"And it is not the five of you, and I am not being kind about it. You are not on the rota and you cannot be, and I said that out loud on the first day of this and I have said it four times since and I am saying it a fifth time on a morning when it would be very convenient not to."

Nobody in the room said *I will do it*, which is the correct thing, and which Dalla Sen entered in her own hand with the time on it, and which was the first time in eleven weeks that a post in that building had been offered to nobody.

---

Bel Ordry had the bar against the throat's north-east cheek at the second bell on Wednesday, and he was still there at a quarter past it, and when he came up the nine steps into the anteroom his hands were white to the second knuckle and Dalla Sen had the ledger open before he had got his coat off.

"I have got a sound," he said.

Dalla Sen's pen did not move.

"Not — no. I want it in the right order. I have got a sound and I cannot tell you what it is, and I want that written down first, before I tell you what it might be."

"Go on."

"Bar on the wall, same place, same time, same hand, fourth morning running. On Monday it was nothing. Tuesday nothing. Wednesday there was a sound, and I will tell you the shape of it and then I will tell you the problem with it. The shape is a tick. Short, dry, out of the stone, up about the height of my chest, and it came three times with a space between, and the third one had a slide on it like a hand leaving a door frame."

The room stopped.

"That is the knock," said Talla's voice, and then stopped, because Talla Roke was four hundred miles away and there was nobody in the anteroom who had said it.

"That is the shape of the knock," said Bel Ordry. "That is not what I am stuck on. What I am stuck on is this: **it is four degrees in the throat and the bar is cold and the wall is cold, and when a cold bar goes against cold stone, the stone ticks.** I have put a cold hand on a cold wall every morning this week and it ticks. I have done it in three places on this cheek where I know the stone and it ticks in all of them. I have got a bar and a wall and a cold morning and I have got a sound that could be a door eleven feet down and it could be a stone that has a crack in it and it could be nothing at all, and I am not going to write down *knock* and I am not going to write down *nothing*, because either one of those is a thing I do not know and this room has spent eleven weeks teaching me not to do that."

Nera Oduya put both hands flat on the valve housing and left them there.

"Then we write the shape," she said. "The shape, and the cold, and the three places it ticks, and the fact that it was a cold bar on a cold wall, and the fact that Bel Ordry has stood at that wall four mornings running and has written down three nothings and one thing he cannot classify, and **no word, and no classification, and no conclusion, in either direction.**

"And I want the next thing said in this room about this, so that it is on the page before somebody decides it, and here it is: **on Wednesday the instrument stopped being an instrument.** On Monday it was a bar and a wall and an answer. On Wednesday it is a bar and a wall and a man in a cold corridor telling us he does not know. I do not know whether that is a watch or a diary. I have been refusing to publish a rate for eleven weeks on the grounds that a rate I cannot stand behind is a rate that kills somebody, and this morning the thing I refused to publish is the thing that would have told me whether a wall is talking, and so for one morning in the twelfth week my own refusal and my own method are standing on opposite sides of the same wall, and I am not going to pretend that is a small thing to sit in a room with."

"Then what do we do," said Bel Ordry.

"You keep doing it," Nera said. "That is the whole of what I have. Four mornings of a man and a bar and an answer that is sometimes nothing and this week was not nothing and might be a crack. **You keep doing it and you write down what you have and not what you think it is, and on the morning it is a knock you will be the only person in this building who can say that he wrote down nothing for thirty-one mornings before it, and that is worth more than a rate, and it will be worth more than a rate for as long as we are all still here.**"

And Bel Ordry said, "Then it cost me a day," and Nera said, "Say it to the clerk and not to me, because I have no column for it and I have never had a column for anything."

"It cost me a day," Bel Ordry said, to Dalla Sen. "I was going to fix my mother's door. It is not a fix, it is taking a hinge off and putting it on the other side, and I have had the hinge in a bag for six weeks because a man does not have a Saturday in this building, he has whatever day he can get, and this week I got the cold morning and I spent it at a wall."

"Wrote," said Dalla Sen.

---

Marek Venn went down to the fixed first landing in the afternoon of the second day, inside the terms, with the terms read at the school door, and put his left hand and then his right palm flat on the dressed stone over the lip and did the thing he has done seven times in eleven weeks and has never once been able to describe to another person.

Stone. Cold — and the cold was *in* it, a real cold, a hand's breadth of difference between the stone and his skin that was larger than anything he had ever been trying to feel under it. Damp at the left edge. And under the stone, eleven feet of shaft, and the enormous patience of it.

He waited as long as he had ever waited. Nothing came.

He did not write *null*. He stood on the common landing with the chalk in his left hand and understood, with a clarity that was almost restful, that he was not going to write it, and that the reason was a fact about the instrument and not about the target.

A null is a null. Tams was right about that, and Tams would insist on it, and Tams would be right to insist, because a column that has a hole in it is worse than a column that is honest. But a null in the cold is not the same measurement as a null in the warm. It is a different instrument reading a different room. In September his hand was a hand and the stone was a stone and the difference between them was a small warm thing he was listening past. Now the difference between his hand and the stone was the loudest thing in the room, and any reading he got would be about his hand and not about the lip.

He came up the nine steps and asked Dalla Sen for the tally board, and Tams Quill gave it to him across the bench without a word, and Marek stood at the end of it with the chalk in his left hand for about a minute and a half.

"Put a column on it," he said. "Empty. And put a one in it, and put the date and the hour and the temperature of the room, which is Nera's, and do not put it in the nulls."

"That is a null," said Tams Quill.

"That is a null in a different instrument, and if I put it in your column then in the spring somebody will come along and read eleven readings in a column and work out a rate, and there is no rate, and I have spent eleven weeks writing the word *null* in a column in a book that people believe, and I am not putting a number in it that I know is a lie about what I was listening to."

Tams Quill looked at the board for a while with his right hand in his lap and the other one flat on the wood.

"Right," he said. "A column with no heading, and a one in it, and the temperature, and the reason in your words under it, and the reason is going to be that you could not hear past your own hand, and that is the first thing anybody has ever entered on that board that is a fault in the reader and not a fault in the building." He wrote it. "And I am going to say the other half, because a room that only writes down the half it likes is the guild, and it is not going to be the guild. **A null in the cold is worth more than a null in the warm and I want it counted twice.**"

---

The review was called at the first work-bell on Thursday, which is the first of the three things Nera Oduya named in the tenth week, and the first of the three was a sound from the throat, and it was the first time in eleven weeks that the short review had ever been called.

"It is not the long one," she said. "It is the one at the first work-bell, and it is a working morning, and here is what is on it and I am listing it out loud before anybody adds anything to it, because a review that grows is a review that has been planned.

"One. The throat. Bel Ordry's four mornings, three nothings and one thing he cannot classify, and no conclusion in either direction.

"Two. The check. Jo Verrick has lost her instrument and the pump is attended and the note she gave this room is dead in the cold.

"Three. **The six minutes.** The shaft has stopped being a chimney and the terms have not changed and the terms were written for the worst case, and the worst case is now a six minutes in which nothing moves because nothing is moving, and I want somebody in the room to say out loud what happens in the corridor's low end in those six minutes now, and nobody has said it and I have been waiting two days for somebody to say it."

It was Mikka Hale who said it, and she said it flatly, from the frame, with a cloth in her hand, and she said it as though she had been carrying it since Monday and had only now been asked to set it down.

"The water comes up," she said. "In those six minutes the pump is off, and in those six minutes the water in that dip comes up, and the corridor's low end is two foot eight wide and it stands on the edge of the shaft, and in those six minutes the low end of that corridor is filling. That has been true since the seventh week. The difference is that in the warm the six minutes were quiet and everybody was on the stair being quiet, and now the six minutes are the loudest six minutes in the building and the loudness is underneath the floor."

Nobody said anything for a moment, and then the pump came on under the floor, twenty-six strokes, and the anteroom heard it, and the check hissed, and the check was thin.

And Jo Verrick, at the frame, said the thing that the whole of the next three weeks is going to be about.

"Then here is where we are," she said. "The seat goes in the cold. I have said it since the tenth week and I have not moved it. The cold came on Monday and it is here and it is going to be here for five weeks. I have got two cleanings on that seat or fewer, unmoved in eleven weeks. I have got no instrument to tell me where it is up to. I have got no wood in this Hearthhold that a seat can be cut out of, and I have spent a day this week finding that out, and I have been told no four times by better people than me.

"So the seat is going, and the check has gone, and the cold has taken the last thing I had for reading it, and I am standing at a frame at the second bell with my hand on it and **I am going to be able to tell you on the morning it goes and not on the morning before, and that is the position, and it is on the board, and I am not going to move it.**"
