# Chapter 186 — The Work He Turned Down

The first day of the thirty-sixth week is a Monday, and a shut Monday in this building is nine steps, one pump, and a floor that is colder at the kitchen-line end than at the top of it. The school did not run and lost nothing. Six people who came up the middle passage on the second day of last week are still in the building, and on this morning none of them was on the common landing, because Emrys Callow had brought his five up the nine steps at the first bell and set their packs down in a row along the anteroom bench.

That is not where a crew that is working puts its packs. A crew that is working puts them where the foreman can see them and where nobody has to walk round them. A crew that is not working puts them where it can pick them up in one motion, and the whole anteroom knew which of those two things had happened before anybody said a word about it.

The wall is on the other side of the room. It has five and a half shores standing on the common landing side where a district's book says six. A brace of a school's own beech pushes on the same wall from the kitchen-line side, and a line of nothing about two fingers wide runs along the join on that side, and none of the three of those is being looked at by anybody at the first bell this morning.

"**I have said my four days out loud in this room once,**" said Callow, with his hands still behind him, "**and I am not going to say them a second time, and I am going to say the rest of it, and the rest of it is worse.**"

He kept them there, which is a man holding still so that the next sentence comes out of the same place as the last one.

"**My four days ended on the Friday. I stayed on the Saturday because a clerk wrote six terms and read them back to me and I wanted to hear a stranger's mouth do it once. That was a day past my four and I did not ask anybody for it and nobody offered, and I would like that entered, because the next thing I am going to say is going to sound like a man asking for something and I am not.**"

Dalla Sen entered it in her own words, and entered that she had not offered and had not been asked, and the pump got three strokes in while she wrote.

"**There is work at the middle gate,**" Callow went on. "**It came in on the fourth day. Six weeks of shoring on a face that has let go, on a job a week old and going bad in a week. It is paid at a district rate, and the rate is the rate my five are held to, and it is more than they have had in nine months.**"

Nobody spoke, and the pump got two more strokes in, and the light on the flags had not moved off the wall it was on at the first bell.

"**I have not taken it and I am not going to take it this week, and I have been not taking it for four days, and I would like this room to sit with the fact that a foreman of nine years has turned down paid work four days running and cannot give a reason for it and has stopped looking.**"

Bel Ordry came down one step from where he had been standing, which is more than he has done in this room for a month, and he had both hands out in front of him and no page in either of them.

"**That is your sentence and I have been carrying it for a month, and I would like the rest of it,**" said Bel Ordry, still one step down with his hands out and empty.

Callow nodded once. "**You have been arguing for a month that a crew with nothing to do and no pay takes the next work that comes,**" Callow said. "**You said it on the second day of last week, in front of a woman who had just given you her name. And this morning the crew with nothing to do and no pay has turned down the next work that came, in a room, out loud. So you were right about us. I would like that entered too, because I do not think you were right about why.**"

"**I do not know why either,**" said Bel Ordry, still one step down with his hands out and empty. "**I have been in this building thirty-five weeks and I have never once known why a man did not do a thing, and I have spent most of that time assuming it was going to turn up in a week.**"

At the second bell Renn Vale came down the four steps with her slate and stood in front of the party wall, and nobody made a place for her, because a shut morning is hers. She put her ear against the stone on the anteroom side, not the kitchen-line side, and she was still there for about the time it takes to say a sentence, four times over, and then she came back and said what she could hear out loud.

"**Nothing, four times,**" she said, coming back to the trestle. "**And I am going to say the second half of it, because I said in this room on Saturday that I would say what I can hear and would not know what it is about and would not pretend to. The second half is that I have now said *nothing* in this room on the first bell of a day and on the second bell of six days, and *nothing* is a thing I am going to keep saying, and a person who says nothing out loud every morning is a person nobody can check.**"

"**Then somebody checks you,**" said Dalla Sen, chalk up and not writing yet. "**I enter every morning what you say you can hear, including the nothing. I am entering this one and I am entering it as a figure of nothing and not as a word, and I do not know what a clerk enters for that and I am going to find out by doing it.**"

Renn Vale looked at her for a long moment, and then went and picked the slate up off the trestle where she had left it. "**Enter it,**" she said. "**And if it turns out I have been saying nothing into a book for thirty-five weeks then that is a thing about the book and not about me, and I would rather have it written down than be pleasant about it.**"

Then the cook came to the kitchen-line doorway with the cloth over her arm, and she had not come further into the room all morning, and she gave the arithmetic, which she says.

"**Seven days,**" said Mikka Hale, and did not come through the doorway. "**And the seam gave me nothing on Thursday, nothing on Friday, and one on Saturday. The seam is at four threads and I am not going to stand in this doorway and put a number on how fast that is going, because I have been asked to be careful with that pot four times this week by four different people, and four times I have been careful, and four times the answer has been the same, which is that I do not know.**"

She turned the cloth over once and did not put it down.

"**Here is the other half and I am saying it before anybody says it for me. Seven days, and six people, and the six of them have not eaten out of that pot since the second day of last week, because I have not given them any and I have been finding them things, and there is nothing left to find them. So I have made a decision and I made it on Friday night, and I have not told anybody, and I am telling a room now, and I want it written in the space where a reason goes and not in a column.**"

"**The decision, then,**" said Renn Vale, from four steps down and not going up them. "**And what it costs. And do not let anybody do the second one for you.**"

Mikka Hale took the cloth off her arm and folded it in half and then did not fold it again.

"**They eat here from today. And I decided that alone, and nobody asked me and nobody is going to, and I am telling a room about it because a cook who decides six men's mouths on her own for six days is an office, and I have been an office since Friday and I did not notice it until this morning.**"

"**Then you have done what I did on Saturday, one day later and about a different thing,**" said Renn Vale, and let it stand there for a pump stroke before she finished it. "**I would like a person in this room to notice that two women in it have decided something this week that neither of them can be asked about by anybody, and that neither of us has a page, and that this is a week and not a coincidence.**"

Then the cook gave the number and would not give the word.

"**Seven with six more is four. That is the arithmetic and I am saying it in the middle of a morning, and Dalla is going to put it next to the thing about the road, because the road is four days, and I am not going to say anything about that and I would like her not to either.**"

Dalla Sen wrote nothing for a moment, which everybody in the room noticed and nobody remarked on.

"**Entered, and entered that the cook asked me not to,**" she said. "**Entered that the road to the middle gate is four days and that the pot is four days. And entered that I am putting the two figures next to each other with nothing between them for the third time in this volume, and that I am not entering what they are.**"

"**That is a clerk managing a record,**" put in Bel Ordry from the step.

"**That is a clerk who has been asked by a cook in front of a room,**" said Dalla Sen, chalk down again, "**and I am entering the fact that I was asked, because a clerk who does not enter being asked has entered something else.**"

Marek Venn had been at the end of the trestle since the first bell with his own book shut under his forearm, doing the thing he had got good at last week, which was standing in a room and wanting to be the man in it. By the third stroke of the pump he had a sentence ready about four days and a pot and a road, and he was not going to say it.

What he had instead, and kept, was smaller. A cook had decided six men's mouths alone on Friday night and had carried it two days without telling anybody, and this morning she had told a room and asked to be left alone about it, and the reason she asked to be left alone was not confidence. It was that a person who has been asked to be careful with a thing four times in a week has learned what happens to the person who is careful. He knew that arithmetic. He had watched a man teach it to a boy on a landing in the ninth week of this book and had not understood it for a year.

The cost of the cook's decision was not the pot. It was that she had said *nobody is going to ask me* out loud, in a room, to a man with no post who has spent thirty-five weeks waiting to be asked for something, and she had meant it kindly, and he was going to have to do the rest of the week without it.

Then Iven Tallo came off the anvil end and put the chisel down, which is what he does before he says a thing he means, and his right hand was at his side and his left was open and flat against his thigh.

"**There is work in this building nobody has asked anybody for,**" he said, not looking at the anvil. "**Two hundred and fifty-seven hours in that column since the keeper signed, and not one hour of it is for a piece of timber. A crew of six has stood on a landing for six days reading a wall, and not one hour of theirs is in that column either, and the number that would go in it if anybody asked is the one number in this room that both of those facts add up to.**"

"**Then nobody is asking,**" said Dalla Sen, and left the sentence where it lay.

"**The half against it, and it is mine, and I am tired of giving it,**" said Iven Tallo. "**A room which has taken a stranger's arithmetic into its own mouth this morning has started to keep two sets of books, and the second set is a cook's, and a cook's arithmetic is the only arithmetic in this building nobody has ever been able to check. She is right that seven with six more is four. I believe her. And I would rather be hungry for a week than find out in a month that I was hungry for the wrong reason, and I am not going to stand in this room and pretend those are the same thing.**"

"**They are not the same thing,**" said Mikka Hale from the doorway, "**and you are the first person in this room in a month who has said so to my face, and I would like the clerk to enter that a carpenter said it and not a cook.**"

"**Nobody is asking this morning,**" said Renn Vale, and came up one of the four steps and then stopped as though she had not. "**The reason goes in the book, and the reason is that a cook has told a room she decided six men's mouths on her own, and a foreman has told a room he turned down paid work for four days, and a clerk has entered that a road and a pot are both four days. This room is not in a condition to hand a fourth thing of that shape to five people before the sixth bell.**"

"**I will put the reason in the book and I will say that it is the fourth morning-specific reason in six days and that none of them is the same as the last one,**" said Dalla Sen, and wrote it, and the seventy-one on the crew did not move and a clerk who is going to be asked about it in a year will find the reason four lines down from a pot.

Nella Bray had been at the kitchen-line doorway since the second bell with a slate under her arm that she had not written on, and she now put the slate against the doorframe and gave the room the thing she had been carrying since the second day of last week.

"**There is a version of this room where a clerk is given a number of what is in it,**" she said. "**I have stood at a counter for nineteen years. I have never been sent one. If a room like this existed on paper there would be a figure on it, and it would be a figure of what the room is permitted, and it would be called a permitted occupancy, and the woman in this building has been saying for thirty-five weeks that she does not have that and has not got the other kind either.**"

"**And if a room like this existed,**" said Bel Ordry, off the step again, "**the number would be in a column and the column would have a name over it, and the woman you are describing has spent thirty-five weeks refusing to head a column.**"

"**Then we are two people who would both refuse it,**" said Nella Bray, "**and that is the first pleasant thing anybody has said to me about this building, and I would like it to stop.**"

"**Then here are the anteroom's terms, and a person is saying them,**" said Renn Vale, "**and I am saying them on a shut morning in a room with six strangers in it, which is a term a room has to say out loud and not a term it can stop saying.**"

The count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change. The outer service door was the one a body went out of. The fifth of those six terms was in a room with both the people it names in it. The pump was attended. Dalla Sen repeated all six and the room let her, and nobody read the fifth out loud, and she entered that nobody had, which is the fourth morning running she has entered that.

At the sixth bell the clerk read the day's figure and the total out loud, both of them, because a cook had said a number in the middle of a morning and a boundary that hears a number out loud in a room and then says nothing about its own is a boundary keeping two sets of books. One today and two hundred and sixty since the keeper signed. The boundary is at three hundred and seventy-seven and a quarter, and it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen and is carried.

The count was read at one hundred and twenty-one, and nobody said one word about it, and the clerk said out loud that it was a number of days and not a number of anything, which she has now said in thirty weeks running and which she is not going to stop saying, and then she walked the board at eighteen entries and stopped there. A man who reads numbers had given two refusals out loud in a room that morning without being asked to, and a board is the third of those things, and she was not going to make it three.

Tams Quill read the eighteen. Eleven nulls, then a claim, a mark, a row, a plan, a condition, a store and a brace; nine in the ordinary column and nine in the column with nothing over the top of it. He gave no reason for the column and nobody asked him, and Dalla Sen entered that he had given none and that she had not asked, and entered that this was the seventh morning running and that she was not going to keep a figure of it.

The pot is four days and the seam gave nothing on two mornings and one on a third, and the four households at a second gate have nothing, and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody in this room or any other.

Then the anteroom was a room with a trestle in it and a pump and six packs along a bench, and a hole in the far corner of the floor where a flag stone has been lifted twice in thirty-five weeks and put back twice. The corner was colder than the rest of it. At the seventh bell the light off the flags had gone the colour of a wet slate, nobody turned a lamp on, and the lamp on the anteroom floor by the party wall stayed unlit where it has stood since the seventh day of the thirty-fourth week.
