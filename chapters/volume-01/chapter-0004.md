# Chapter 4 — Water Against the Grade

The water reached the lowest stone of the lean-to in the late afternoon.

Marek moved the shelter first. They dragged the low frame toward the chamber’s higher side, but the floor tilted under their feet. Each person shifted the load a little, and each shift changed the way the old stone answered. The broken shaft above admitted a blade of daylight, then a shadow, then daylight again.

Leena could not use her right arm. She kept the sling close and directed the litter with her left knee, calling out when Oren’s shoulder or Tams’s boot found a hollow place.

“Left,” she said. “No, left of the seam. The seam is carrying the wall.”

“You cannot see the seam from there,” Oren said.

“I can see where your boot is about to be.”

He moved.

In the lower recess, a pump stood on a stable plinth. It was an old reciprocating pump, its iron frame painted once in a color that had faded to brown. The handle ended in a wooden grip split down the middle. A bronze fitting surrounded the piston rod, and the gland was shattered. Several pieces lay in the silt. A square hole in the crank housing showed that the drive-dog key was missing.

Iven wiped his hand on his shirt and studied the pump. “It was built to draw from a sump.”

“It cannot draw from anything,” Marek said. “The piston chamber loses its seal, and the key is gone.”

“Can you repair it?”

“Not with what we have.”

The water climbed another mark along the wall.

Marek crouched beside the pump. He could see the ordinary physics of it: a wet mineral seam carried pressure beneath the chamber, the pump bay sat lower than the shelter, and the visible floor was not the true floor. The water was not climbing because the room had changed its mind. It was climbing because pressure was finding a path with less resistance.

Then he felt something else.

He touched the plinth with his left hand. A pressure ran through the stone, up from below and away toward the wall. It was not the pump’s vibration. It had a direction, a changing weight, and a limit he could not name. The sensation vanished before he could make a measurement of it.

“Marek?” Leena said.

“The pump bay is receiving a shifting load,” he said. “That is all I can say.”

“That is not all you can say,” Iven said. “It is all you can prove.”

Marek looked at him. “Yes.”

The admission left him less comfortable than the argument would have.

A hinged flood gate separated the pump bay from the lower recess. Its bronze pins were intact, but the gate had dropped once and the lower hinge had taken a set. It could be closed to delay the water. It could not pump anything.

Marek pointed to a pale seam in the wall behind the gate. “Iven, take the timber line. Oren, second chock. Tams, watch that seam and call every change in the water. Leena, call the shelter line from above. Nobody touches the pump until the gate is seated.”

“You are not taking the jack alone,” Oren said.

“I was not going to.”

“You were thinking it.”

“Thinking it is not the same as doing it.”

“It is close enough to watch.”

They set the two short timber uprights beneath the gate’s outer frame, using them as chocks rather than cutting them into the frame. Iven placed wedges at the base. Oren took the second chock line and braced his boots against a stable ledge. Tams crouched near the mineral seam, one hand on the wall, watching the water.

The first shove brought the gate inward. Stone grit burst from the hinge. Iven’s shoulder went back. The wall behind him cracked from the bottom upward.

Everyone stopped.

“Do not pull,” Marek said. “Iven, keep your hands where they are. Oren, hold the chock. Tams, is the seam moving?”

“Water is coming faster,” Tams said. “Not much. Enough.”

Marek moved to the wall beside the gate and felt the pressure pulse through his left palm. It was stronger near the crack, but he could not tell whether the wall was carrying the gate or the gate was carrying the wall. The distinction mattered. If the gate pulled the wall inward, closing it would make the shelter unsafe. If the wall was already being pushed outward, the gate might buy time.

He did not know.

He drew the distinction aloud.

Leena’s voice came from the higher floor. “If you need a decision, make one. Don’t make a speech while the water makes it for you.”

Marek looked at the damaged tool kit beside the pump. Its case had split open in the fall. Inside were a small saw, a few files, wedges, and a roll of clean cloth. The clean cloth was the last they had. They could use it to pack the hinge and stop grit from scoring the bronze. They could not wash it afterward, and Leena’s shoulder would need clean dressing if the skin opened.

The tool kit could also be driven into the gap as wedges. It was already damaged. The difference between a ruined tool and a ruined person was not the value of the object but what it could keep from happening next.

“Pack the hinge,” Marek said. “Use the kit pieces after that. We are buying time, not repairing the pump.”

Iven took the cloth. “You are asking me to waste good material on a bad gate.”

“The gate is the only material between the pump and the shelter.”

Oren and Tams held while Iven folded the cloth beneath the lower pin. Marek worked the pry bar into the gap, using his left hand. The gate crept shut. Water pushed against the other side with a flat, heavy sound.

A crack opened in the upper frame.

Marek felt the floor tilt and his knees nearly went. The pressure behind the wall surged, then eased. For an instant he heard the tunnel’s old stone groan in a voice that seemed to come from his own chest.

He pulled back from the wall.

“Stop,” he said. “The gate is holding. The frame is not.”

Iven looked at the crack. “If we leave it, the water comes in when the hinge shifts.”

“If we add timber, the wall may come in first.”

“There is a third answer.”

“There is always a third answer,” Marek said. “It is just usually the one with a body under it.”

Oren pointed to the stable outer plinth. “Take the load there. The gate is hung from the frame. If we let the frame hinge and brace the gate itself, the wall can settle without the gate striking the pump.”

Marek stared at him. “That is not the way the bracket was drawn.”

“The bracket is not the room,” Oren said. “The room is what the water is doing.”

It was a good observation. It was also the kind of observation that could make a strong man trust a weak plan because the weak plan sounded humble. Marek checked the plinth, the gate, and the line of force twice. Oren was right that the gate could be isolated. He was wrong that the plinth had enough margin to receive the difference without help.

So they used the wall as the receiving support.

Iven and Oren set the second chock against the stable side, while Tams watched the seam and called the pressure changes. Marek directed them with his left hand, keeping his injured right hand braced against his chest. The timber groaned. The wall cracked again, but the gate held. The water rose behind it, stopped, and began to push through a new line along the floor.

Marek called the result aloud. “This is a delay. We have not repaired the pump. We have bought the upper floor.”

The distinction cost him something. He could feel the wish to make it more—the wish for a word that turned a temporary obstruction into a solution. Leena heard it anyway.

“Delay,” she repeated. “Not repair.”

“Delay.”

“Good. Keep the difference.”

The cloth under the hinge was dark with grit now. They used the same fouled strip to secure Leena’s sling rather than lose another clean dressing. The damaged hand-tool kit broke into pieces under the pry bar. The surviving screw jack, the pry bar, and the two short timber uprights remained, but the pump still lacked a gland and a drive-dog key.

The water found a lower route and began to climb again.

When the floor shifted, a section of wall receded and exposed a dry room beyond it. Inside, six shallow sleeping pads were set into a stone shelf. Above them, a row of hooks still held the ghosts of coats and school satchels. A ventilation grille was set high in the far wall, its outer face bricked shut. Beyond the pads, two thick piers carried a broad lintel over the room.

Iven looked from the pads to the grille. “That is not a room we can breathe in.”

“Yet,” Marek said. “It is higher than the water.”

“High is not the same as safe.”

“No,” Marek said. “But it is where we can examine a different set of failures.”

The pump housing shuddered once under the pressure, then went still. Without its gland and drive-dog key, it had no prime to lose. Its repair would need a seal and a key. The water would keep pressing against every mistake they had made above it.

Marek looked at the dry pads and the blocked vent.

For the first time, the room looked like a promise. That made it more dangerous, not less.
