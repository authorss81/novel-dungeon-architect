# Chapter 196 — The Second Line

The fourth day of the thirty-seventh week is a Thursday, and the corridor is shut for the third time in four days, and the school did not run and lost nothing. Eleven people live in this Hearthhold and six more do not, and this anteroom is rated ten including one litter for no more than two hours, and it was emptied for the length of a carry at the second bell and filled again at the third, and nobody could say afterwards what had been carried.

Jo Verrick had the frame at the first bell, a finger and two-thirds, and the party wall was a quarter of an inch, the same as seventeen, and the string was where the string was. Renn Vale came down the four steps at the second bell, which is her bell on a shut day, and put her ear against the party wall on this side of it, and was there about the time it takes to say a sentence, four times over. She came back and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times. The clerk wrote a figure of nothing against it, and entered that she had now entered that figure in this room six mornings out of seven, and that she had begun to notice her own hand entering it, which she also entered, and she gave the noticing no number.

Iven Tallo stood at the anvil end from the first bell to the seventh, the chisel in his right hand, his left open and flat on the anvil where it has lain all month, and he carved nothing and he did not put the tool down once. Four people in that room looked at him over the course of the day and he looked at none of them, and by the afternoon the room had stopped looking.

The anteroom's terms were said by a person and repeated by the clerk, and the count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change, and the outer service door was the one a body went out of, and the fifth of those six terms was in a room with both the people it names in it, and the pump was attended and stopped on the rising water line that has not moved.

Mero Senn came off the doorway about the second bell with his bag against his hip, and he had the thing he had come to say ready and he had had it ready since Sunday, and the room got it out of him in about nine seconds.

"**A plan that assumes the air engineer is in the building,**" he said, "**is a plan with a named person in it who is not a post and has no page and cannot be sent to, and who comes down three steps for air and for nothing else. And I have watched two rooms in this district get emptied on a document and I want a person to tell me what happens in the eleven minutes it takes to walk nine children down a corridor past five and a half shores if the one person in this building who answers for the air is somewhere else.**"

Nobody answered him.

Nera Oduya was in the room. She had come down two of the three steps at the second bell with the valve board against her hip and she was standing at the bottom of them with the dyed cloth out, and she heard the whole of it, and she did not answer it, and after a while she folded the cloth and went back up the steps and did not say anything on the way.

Dalla Sen entered the question in his words and entered that nobody answered it, and entered that the air engineer was in the room at the time and did not answer it, and entered that she had noticed herself not asking her a second time, and entered that the noticing had got no number and would not get one, and that a clerk who puts a number on the not-asking ends up keeping the number.

"**For the record,**" from the doorway, and he put his bag down on the floor to say it, "**I am not asking her. I put it to the room and the room is what I put it to.**"

"**Entered, and entered that he said so in the room and in his own words,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that a physician who says *for the record* in a room has said it to the record and not to the person, and that those are two different things and he knows it.**"

"**I know it,**" and he picked the bag up again.

Then the cook, from the doorway, with the cloth over her arm.

"**Two days,**" said Mikka Hale. "**Three mornings now that the seam has given me nothing, and it is at four threads, and two days is what the pot is, and there are six people in this building who are not this building's and who have eaten out of it for a fortnight. And I have done the arithmetic out loud in this room three mornings running and nobody has answered it, and nobody is going to ask me to. And there is a thing I want entered and it is not about the pot.**"

"**Then it goes in,**" said Dalla Sen, with the chalk up.

"**That I have been left alone with it,**" said Mikka Hale. "**For a fortnight. And that a fortnight ago I would have been in a room of this building in a different mood, and I have not been, and I have looked for the reason four times this week and the reason is not there, and I am entering that I looked and did not find it, and that I am a cook and not a person who goes looking.**"

The clerk entered it and entered that she had been asked to enter a looking and had done so, and that the not-finding was the cook's own and not the clerk's.

Then the afternoon went the way shut afternoons go, and nothing happened in it, and that is what shut afternoons are for.

Emrys Callow's five cleared the anteroom floor at the third bell without being asked, and then stood along the bench with their packs on and did not talk, and one of them asked whether the timber was wanted and was told that it was, and the answer given was a word and not a figure, and the answerer entered that afterwards.

Bel Ordry came down the step twice and both times he was about to say something and did not, and the second time Renn Vale looked at him and he said, "**Nothing. I had a sentence about a man at a counter and it was about somebody else and it is not true and I am dropping it in the room so that a clerk can enter that I dropped it.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that a man who keeps a page brought a sentence to a room and dropped it there and would not say what it was about, and that the clerk did not ask, and that this is the fourth time in three weeks that she has not asked him something and that she is not going to keep a figure of the not-asking.**"

Tams Quill read the board at the sixth bell and it is at eighteen entries, of which eleven are nulls and seven are not, and the seven are a claim, a mark, a row, a plan, a condition, a store and a brace, and the two columns are nine and nine, and the second of them has nothing over the top of it. He gave no reason for the column and she did not ask him for one, and entered that he had offered nothing and that the offer was not made, and that a man who has refused five things in three weeks read a board out loud on a Thursday and refused nothing, and that she had watched him not refuse and had entered the watching.

The sixth bell came and the day's figure and the total went into the book and neither of them was read out, and the reason went in beside them: a room had gone four days without anybody saying the thing that was being said in it, and a clerk does not stand up and read a total over four days of that.

Two today and two hundred and ninety since the keeper signed. The boundary is at four hundred and seven and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.

The count went in under the day's figure in the ordinary way, in the ordinary hand, in the ordinary place, and nobody remarked on it. The clerk said it out loud once, to the anvil end, without stopping in what she was doing: the fortieth week running of a number of days and not a number of anything, thanked once by a man who was not in the building and had not meant it. One hundred and thirty-one days and one hundred and thirty-one nights, and it is not going to be one hundred and thirty-two.

Seventy-five and a half on the crew, unmoved, and the reason for the day in the book where a reason goes: a man at the anvil end has not said nothing for two days, he has said four sentences and one of them was a refusal, and a clerk who cannot tell a silence from a fault is not going to hand five people a piece of work in the middle of it.

The pot is two days, the seam gave nothing on three mornings running, the four households at a second gate have nothing, and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody.

At the seventh bell the pump stopped, and the anteroom emptied the way an anteroom empties on a shut evening, in ones and twos. The six who are not this building's went into the two rooms off the far end, past the pump, which are not in the sixteen rows and have not been in the sixteen rows since the twenty-ninth week, and the last one in was Nella Bray, and she stood in the doorway of them for a moment with her slate against her hip and then went in.

What was left in the anteroom was five people, and one of them was Iven Tallo, and he had not put the chisel down all day and he put it down now.

"**Dalla,**" he said. "**I want your book and your chalk.**"

Dalla Sen did not move for about four strokes of nothing.

"**The reason for asking is a clerk's business and not mine,**" from the step, and then he caught himself, and said, "**No. I have withdrawn that. Give him the book.**"

She gave him the book. She gave him the chalk off the anvil end, which is where it had been since the seventh day of the thirty-sixth week when she had put it there out of her own reach, and he came and took it, and he wrote in her book, standing, in a carpenter's hand, which is a heavier hand than a clerk's and does not slant the way hers does, and he wrote the refusal and then he wrote the reason next to it, in the space where a reason goes.

Then he read it back to the room himself, out loud, and the room heard it.

"**A route-holder's post, for the working life, a bond lodged with the office and held, a wage in coin on the first and the fifteenth which I have not had in money since I was twenty-two, hours entered against a named route in a register kept by the office, and an apprentice back on the roll with a number and his name on it and not *the apprentice of* anything. I am turning all of that down.**"

He put the chalk on the anvil end and left it there.

"**And the reason, which I am going to say in my own mouth and not in a keeper's, because a keeper would make it sound better than it is.**"

"**A route-holder's hours go in a register against a named route, and the register shows them, and anybody can look at the register and see that I was there. That is the whole of what a post is. It is a way of being found.**"

He looked at the book on the trestle and not at anybody.

"**And I have got very good at not being found. Thirty-seven weeks in this building and there is no page in it with my hours on it, and there is no register anywhere with my name in a line of it, and the one page in this building that would take a man's hours has been left blank for thirty-seven weeks on purpose, and the last of the three reasons on it is the only thing I have ever wanted written down anywhere, and nobody is ever going to write it down anywhere. And I have got extremely good at that, and I have got good at it in a way I could not have got good at anywhere else in my life, and the only reason I have got good at it is that this room made it possible and then had nothing to do with what I did about it.**"

"**And that is the reason,**" he said. "**Not the money. I want the money, and there is no version of this evening in which I pretend otherwise. The reason is that a post would take my work out of the only place in this district where nobody owns it, and put it in a book four and a half days up a passage with a heading over it, and a heading is a column, and a column is a post, and I have been in this building thirty-seven weeks arguing with a keeper about exactly that and I will not win my own argument by taking the other side of it.**"

Nobody in the anteroom said anything, and the pump was not running, which is why there was nothing.

Then Emrys Callow said the objection, and he said it flat and without any of the shape a man puts around a thing he does not want to be unfair about.

"**You have just turned down a wage and a boy's roll number on the ground that a register is findable. And a boy on a roll is a boy with a master. And a boy with a master is a boy who can be sent.**"

"**I know,**" said Iven Tallo.

"**The rest of it. You have spent five days telling a room that the best thing about this building is that nobody in it can be sent for anything, and you are on your way to a camp four and a half days up a road where a man in an office can send a boy with a crutch to a third holding in the morning. Say which of those two you have chosen and say it in words and not in a shape.**"

"**I have chosen the one where the boy is not on the roll,**" said Iven Tallo. "**And I have chosen it for me and not for him, because the man losing the wage is me and the boy is fourteen and in a room with a bench and a splint, and I have not asked him and I am not going to, and I would like the clerk to enter that.**"

"**Entered, in his words, and entered the reason, and the reason is the same reason that has been in the clerk's book since the sixth day of the thirty-second week about a boy on a bench and a page with a column in it,**" said Dalla Sen, and then stopped, and looked at the slate, and did not write.

"**The rest of it,**" from the step.

"**The reason is in the book and it is not going to be read out again this evening,**" she said, "**and I am entering that a man has asked me to enter something I have already entered, and that the entry standing is the reason he gave, and that the second reason he gave this afternoon is a new one and it is his, and it is the second line of this entry, and there is nobody's name on either of them.**"

"**Then nobody is going to use the boy,**" from the step she had come down to, "**and I am going to say why in a room, and the reason is that the last thing this building did with a page was try to put a person in a column, and I am not starting with a boy of fourteen on a Thursday, and I have not asked him and neither has the carpenter and neither has the clerk.**"

Then Mero Senn said the half against, from the doorway, in the voice he uses for the one sentence he has said in nine rooms.

"**You have just handed me a plan with a named injured person in it,**" he said, "**and a plan with a named injured person in it is not a plan, and I have said that in nine rooms and I have never had to say it about a man who was being honest. You named yourself instead of the boy. That is not a different instrument. That is the same instrument with a different man standing in it, and I have watched men do that for thirty years and it is always the same plan and it always comes out of the room with a man in it instead of a boy, and the room thinks it has been spared something.**"

"**And the half against your half against,**" said Iven Tallo, without turning round, "**is that I did not name myself instead of the boy. I named myself because the man who is losing the wage has to be the one who says the reason, and if a boy of fourteen stands in a room and is told that his master has given something up for him then the boy learns that a man can be made to be sorry, and that is a thing a person learns at fourteen and keeps.**"

Nobody improved on that either, and Dalla Sen entered it in his words, and entered that a physician had put the half against a carpenter's refusal in a room and that the carpenter had answered it from the anvil end without turning round, and that the answer was worse than the objection and that she was entering the answer because it was in the room and not because she agreed with it.

Then Nella Bray's voice came from the doorway of the two rooms, and she had not gone in after all, or had come back out.

"**There is a thing I want on the record before anybody in this room puts another thing on it,**" she said, from the doorway, and did not come in.

Nobody told her not to.

"**I have stood at a counter for nineteen years,**" she said, "**and I have watched about four hundred men turn down money, and in every single one of those four hundred cases somebody in the room reached for a word, and the word was always the same one, and it is a word for a person who has given something up and died for it, and none of those four hundred men died for anything. They took a different offer. They went home. They were difficult for the rest of their lives.**"

"**So the clerk is not going to write it,**" she said, "**and nobody in this room is going to say it, and if it has already been thought by somebody at the back of this room then I would like the thought entered as a thought and not as a word.**"

"**Entered, in a counter clerk's words, and entered that the word was not used,**" and then, after a moment, "**and entered that a man has given up a wage on the first and the fifteenth and a boy off a roll, and that nobody in this room has thanked him, and that the clerk has not thanked him either, and that she noticed herself not doing it, and that she is entering the noticing and not a number of it.**"

Ilsa Norr came down the four steps from the kitchen-line end, which she does when a thing at the anvil end has gone on long enough, and she had her slate in both hands and she did not sit down.

"**Then a condition is going to be read out in this room, because a man has just written something in this building and a standing condition should be heard on the same day as a refusal.**"

"**The brace stands while the two of us still agree that the wall can take it,**" she said. "**The first of us to stop agreeing takes it out. Neither of us has to give a reason to the other one first, and the second of us to find out is the one who acts. The two of us is me and the man who put it there.**"

"**It has not been used in a week,**" she said. "**And the man who put it there was not asked on Thursday of last week and has not been asked since, and he is not going to be asked, and I am not agreeing for him. So the condition is standing and it is unexamined, and it has been unexamined since the fourth day of the thirty-sixth week, and I spent it once that morning and I have thought about it every day since.**"

"**And the man who built that brace,**" she said, "**is the man who has just written a refusal in this room, and I would like it entered that I have said so out loud, in front of him, on purpose, and that I am not asking him anything and that nobody is going to ask him anything.**"

Nobody asked him anything. Iven Tallo stood at the anvil end with the chisel on it and his left hand open and flat and did not look up, and the clerk entered that she had not asked and that he had not answered and that neither of those was a surprise to her.

Then the anteroom got to the end of the day the way an anteroom does, and Bel Ordry put the plate and the coat and the rule where they go. The brass survey rule had said nothing for twenty-eight weeks and the wrapped plate had been unopened for thirty-seven, and neither of them had come into a room today, and he entered them in the same state. A man who counts those weeks out of a calendar this building does not keep is the only person who can be wrong about them.

"**Entered, and entered that it was a figure out of a private calendar and not out of the clerk's book,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**Then the thing that has to go up the passage,**" said Renn Vale, "**and it is not going today, because a Thursday is a shut day and the middle passage is shut on a Thursday, and a carrier does not walk a shut passage for a piece of paper that a man wrote in a clerk's book at seven in the evening.**"

Dalla Sen wrote it out on a single sheet, and she wrote the day of the week on it and not a date, and she gave the reason out loud, which is the same reason she gave for the message about a piece of timber on the fifth day of the thirty-sixth week, and which is that a person in a year has to be able to tell a message from a form.

"**And nobody is going to hand it to a man who will put a name in a second line,**" she said, "**and the second line of that stiff cover is empty, and it was empty this morning, and it is going to be empty when that paper is next held by anybody at all, and I am entering that it is going to be empty and that I am entering it in my own book and not in a column, and that this is the second time I have entered something about that line and the first time I have entered what it is going to be.**"

Then the light went off the flags and the room was the room it had been two minutes before with two people gone out of it, and at the last of it Renn Vale came back in from the door and said that she had put her ear against the party wall at the seventh bell with nobody with her and at no bell, and that there had been nothing, four times.

"**And I am going to say the other half of it because there is a room nearly empty now and both of the two people who can stop me are standing in it,**" she said, "**and neither of them stopped me, and I told this room on the fifth day of last week that I would say where they were and I have, and nobody has done anything with it, and I am not going to be the one who does something with it either, and it will keep not being done until somebody in this building decides to do it, and nobody has.**"

The clerk entered that, in the watcher's words, and entered that the room had two of the three who could stop her in it at the seventh bell on the fourth day of the thirty-seventh week, and that it had not used them, and that she was entering that the room had not used them and was not going to say whether the room was right not to, because a clerk who begins deciding which nothings are the good ones stops being a clerk and starts being a keeper.
