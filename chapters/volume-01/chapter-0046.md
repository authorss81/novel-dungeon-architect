# Chapter 46 — The Load It Repeats

A shore that is holding nothing is a piece of firewood leaning on a wall, and for a day and a half every person in that room knew exactly what they were looking at.

Oren Sill packed the last of the foot on the second afternoon and sat back on his heels on the common landing's floor with his hands on his knees and looked at six feet of hardwood leaning against a wall, and said the thing the whole of the project had been walking towards for nine weeks.

"It is not doing anything," he said. "It has not held a thing in its life. I have packed four joints in this building and every one of them was holding something that was already falling, and this one is holding a wall that is not falling, and a shore that is holding a wall that is not falling has no way of telling us that it is any good at all."

"Then we put a load on it," Marek said.

"No," Iven said. "We put a *line* on it. A load is a man with a bar and a number, and a man with a bar and a number is a test, and I have told six people in this building that this is not a test and I am not going to start on a Tuesday afternoon. The line is the work. Put the line on and the shore has a job and the line has a job and the two of them find out what they are together, which is the only way a joint finds out anything."

The party was at the near landing for the making fast, and the terms were read at the threshold first, and Jo said the quarter of an hour had grown every time because the number of people who wanted to add a clause had grown every time.

The stair stayed corded. No person on the slab. A named period on a day the room had agreed, an end set by the cord, the cloth, or a watcher's word, the party out of the step in under a minute, and the near side as found, with Renn's exception on the page in her own hand in both words: refused as a clause, granted as a fact.

Renn re-tied the cord, with Sefu, at the top of the three steps, with the party standing behind them and not one of them within reach, because the re-tie is hers and Sefu's and nobody else's and has been since the thirty-eighth day and was reconfirmed on the fortieth.

The bell at the threshold was rung by Ruk Sella, who had been asked in person at the anteroom door by Dalla Sen and who had said yes in one word and had been told, this time, exactly what she was being given, which was a rope and nine steps and an instruction to stand at the top of them, and who had repeated it back in one sentence and got it right, and Jo wrote down that she had repeated it back.

The bypass hatch stayed latched. The stair-side grille stayed shut. The anteroom's cloth stayed on its string.

---

The cord went up first, and it went up the *face* of the wall, and that was Iven's decision and he made it in front of them and gave the two reasons.

"First: a line in a joint is a line that is being cut, and I have a lead on this lead, and the cord is the lead, and if I put forty feet of cord into a bed joint I have spent the boundary's only line on a thing that will be a new thing in four months. Second, and this is the one that matters: a line in a joint bears in the joint, and a joint is the weakest part of any wall there is, and I am not putting a hundred-year-old joint of a stair this building has corded for nine weeks." He put his hand flat on the dressed stone. "So it bears on the face. On the open face. Where anybody can see it, and anybody can count the wear on it, and nobody has to be told what a line is doing because it is doing it in plain sight on a landing under four people's floor."

"And that will be the first thing a child touches," Ilsa said.

"It will be the first thing a child touches, and it will be a hard cord at shin height on a common landing, and the first thing a child does with a hard cord at shin height is put a foot in it and fall over," Iven agreed. "So it goes up at a hand's width of the face and it goes up behind the shore's head, and the shore's head is four feet up, and that is a child's shoulder and not a child's shin, and it is the only part of that wall a child can reach. Take it higher than the head and we have a hanging cord, and a hanging cord is where the wind — and there is no wind, it is a stair, it is pressure — a hanging cord is where a thing gets into a line and a line gets into a person's neck. Four feet, on the open face, behind the shore. And Jo, that goes on the board, and it goes on the board in a line by itself, and it does not go on the board in a paragraph."

The forty feet came off the hook in the boundary stores and went up nine steps and along a corridor and through a doorway two foot eight wide under a cracked lintel with three wedges in it, and nobody had to decide anything about that except the size of it. It went through on its own, coiled over Iven's forearm, and he said that a coil of forty feet of black cord is about the width of a child's arm and that if anybody had been sentimental about it they would have carried it in pieces and been four hours on the stairs for nothing.

Sefu Aran stood on the common landing while the line was set and did not touch it, and Renn Vale stood with him and did not touch it either, and when Iven asked her, twice, whether the cord's bearing on the face was acceptable to her, she said, "It is on the landing and it is six feet below my floor and I will look at it tonight with a lamp and not with a man, and you may go on."

---

The lead went down the face from the shore's head to the top of the block, four feet of open dressed stone, and the party went down the three steps for the making fast and the lamp went in over the lip and came back off water, as it always did, and the far wall of the throat was not seen.

Under the near wall's base, in the angle where the wall met the throat, the block sat with its hollow cut into the top of it, and the hollow had two things in it, and they had both been there for forty years.

The groove in the bottom, two fingers wide, rounded and deep and a hundred years polished. And the wedge-shaped piece of a dressed block that a woman had driven into the gap with stone chippings in the cold year, which was not a bearing and had never been a bearing and was a stop.

Nobody touched it.

"Take it out," said somebody.

"No," said Renn Vale, from the three steps, where she was not allowed to be and had come as far as the top step and no further, in her coat, the way she came in.

"Renn—"

"You are not touching my mother's repair," she said. "You have told me in three meetings that it is a stop and not a bearing and I have believed you, and I have told you twice that it is not a code, and neither of those things is the same as saying it can be taken out by six people who are going to be gone. Leave it where it is. It has been doing nothing for forty years and it can go on doing nothing for as long as this building stands, and if your line needs the hollow then the line goes in beside it, and a woman put it there and that is the only claim anybody has on that block that I know of."

And the line went in beside it.

Iven made it up himself, on his knees on the near landing with the party's lamp held low by Jo, and it was the only piece of skilled work at the stair in nine weeks that he did not explain, and he explained it anyway, out of habit, in the flat voice.

"Three turns and a seizing and nothing else. Not four, because four is a hitch that bites, and a hitch that bites in a groove is a thing you cannot get back out of without a bar. Not two, because two is a bend and a bend in a groove under four hundredweight is a nip. Three turns, and the seizing is the same cord doubled and pulled with a stone, not with a bar, and the stone is off the step and not out of the bay." He got the turns in. "There. It is in and it is not pretty and it will not come out by hand, which is what I want. In a year somebody will come down here and want to take this line out, and I want it to be a job with a bar in it, not a thing a person can undo in a hurry at the wrong moment."

Then the bight came tight, and Iven put his own hand on it and pulled it, gently, two inches, and let it go, and nothing happened at all.

"That is the line," he said. "Now somebody has to be the shore's hand when it comes tight for the first time, and I want to say what that means before I ask for it, because it is not a favour and it is not going to be one of those things that happens to a man."

Nobody said anything, which was the correct response.

"A packed joint moves when it is first loaded," Iven said. "Not much. A fraction. And a fraction at this end of the wall is a fraction at that end, and if the head moves while the line is coming tight then the line does not come tight the way I have told you it comes tight, it comes tight *sideways*, and a line coming tight sideways in a groove that is a hundred years old puts a mark in the groove, and a mark in that groove is the four inches of bright stone on the front of that hollow, and I would rather that band stayed exactly the size it is for as long as the four of us are alive."

"So somebody holds the head while the line comes tight," Marek said. "For how long?"

"For the count of about thirty, and then the head settles and there is nothing to hold. And it has to be a hand that knows what a joint doing a fraction looks like from the outside, because from the outside it is nothing, it is a fraction, and a man who is not expecting it will feel the wood move and will pull, and pulling is the one thing that turns a fraction into a two-inch gap and a bang that eleven feet below wakes everybody who sleeps over it."

He did not ask the room. He turned round and looked at Oren Sill, who was standing on the near landing with his sore wrist from the twenty-fifth day held against his chest because it was the way he held it, and who had packed four joints since the collapse with sized wood and his knee and had never once had to be told what a fraction was.

"Will you hold it," Iven said. "Not help. Hold it. And I want you to know before you answer that the reason I am asking you and not the reason you are the biggest man here is that you are the only man in this room who has watched a face rather than a number, and I am asking you because your hands are better than my mouth, and I would have said so in the ninth week if I had had anything to say."

"I will hold it," Oren said, "and I will not pull, and I will not be thanked for it, and Iven you can say what you are about to say and then stop saying it."

"I am going to say that if it comes tight and it goes wrong then you will be the one holding it, and that is a thing nobody in this building can be relieved of, and that is also the reason I am asking, because you will not be frightened of it."

The line was hauled taut on the second stroke of the cord.

The slab was on the far side. The wheel took up. And the line came in through the hollow, up over the block, and up the open face of the wall, and into the head of a shore that Oren Sill was holding with both hands and his weight, and the shore took it, and the foot took it, and the six feet of hardwood that had been leaning on a wall doing nothing for two days took four hundredweight of a line and a hundred-year-old groove and a wall nobody had ever seen, and the wall did not come out, and the joint did not go anywhere, and Oren Sill's hands stayed where they were for the count of thirty-one, which was one more than Iven had asked for and which he did not mention afterwards.

Then it settled, and he took his hands off, and there was nothing at all to see.

The wheel came round. The rope came back. The slab came back to the first landing and stopped, and it stopped the way a thing stops when it has arrived rather than the way a thing stops when it has been beaten, and the whole of the party felt the difference in the soles of their feet and none of them could have said what it was in words.

"That," Nera said, from the string, "is the first time that mechanism has arrived in nine weeks and I will not be asked what I think of it, because I do not think in words about this and I have said so."

---

It was quicker.

That was the price and it was the only price, and Nera found it inside a day and told Sefu before she told anybody else, which she had done once in nine weeks in the thirty-sixth day and did again without anybody remarking on it.

"It is not slower," she said. "It is quicker. It took a second and a half when it was fighting a stone and it takes about a second now, and it comes round more often, and the reason is that the wheel is no longer being driven against a stop. Nothing has got worse. The work is simply being done properly now and properly is faster than stubbornly."

Jo asked for the number and Nera would not give it, and the room did not leave it there.

"Write it," Jo said. "Write that the window is shorter. And write the reason in the words of the person who found it, and the reason is *the work is being done properly now*."

"And I am not going to put a figure on the valve board that Jo will then read as a plan," Nera said, "and I will say why out loud so that it is not mistaken for modesty. In the thirty-sixth day I told Sefu before I told you, and I told you a count of forty-one turns over eleven days, thirty-four of them between one and nine minutes after the lever came off, and I said I would not put an unproven pattern on a page I did not hold. That has not changed. I have a page. The valve board is my page and I keep it and it is mine, and I have written on it today, in my own hand, in a column nobody reads, the number I am working to, and the number is mine and it is a working number and it is not a promise, and it goes on my board and not on Jo's and not into Dalla's ledger, and I will say that to Sefu now and then I am finished."

"Go on," Sefu said.

"Then it goes in the ledger as what is true of a room and not as what is true of a mechanism," Nera said, "which is: *the dead window after the lever comes off is shorter than it was, the air engineer holds the number, and no person is to plan a crossing to a number they have been given by nobody.*"

Dalla wrote it and read it back, and it is the first line in the ledger in nine weeks that says *no person is to plan a crossing*, and the four words after it were hers.

And the band was not explained, and it was nearly explained, and Iven stopped it.

He put his finger against the four inches of bright stone on the front face of the hollow, without touching it, and looked at it for a long time.

"I am going to say one thing about that band and then I am going to shut up about it for the rest of my life," he said, "and Jo is going to write it down, and Jo is going to write *not* down whatever I do not say, and both of those are the correct things to do. That band is line wear. It is the wear of a bight that has been hauled up and let down in that face hard enough to take the skin off the stone, and I have just made one, and mine is not visible, and mine is not four inches, and I do not know why mine is not four inches and I am not going to guess out loud in a room where four people sleep over the answer."

"And the rest," Renn said.

"And the rest is that it is not old," Iven said, "and I said that in the thirty-third day, and the groove underneath is a hundred years and the band is not, and I am a carpenter and not a clerk and I am not going to be the man who stands in a room and tells a woman with a dead mother that her mother's mother knew something about a stone. That is Renn's. And I am not going to ask, because I asked in the thirtieth day and I was told no and I have not asked since and I am not going to start again on the forty-first day."

Renn said nothing at all, and nobody filled the silence, and it lasted about four seconds, and then Oren Sill said, "Aye," quietly, which was the right thing and was all of the answer she needed, and she went up the three steps in front of everybody and did not look back at it.

---

The party was out of the step in forty-one seconds, which was three seconds better than the last one, and which Jo read out and then read the reason out with it, because she had made a rule about reasons in the thirty-seventh day and she was not going to stop making it because it suited her. The reason was: the forty-four was a turn, and a turn is the worst figure there is, and this was the first time of anything at all with a line in the block, and a line in the block takes the fraction out of a return. The near side was as found, and the terms were read back at the bend by Jo in the order a person leaving would find them, and the period closed, and the ledger had a line in it that had not been in it before: *the line-block on the returning stair is now a running block with a line in it. The lower side has a load path on this side of the throat. Nothing has been crossed. Nobody has been on the slab.*

Which was true, and which was the whole of it, and which Jo read out twice, and which did not make anybody in the anteroom say a word.

Except Tams Quill, who had the board turned round, and who had a new column on it again, and who read it out because he always read it out.

"Kept," he said. "One shore with a cut face in a wall, and its keeper is Renn Vale, and she has a road with her name on it and no name under it yet."

And Mikka Hale said, from the shelf, with her hands in a pot, "Nobody in this building has watched anything at night for nine weeks and the first thing that wants watching at night is under my neighbour's floor, and I would like the room to notice that I have said that and that I am the one who said it and that I am not offering."

Nobody offered, and it was on the page, and the page had nothing under it, which was the correct condition of a page and the worst condition of a building.
