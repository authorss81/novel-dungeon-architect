# Chapter 201 — The Line Has Moved

The fifth day of the thirty-ninth week is a Friday, the corridor is open, and the school ran for the forty-second morning in the room at the kitchen-line end, and the nine went up the nine steps at the first bell and came down them at the fourth and went round the corner of the anteroom floor without being told to. The pump had been started at the second bell every working morning for nineteen weeks and the man who started it was a man who had a sling on his right arm and took no weight and had not been asked to do it by anybody in that room, and on this morning he was late by about four minutes and the pump was running when he got there.

Jo Verrick had the frame at the first bell, a finger and two-thirds, and the party wall was a quarter of an inch, the twenty-first reading, and the same as twenty, and the string was where the string was. She put her thumb under the string to find out whether it was still tight, which is the standing practice and has been for twenty-one mornings, and the thumb came out of it right.

"**The wall is where it has been,**" she said, "**and I am going to give you the other half of that in my own words before anybody asks me for it. The line under the string, along the join, where the lime let go in the thirty-fourth week, is in two pieces and it has been in two pieces since the seventh day of the thirty-seventh week, and which end is the wider one is mine to keep. There is no number on either of them and the rule stays in its belt, and I would like the clerk to enter all four of those refusals as four refusals and not as one, and I have said it every morning this week and I will say it every morning next week.**"

"**Entered, in the words of the woman who reads it,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that she said it before anybody asked her and not after, and entered that the folding rule is on a belt which is staying where it is, for the twenty-first morning running, and entered that a person who has been refused four things by a wall this week is the only person in this room who has been refused anything at all.**"

Renn Vale put her ear to the party wall at the first bell, the way she does on an open morning, for about as long as a sentence takes to say, four times over, and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, and left it there. A figure of nothing went into the book against it, and into the space where a reason goes the fact that this was the ninth morning out of nine and that she had stopped expecting it to be anything else, and that this was a change in her and not in the wall.

The anteroom's terms were said at the first bell by a person and repeated by the clerk, all six, and the count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change, and the outer service door was the one a body went out of, and the fifth of those six terms was in a room with both of the people it names in it, and the pump was attended and was running, and the third of the six terms was read out afterwards and not before, which is the order it has been read in for a month.

Then the pump stopped, and it was not stopped by anybody, and it was at nine minutes past the second bell.

It did not stop the way a thing stops when a man has put his hand on it. It stopped the way a thing stops when it has arrived somewhere, three or four strokes, slower each time, and then nothing, and then a sound off the back of the frame that was the sound a bucket makes when a man stops carrying it before he means to.

Leena Orr was across the room before it finished. She had her good hand flat on the stone at the head of the frame and her right arm in the sling and she did not put the right arm anywhere, and she got her face close to the mark without touching it, and she stayed there about the time it takes to say a sentence.

Nobody in that anteroom said anything for a while, and Dalla Sen came and stood where she could see it, and she did not write.

"**That is my line,**" Leena Orr said. "**I put it there in the week before the corridor was shut three days, and I put it where the frame has been since the second week, and I put it at the height the water stood at in the eleventh week and not at a height I chose. It is a line of chalk on a stone and it is not a reading and I have never once said it was a reading and anybody in this room who says it was a reading is being kind to me.**"

"**And the pump's rule is the pump's rule,**" said Renn Vale, from the wall, without coming away from it. "**Six terms and one of them says the pump is attended and stopped on a rising water line, and that line is that line, and it has not moved since it went on there, and the pump has been running against it every working morning for nineteen weeks and nobody in this room has looked at it once since the week it was chalked.**"

"**Then the pump stops,**" said Bel Ordry, from the top of the nine steps, where he stands on an open morning, "**and the rule is not mine and I am glad it is not mine, and I am going to say the half against it before this room starts treating it as a decision. A line of chalk is a mark. A mark says where a thing was. A rule says what to do. This room has spent thirty-nine weeks refusing to confuse the two and it is about to walk a line of chalk across a floor and call it a rule because it is the only line anybody has got.**"

Jo Verrick took her hand off the string and came across to the frame, and looked at the chalk for a while, and did not touch it.

"**I will not touch it,**" she said. "**I will tell you what I see because I read this frame every morning and I have seen that mark every morning for nineteen weeks, and the mark is at the height it was put at, and there is a second mark under it.**"

The room came in about half a step.

"**Under it,**" she said, "**and a hand's breadth lower, and it is not chalk, and I have been looking at this frame since the second week of this building and I did not see it on Monday, and I did not see it on Tuesday, and I did not see it on Wednesday, and I did not see it on Thursday morning at the first bell when I had my thumb under the string, and I saw it at about two o'clock yesterday and I have not been able to account for that and I have not told anybody since and I would like that entered before I say anything else about it.**"

"**Entered first, in the words of the woman who reads it,**" said Dalla Sen, and she wrote, "**and entered that she saw it yesterday and did not report it for about a day, and entered that the reason is not yet given, and entered that the clerk did not ask for the reason, and that the two of those are the same fact.**"

"**A hand's breadth,**" said Bel Ordry. "**That is three inches and a bit, and I am going to say the thing this room has been not-saying for a week and I would like it said before the pot is said. I have been in this building thirty-nine weeks and I have not seen a second mark on anything. I have a brass rule in my coat that has said nothing for thirty weeks and a plate on that shelf that has not been opened for thirty-nine, and I have explained to this room twice in five weeks why a mark on a thing is not a rule and a rule is not a mark, and I have been right both times, and I would like a clerk to enter that I said so and that it has not been one single help.**"

Nobody improved on that, and the pump stood in the middle of the floor of the anteroom where it has stood since the second week, and there was no water in the pot that had not been in the pot yesterday, and the pot was one day.

Mikka Hale was in the kitchen-line doorway with the cloth over her arm and she said the thing about the pot before anybody asked her about the pump, which is what she does.

"**One day,**" she said. "**And I am not going to say the arithmetic out loud in that room again this week because a number said out loud in a room has stopped meaning anything in this building and I said so last week and I meant it. What I am going to say is a fact and it is a fact about my pot. Four days ago the seam gave nothing. Three days ago nothing. Two days ago nothing. This morning nothing. It is at four threads. Jo says four threads and has said four threads for a month and has not measured it in between and has told the clerk she is not going to be the one who makes a number for how fast that is going, and I agree with her and I am not going to be the one either.**"

"**Entered, and entered that she was not asked,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**So that is the pump, and that is the pot, and I have six people at my pot who are not this building's and they are eating out of it because I decided on a Friday night in the thirty-fifth week that they would, and I told this room and I asked never to be asked about it and I have not been asked about it once, and this morning I am telling you it again because a stopped pump changes how long two days lasts and I would like it in the book that I said it before anybody worked it out.**"

"**Entered, in the cook's words, and entered that she was not asked,**" said Dalla Sen.

Then the room got to the part of the morning that it could not get past, and it got there by accident, the way it has got there four times in five weeks.

Renn Vale had the slate in her apron and did not take it out.

"**I have six pails a day in this boundary,**" she said. "**Every day in seven, in two crossings, four and then two, and it is the third of my six terms and it has been the third of them since the twenty-second week, and I have never once read it as anything other than six pails, and it is six pails and it is a floor and a count of loads and not a number of people.**"

"**Then you can do six pails,**" said Emrys Callow from the anvil end, where he had been for a while with a cup in his hand that was not empty.

"**I can do six pails,**" she said. "**That has never once been the question and I have never once been asked it, and I am going to say the thing that is the question before anybody in this room finds it, and I would like the clerk to have her hand near the book.**"

Dalla Sen had her hand near the book.

"**Six pails a day is a number of loads,**" Renn Vale said, "**and loads are made for people, and the number of loads is decided by how many people there are, and this building has spent nineteen weeks deciding not to know how many people there are, and that was the right decision, and I said a good deal of it, and the pot was at nine days then and the decision cost this room nothing at all.**"

"**The pot is at one,**" said Mikka Hale.

"**The pot is at one,**" Renn Vale agreed, "**and there is no pump, and six pails a day does not scale. It does not have a number in it to scale. There is a figure on a stranger's sheet in this room and it is a ten and it is a permitted occupancy and it has been a permission since the second week and it is not what I am carrying. So I am going to stand here and be asked a question I have not got the instrument to answer, and I am not going to make a number up at eight in the morning on a Friday, and I am not going to be asked to make one up, and that is not a refusal of this room, it is a refusal of my own term, which I have been carrying for seventeen weeks and have never had cause to look at.**"

The room was very quiet, and the pump was not running, and somewhere behind the far wall two of the six people who were not this building's slept through all of it.

Marek Venn had been at the end of the trestle since the first bell with his shut book under his forearm and he had not said one word, and he was the man in the room who had spent nineteen weeks being proud of the second of those refusals.

He looked at the pot for a while. He looked at the slate in Renn Vale's apron. He looked at the stranger's sheet with the wax head at each end of it that had been lying on that table since the fifth day of the thirty-fourth week and had not moved and was not going to.

"**I am not going to say anything,**" he said, "**and I want it entered that I had something ready about a head of water in a haunch and I have decided not to say it, and the reason is that I have been wrong in this room for five volumes and the last four times I said a true thing early somebody built a plan out of it before anybody else had looked. So I will look, and then I will say it, and the room can do what it likes with it in about a week.**"

"**Entered, in his words,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that the man who found out had a thing in his mouth at the eighth bell on a Friday and did not say it, and entered that the reason he gave is the third of that kind of reason in this clerk's book and that it is the first one he has given with a number at the end of it.**"

Marek Venn had not expected that to be the part that landed, and it landed anyway.

Then the room did what it does on a Friday, and the anteroom filled up about a quarter past the eighth, and the fourth bell came, and the school had already gone out at the far door and the nine sets of small footprints were in the dust of the seam by the corner of the floor, and the wall with five and a half shores in it stood on the common landing side doing the only thing it has ever done.

Renn Vale went down the four steps at the ninth bell, which is not her bell, and came back, and gave the room what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, and entered it as nothing.

At the sixth bell Dalla Sen read the day's figure and the total out loud, and then read the count, and gave the reason herself, and the reason was that a room which has just stopped a pump has to be told two things it already knows and one thing it has never been told.

"**Four today and three hundred and four since the keeper signed. The boundary is at four hundred and twenty-one and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.**"

"**And the count, which is a number of days and not a number of anything, and which is not going to be one hundred and forty-seven.**" She turned the slate over. "**One hundred and forty-six days and one hundred and forty-six nights into a count that is his own, and I have been promising this room for forty-four weeks that I would go on writing it down, and nobody has thanked me in forty-four weeks, and I am not going to stop, and this morning I read it out because a pump in this room stopped of its own accord and the least I can do is say one thing I know out loud.**"

The board was not walked, and the reason was entered, and the reason was that a pump has stopped and a clerk does not walk a column in the same hour as a pump.

The crew are at seventy-five and a half and did not move, and the reason for the day is in the book, and the reason is that five men who have not been asked in nineteen weeks cannot be asked on a morning when the thing they would be asked about is a line of chalk that a man has not looked at since yesterday afternoon.

At the seventh bell the anteroom was a room with a hole in the corner of its floor and a trestle with a book on it, and a pump in the middle of the floor that had run for nineteen weeks and had not run for six hours, and a line of chalk on the head of its own frame, and a hand's breadth under it a second mark that nobody can account for and that nobody in that room has touched. Behind the party wall there were five and a half shores and a spreader and a sixth place with nothing in it and a brace of a school's own beech and a bad repair on one face only and a keeper's nine. On the far side of two rooms off the far end of that anteroom there were six people who are not this building's and a door that latches and a corner that has been quiet for six hours for the first time in a fortnight.

And the water-carrying term of a boundary that will not say how many people are inside it was six pails a day, and the pot was one day, and the corridor opens again on Wednesday, and a piece of paper went up the middle passage on Monday with a day of the week on it and no date, and a book in a coat has a line in its top corner that is the only date in this building.
