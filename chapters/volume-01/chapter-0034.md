# Chapter 34 — Work That Outlives Us

The thing that got built in the second week was a drain in a school corner, and it took four people, three days, and the last of the boundary's grease.

It started because Isel Ferrow asked to be taught the water mark, and because Jo said no.

"You have been in a bed for two months," she said. "You read a number off a stone that a fourteen-year-old reads off a stone. If I teach you the mark, and you get it wrong, then a flood happens on a day when you are the only one who is not doing anything else, and it will be your fault, and you will be twenty-one and you will still be the man who got the mark wrong. So no."

"That is not an argument," Isel said. "That is you protecting me from being useful, which is the same thing you did with the register boxes and it is why people here do not tell you things until the work is nearly over."

"I am protecting you from being a load in somebody's plan," Jo said, and then stopped, because she had said it twice now in a fortnight and both times it had come out of her as a rule and not as an explanation.

"Fine," Isel said. "Then I will do the boring part. I will sit here and call the mark out loud every half hour and you will check me, and in a month I will be the second pair of eyes and not the first, and you will have trained me without betting a flood on me."

Which is what happened, over nine days, and it took nine days because Jo checked him nine times and he was wrong twice, and she said both of them out loud, which is how he learned that getting it wrong was survivable.

Then Ilsa Norr said the thing that turned nine days into a project.

"The school corner floor," she said, "has grit in it."

Everyone looked at the floor. It was stone, swept, and looked like stone.

"Not this week," she said. "Since the fourth week. There is grit coming up through the joint by the store wall and through the joint under the bench, and it comes up in the dry, and it comes up in the wet, and it comes up a little more every week, and I have nine children sitting on that floor and ten on market days and they are on it four hours at a time, and I have watched a child put his hand flat on the joint to feel where the grit was." She put her own hand flat on the joint. "There is grit under this. Something under this floor is being ground, or is being crushed, or is coming apart, and I have asked twice and been told that the school corner is a waiting room and not a work area."

"The school corner is a floor over a room," Marek said. "Do you know what is under it?"

"It is not my floor to open," Ilsa said. "It is my children on it. Those are different and I would like somebody to keep them different."

Pell read the joint from the bench before anyone had a lamp on it, because he had spent a fortnight listening to the plinth and had started, without permission and without saying so, to read floors.

"The fall is wrong," he said. "Water should go to the channel that runs to the corridor. It is going the other way. The grit is coming *up* through the low joint because whatever is under this floor is pushing air, and the air carries the grit, and the air comes from the drain." He looked up. "It is not a hole. It is a floor with a mouth in it."

Nobody had any material.

That was the first day's whole content, and Tams put it on the board in four lines: *two sound boards for the frame of a cover; a length of drawn iron rod, and there is one, and it belongs to the baffle; the last of the boundary grease, which Jo has been hoarding for a hinge; and the stone.*

"We have stone," Jo said.

"We have eleven feet of parapet slab with *removed for safety* on it," Dalla said, before anybody else, "and we are not building on it."

"Then we do not use that. We use what is under the floor." Jo got down on her knees at the joint with a lamp and a flat bar and came back with grey dust and a chip of dressed stone about the size of a thumbnail, and held it up. "That is the channel. It has been washed out from underneath and the fill has gone, exactly like the corridor at the waist, and it has been washing out for a long time because nobody has ever opened this floor. It is the same repair the corridor got. It wants a kerb, a fall, a cover, and a way to clear it without two people and a board."

"Which is a day and a half," Iven said, "and a plate of grease, and a piece of iron, and a board, and I can tell you the whole of it before we start, and I would like the price of it said out loud before we start, because I have watched four projects in this building die of a cost that nobody said while they were enjoying themselves."

They said it out loud, and Dalla wrote it, and it was a day and a half of Jo's shutter route, which put the household's shutter another week late; the whole of the boundary's grease, which was the grease for the boundary gate's lower hinge, which had been squeaking since the second month and which Sefu had been meaning to have done; one sound board off Jo's shutter stock, which Jo released herself and said was hers to give; one sound board out of the boundary work area, which Mikka released and asked to have counted as her third; and one length of drawn iron rod, which Nera released herself, and only after she said what it would cost.

"That rod is the baffle's retaining pin," she said. "I made the baffle's pin in the fourteenth day out of that rod because the original was gone. If Jo takes it for a lift bar, then the baffle has no spare, and if the baffle's pin lets go, the baffle is loose in the frame, and the baffle is the thing that stops the pump chamber's air going into the anteroom. So the cost of a school corner drain is that the air baffle will have to be rebuilt with a piece of something that is not iron, and I do not have a piece of something that is not iron, and that is a debt and not a solution."

"Recorded as released by Nera Oduya for the school corner cover, with the baffle's consequence in the same line," Dalla said, and Nera read it back and marked it, which was her practice now too, and which she had picked up from Sefu and never said she had picked it up from Sefu.

Iven built the lift bar on the second morning, and it was a piece of the drawn rod bent over a form, and the form was a piece of the channel's curve cut out of the fall's own broken stone, and the bend was made over that form in eleven strokes with a shoe and a hammer and a great deal of swearing on Jo's part, and the bar came up with a flat lip at one end and a loop at the other and did not look like anything that had been important two days earlier.

"That is the air-baffle method," Nera said, and then stopped, because she had not expected to hear her own method come out of a different pair of hands in a different room for a different reason.

"It is a bend," Iven said. "It is your bend and I have watched you do it twice, and I could not have told you the reason for it, and if you are not going to say so then I will say it: you bend it over the thing that is the same shape as the hole, because then the bar is the shape of the hole and not the shape of your hand. Every carpenter knows it. You just have to be less proud of knowing it than I am."

Jo set the kerb in the fourth hour of the first day, and did it badly, and took it out, and did it again, and Marek watched her do the second one and understood that he was seeing the same competence he had been trying to hire for six weeks and could not buy, and that it belonged to a woman who repaired shutters.

"Under the bench is not the worst of it," she said on the second day, with the boards up. "The worst of it is here." She was on her side with the lamp in her teeth, at the far end of the joint, where the floor met the store wall. "Whoever built this floor did not lay it level. It falls the wrong way, back toward the store, and there is a gap under the wall plate about as wide as two fingers, and it goes down into a void, and that void is not the channel. The void is under the old wash room."

The old wash room. The wash room with a door made of two-thirds of a stair's lower receiving landing, cut in three, in the ninth week of the long cold year, to a wash door, on the account of a woman named R. Vale senior.

Nobody said it. Then everybody said it.

"That is my mother's wall," Renn said from the doorway.

"It is a wall," Jo said. "I do not know whose it is and I am not going to stand in a hole and guess. I am telling you there is a void under the far end of this floor, and the void is bigger than the channel, and the channel is the low point, and the void is a draught."

Then it was a two-day job, and the second day's argument was about how wide the cover should be.

"Two boards," Jo said. "One each side of the channel, and they lift, and it is a lid, not a grate, and you can get a lamp down the hole and a hand and a bar, and that is what it is for."

"It is an air hole," Nera said, from the doorway, which she had come to and was standing in as a visitor. "I want to be exact, because I am going to be asked later and I would rather be exact now. I am not saying the cover is wrong. I am saying the cover is doing two jobs and only one of them is on the slate. It is keeping grit out of a hole in a floor where nine children sit. And it is also closing a mouth. And the air in this building comes up out of drains, and I have a valve board that says which ones I have watched for two months, and the school corner floor is not on it. It has never been on it. I am saying that out loud now so that nobody is surprised in a month when the thing I have not been watching turns out to be the thing that is doing it."

"Write it on the board," Jo said, "as a thing I have to check, not as a thing you have told me to check. I do not mind checking it. I mind being told by an air engineer to stop maintaining a room, and I am not going to be."

"Write it as a thing you have to check," Nera agreed, "and I will put the air note beside it in my own hand and the two of you can argue about whose name is on what."

They argued about it for an hour. The outcome was a routine, and the routine was the second half of the day's work, and it was Mikka's idea and not Jo's.

"You are all teaching the crew," Mikka said. "Jo teaches the pump. Iven teaches the bend. The engineer teaches the channel. Very good. Now: who teaches this when the six of you are on a stair with a cord across it, or under a stone, or arguing with a guild in a room I have not been in?"

Nobody answered, because the answer was that nobody would.

"Fine." She took a slate. "Then here is what it is. Every hour somebody looks at the water mark and says the number out loud. Every day somebody at the second bell and somebody at the fifth bell lifts the school corner cover on the bar and looks down it with a lamp and says *dry* or *wet* or *grit*, out loud, to a person, and not to a board. Every day of the week, named, in a rota, and the rota is a list of people and not a list of jobs, because a list of jobs gets done by whoever is nearest and a list of people gets done by the person who said they would." She held the slate out. "Iven teaches the bend to two people. Jo teaches the lift to two people. Isel has the mark and is not to be first at anything for a month. And Ilsa has the floor and the cover and the word, because it is her room and her children."

They wrote the rota on Jo's board, in six hands, and Dalla copied it into the ledger, and the first day's shift was done by Mikka and a woman from the wash rooms who had not said a word in six weeks, and who lifted the cover on the bar, and looked down it with a lamp, and said *dry*, and went away.

Isel was the worst at the mark. On the last morning he called it high once and low once, and got it right on the third, and Jo wrote all three on the slate in her own hand and did not rub any of them out, and Isel looked at the slate for a while and said, "That is a thing I can learn." And Jo said, "It is, and it will be a month before you are first at anything, and I will be here for it. And I have written down the two you got wrong, so that when somebody comes in here in two years and says you read it right the first time, there is a record that says otherwise."

And on the last day of the job, when the cover was set and the kerb was dressed and the fall went the right way for the first time in forty years, and the grit stopped coming up, and Jo's day and a half and the shutter's extra week and the baffle's missing pin were all in the ledger in five hands, Mikka stopped Ilsa on the stair and said something that made Ilsa go and get the ledger back out.

"Write it as theirs," Mikka said. "Not as work the crew did. The crew carried the litter and stood where I told them and Iven bent a rod and Jo cut a kerb, and Marek said four sentences of which one was the right one, and it took him nine minutes to say it in words instead of a drawing, so write *Marek Venn: stated the fall aloud, nine minutes, and did not draw it*."

Ilsa wrote it that way, and read it back, and then took the chalk and put one more line under it in her own hand, which was the refusal, and which was the thing that had been true all along.

"This is not the crew's repair and it is not on their list," she wrote, "and it is not going on a list of things the crew maintains, because the crew will not be here when the bar rusts, and the person who rusts the bar is the person who opens the cover at the second bell, and she is one of mine."

The party came in. The anteroom had its ten. The corridor had closed twice over the dip and opened twice, and the pump had run its twenty-six and its six and its nineteen, and the school corner cover sat flush in a dressed kerb with two lift holes and a bar that did not rattle, and four people in this building could now do a thing the crew could not do alone and would not be here to do.

Marek stood at the end of it with his slate, and there was nothing on it to add.
