# Chapter 55 — A Courier's Order

The paper was not new, which was the first thing Talla Roke said about it, and she said it standing at the bench with the satchel still on her shoulder, before she sat down, before she took anything off, and before anybody had offered her anything.

"It has been folded and unfolded eleven times and it is a month old," she said, "and it is not mine, and I have carried it for thirty-one days, and I have not opened it under anybody's roof until now because a courier who opens an order in somebody else's house has had it in somebody else's house. So somebody is going to have to ask me to open it, and I would like that asked for, out loud, with a name, because I have learned that in this business you do not get to be helpful later about something you were careless with early."

"Sefu Aran," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel, "boundary keeper, and I am asking you to open it, and I am asking it in front of these people, and I am not asking what is in it, and I am asking you to read it out in your own words and then hand it to my clerk, and she will take a copy, and the copy is ours, and the original is yours, and if you want it back after she has copied it you have it back before anybody argues with it."

She gave it back to herself for about four seconds, and then gave it to Dalla Sen, who took it and read it out.

*Ordered by the route-council clerk of Hearthhold Middle, on the fourth of last month, and witnessed: that the fallen parapet slab of eleven feet and the bulk public-works stores held under the air engineer's inventory in the Hearthhold boundary be released to the Middle works, and delivered to the Middle water-lock at the second gate, and there received by the Middle water-lock keeper, whose name is Corin Bexley; and that the said stores be moved by a courier's own hands and not by persons of the boundary; and that the costs of the move be carried by the Middle council and not by the boundary.*

"There is no name on the bottom," Dalla Sen said.

"There is a mark on the bottom," Talla said, "which is a clerk's mark and not a name, and a mark is a thing you can hold, and I have held it for thirty-one days, and I have thought about that mark every day of the thirty-one."

---

The second person came down the nine steps eleven minutes behind her and had been standing at the bottom of them, out of the light, the whole time, which two people had noticed and neither had said anything about, because the anteroom in the ninth week had learned what to do with a stranger who has not been asked for and is not going to leave.

"Corva Nye," she said. "Cartwright route-custody clerk, out of the Marrowgate clearance office, and I am here as an inventory witness and not as anybody's courier, and I want that said first because I have got a badge and a coat and people are going to look at the coat and decide what I am."

"You are here as an inventory witness," said Sefu Aran, "and I am the keeper, and you are welcome in my boundary and you are not a person of it, and you cannot hold a watch, and you cannot be on a rota, and I would like both of those said out loud now rather than discovered at the fourth bell."

"I would like that too," Corva Nye said, and said it as though she had been expecting it, and Dalla Sen wrote it down in her own hand and read it back, and it is the first entry in that ledger in nine weeks that has the word *witness* in it.

---

It was Mikka Hale who found the shape of it, and she did it by refusing to be angry, which was the worst thing she could have done to a room full of people who had been ready to be angry.

"Eleven feet of stone and some sticks," she said, with her hands on the shelf, "has come down nine steps into a room with a pot in it. And I am not going to be angry at a courier. You have walked thirty-one days. Sit down. There is a pot on and it is not a wage and it is not for you and you can have it if you want it, and I am telling you it is not a wage so that nobody in this room can say later that I fed the woman who came with a piece of paper."

"Thank you," said Talla Roke, and sat down on the end of the bench nearest the threshold, with her back to the wall, which is where a courier sits.

And then Iven Tallo sat down on the table in front of her, which he had not done in nine weeks, and put his hands on his knees, and said:

"Now I am going to tell you what that paper does, and I am going to tell you in the plainest words I have, because you have walked thirty-one days to find out and you can have it for nothing.

"That slab is eleven feet long and a foot thick. I have asked for it twice in this building and Dalla Sen has refused it three times, and the third time was to me, by name, with my name written under the refusal, and I asked for it because I wanted to pack the lip of that stair and a man died under the lip and I have not been right about it since. The reason she gives is not moral. It is a guild salvage lien that claims priority in identified material. The moment that slab carries a load in a Hearthhold work, it is material in use under a lien, and every hand that touches it after that is a hand that can be asked in a hearing who authorised it.

"And you have a written order. Which is the one thing in this building that nobody has ever seen, and Iven's condition from the twelfth day says that a courier who wants the bulk tools moved gives a written order saying where they go and who receives them, and you have given it, and it is lawful, and it is exactly the paper we told you to bring. So the paper is good. The paper is so good that it is the danger."

Talla Roke did not say anything for a moment. She turned her cup a quarter turn on the bench.

"Say the last part slower," she said.

"The order is good and the order is the danger," Iven said, "because it does what it says. It takes eleven feet of stone out of a boundary that has no timber over six feet, and it takes the pry bar and the wedges and the jack's shelf off a register that says guild-frozen, and it hands all of it to the Middle water-lock, and it does all of that lawfully, on a clerk's mark, carried by a courier's own hands.

"And in about nine months, when the first of your own people in that boundary asks the Middle council where the stone came from, somebody is going to look at that order and see that it says the *Hearthhold boundary*, in writing, on a mark. And then this boundary is not a place that had a bad day in the ninth week. This boundary is a place that sold something."

The anteroom was very quiet. Somewhere up the nine steps a boy with an ear on a stone was not hearing any of it.

"Say what you want it for," Sefu Aran said. "Not what the paper says. What you want it for, in your own words, and I am asking it as a keeper and not as an officer."

Talla Roke put the cup down.

"Water," she said. "Middle's water lock is dry and it has been dry since before I was a courier, and the council has spent four years patching it with stone and prayer, and the second gate's families have been carrying water up four sets of steps since the cold started. I have two brothers on that list and a sister who is eleven. And a slab eleven feet long and a foot thick is a sill, and a sill is what a lock that cannot hold water is missing. I am not a carpenter and I know that. I have asked a carpenter. I was told a sill and a bed and a wedge of hardwood and two hands a day for four days, and I looked at the two hands a day and I looked at the stores in this building and I thought: there is no timber here, and there is a slab there, and I am thirty-one days out of the middle, and the order was made a month ago and I have been carrying it ever since while I have looked for a cheaper way."

"And you have not found one," said Nera Oduya.

"I have found three," Talla Roke said, "and all of them were worse. The cheap one was to take it and say nothing and hope. The second one was to take the pry bar and the wedges and sell them on to a crew at the third gate and put the money against the sill. The third one was to wait for winter and drink."

---

Marek Venn had been standing up since the word *sill*, and everybody in the anteroom could see it, and the anteroom let him get eight words out before it stopped him, which was eight words more than it would have given him in the seventh week.

"Eleven foot of stone, a bed, a hardwood wedge—"

He sat down, and the sitting was not the hardest part.

The hardest part was the half second before, when a woman on a bench with a courier's satchel said the word *sill* and he was already standing and had not decided to stand. Ten weeks. In the seventh week Nera caught him at a valve and made him say what a work was. In the eighth week a boy on a crutch said out loud in front of eleven people that the record had six names in a column and no line about what happened to the six of them, and that somebody better say it before somebody else said it for them, and Marek had gone into that room meaning to say it and had not got there first.

And now he was on his feet in a room that was not his, over a boundary's slab, for a boundary's sister, on the strength of a word he had heard once.

There was a version of him — he could draw it, he had drawn it in his head about four hundred times in ten weeks — that did not stand up. That version listened to the whole of it first, and asked what the middle gate's water lock was actually failing on, and found out whether four households carried it themselves or whether somebody was carrying it for them, and worked out by the second hour that a courier's order for a slab is not a request for a slab and never has been.

He had never once been that version. He was not sure that version existed, and the not being sure had been fine for thirty-two years and was starting, in the tenth week, to feel like a professional condition rather than a personality.

"He is about to do the thing he always does," Jo said, not unkindly, to the room rather than to him, "and I would like it said out loud so that it is in the air and not only in his head. He has just heard that a boundary has no timber, and that a sill can be made out of a slab, and that two families are carrying water up four steps. And he is thinking: the crew's hours. Thirty-eight and a half, given, owed to nobody, and a named work, and a person who has asked. He is thinking that Iven could cut a bed and Jo could pack it and four people could carry it and it would take four days and it would not be ours and it would not be claimed and it would be the first work this building has ever done for another Hearthhold in its life."

"That is not a bad thought," said Sefu Aran.

"It is a *terrible* thought," Jo said, "and it is bad in a specific way, and the specific way is this. The crew's hours cannot be spent on a work in another Hearthhold. They are hours given by five people who are going to leave, and Tams's rule is that they are not to be spent on anything without a named work and a person who has asked — and the person who has asked is the keeper, and the keeper is you, and if you say yes then the crew's hours go down a road to the second gate and they are gone, and this boundary has a lip with no post and a seat with two cleanings on it and a bell rope and a check that hisses."

"I did not say it out loud," Marek said.

"You do not have to," Jo said. "You have never once had to. That is the entire problem and it has been the entire problem for ten weeks."

---

Dalla Sen read the order a second time, and then she read the part of it that mattered, which was the middle of the third line, and she read it twice, and then she said the thing that turned the morning.

"That line says the costs of the move are carried by the Middle council and not by the boundary," she said. "Which is a good line, and it is a line somebody in the Middle wrote carefully, and I want it read out that way, because I have watched this room get ready to be angry at a woman for handing over a paper and the paper is the only thing standing between that slab and my ledger.

"Which is the other half of the answer, and the half that nobody wants. The Middle council has already decided that this boundary's stores are not its own. There is a mark at the bottom of that paper that says the parapet slab and the bulk stores *held under the air engineer's inventory in the Hearthhold boundary* are its own, and the clerk who wrote it did not write it out of malice. She wrote it out of the register. The register says the tools are public-works equipment on a guild-frozen line, and the boundary received them, and the boundary does not own them, and the boundary cannot sell them, and so the clerk looked at the register and found the only name on it."

"And the name was ours," said Nera Oduya, from the valve. "Because the inventory is mine, and there is nobody else's, and I put the number on the end of that length myself and I said so in the ledger on the day it came up. So the register has a name on it. It has my name on it. And that is the answer to the question you are all about to ask me, so I would like to give it before it is asked: **if anybody ever comes with a piece of paper wanting what is under my inventory, the first thing they will take is not the stone. It will be the fact that there is a name in this building.**

"That is not a reason to open the register to everybody," she said, "and I want that in the same breath, because the second half of that sentence is a lie if the first half is standing by itself. It is a reason to decide, on purpose, in this room, what the register says and who may read it, before somebody forty days away decides it for us. And I am not having that conversation this week, because this week I have a check that hisses, a courier on a bench who does not know what a check is, and a stone I have no authority over."

---

Corva Nye had not said anything for a long time, and when she did, she said it to Sefu Aran and not to the room, and she said it in the voice of a woman doing a job she did not enjoy doing and would do properly.

"Keeper, I have to put one question in my report and it is the only question my form lets me put, and I am going to put it now rather than at the end, so that you can answer it in front of witnesses and I can write down that witnesses were present.

"**Who maintains the route.**"

And Sefu Aran read it out, in his own words, the way he read everything that came off that page, and Dalla Sen marked his paragraph separately as she always did.

"That the stair in the Hearthhold boundary is a route maintained by the people whose rooms it is under, and is not held by anybody. That the maintenance is in Renn Vale's name and is a road and not a wall, and that the line that holds the work is the boundary's own line from the boundary's own store, released by its keeper for a named work, and re-tied by the keeper and Renn Vale and nobody else. That the work was done by people from above who do not live here, are not on the rota, are not a maintenance party, and whose hours were given and are owed to nobody. That the five of them hold no document, no seal, no receipt, no claim, and no part of it. And that no person may add a name to it."

Corva Nye wrote it down. It took her a while, and she wrote it in her own hand, and she did not abbreviate it, and when she had finished she read it back and then said:

"That is the answer to the question I have to ask. And it is not an answer my form can hold. I have written this form forty-one times in six years and the space it gives me for a route is a line for a holder and a line for a charter and a line for the date it was inspected, and I have just tried to put nine weeks of a Hearthhold into three lines and I could not do it, and the reason I could not do it is that the thing you have built has no holder, and a form with no place to write a holder does not have a place to write a keeper, and my office will read that as a route with nobody on it."

"Then read it as a route with nobody on it," said Renn Vale, from the upper landing, where she had come to stand without anybody asking her to and without going any further down than that.

"I am going to," said Corva Nye. "That is not the part I am worried about. The part I am worried about is that in about four years there will be a clerk like me with a form like mine and a keeper like you, and it will be much easier to fill in three lines if somebody is holding it. And I would like it written into my own report — my own hand, in the margin, in a form that does not have a box for it — that a Cartwright clerk of the Marrowgate clearance office, on a morning in the tenth week, in the anteroom, in front of eleven people, was told by a boundary keeper that a route is maintained by its neighbours and held by nobody, and understood it, and wrote it down, and thinks it is the best answer she has ever been given."

"Write it," said Sefu Aran. "And Dalla will copy it, and the copy goes in your file and the original goes in mine, and if the two ever disagree then the one in your file is the true one, because yours was written first and I am not going to pretend I could not have been warned."

And that page, in a Cartwright clerk's own hand, in the margin of a form with three lines for a route, is the first page in forty days of walking that has put the name of this stair in a guild office anywhere.

And at the top of nine steps, Pell Roke had not moved, and had not come down, and a courier who had walked thirty-one days had looked up at the top of nine steps and asked her brother one question, which was: "Are they feeding you."

And he said: "I am on the count."

And she said: "That is not an answer to my question."

And he said: "It is on the rota in Mikka's hand and it is not a duty, it is a debt, and I asked which of the two it was and Tams said a debt, and you can ask him, he is there," and pointed with his chin at the tally board, and Tams Quill looked up from a column of figures and said, "A debt," and went back to his column, and Dalla Sen wrote all four of those lines down in the ledger because they were said, and read them back, and nobody in the anteroom said one word about the rest of it.
