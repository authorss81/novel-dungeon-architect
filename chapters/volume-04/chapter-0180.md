# Chapter 180 — The Order, And The Count

The order came up the nine steps at the first bell on the first day of the thirty-fifth week, a Monday, behind six people, and the six were not in a hurry, and that was the first thing Marek Venn noticed, because a man in a hurry on nine steps is a man who has decided where the room is. The school did not run and lost nothing.

They set six timber shores on the anteroom floor in a row with their heads the same way up, a count-book on the trestle, a folding table, and their own lamps down and unlit. The man who came up last stopped at the top of the steps, looked at the party wall for about four seconds, and said it out loud before he had read the sheet, which is how a competent man tells a room he is not interested in their opinion.

"**That is a service void and it is a bad one, and I have shored nine of them, and yours is the second worst.**"

"**What you are, and before anybody touches the sheet,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**Warden-Officer Ansel Karr, of the Warden-General's office. I brought the sheet and six people. I did not write it and I am the man who has to stand in the room while it is read.**" He put the sheet on the table face up, the way a courier does. "**The Warden-General is Cassian Rook. I am telling you that first because the name is not on the sheet, and a room that has to find a name in a line at the bottom has been reading the wrong part.**"

"**All of it,**" said Dalla Sen. "**The three numbered things, then whatever is on it that is not numbered, then the seal number twice, because a seal number is a thing a room will be asked about in a year.**"

Karr read it.

"**One. The room at the kitchen-line end is to be emptied on the next open morning, and the four rooms of this boundary held as one space while it is empty. Two. The count of that space is to be taken by this crew and entered in the district book, and this boundary is not to enter a competing figure. Three. The party wall is to be shored from the common landing side, and the timber charged to the district and not to the boundary.**"

Dalla Sen nodded once and did not write.

"**The unnumbered part,**" she said.

"**Unnumbered, in his own hand.**" Karr took his time about it. "**A decision without a responsible operator becomes an accident. I am not asking whether you want this. I have done it, and I am telling you what it will cost, and the cost is a figure in a book that is not yours. You may keep your page and I would expect you to.**"

Bel Ordry read the three numbered things and the unnumbered sentence and the seal number off the sheet, word for word, and Dalla Sen asked Karr whether that was what he had said, and he said it was, and she entered that it was and that she had asked for the check.

She entered the rest of it as it stood: that the sheet is a Warden-General's instruction and not a Compact form and not a guild instrument, and that it is three things numbered and one sentence not numbered, and that the unnumbered one is the reason the other three were written.

Then she read the sheet again, and this time she said it as a clerk and not as a reader, and the room heard the difference.

"**And the finding, and I am entering it before anybody objects, because the objection is coming and I want it in the book with a time on it.**" She had the chalk down. "**This boundary has spent thirty-four weeks refusing a post on a form. Three times it was offered two lines and three times the lines were not filled in. On Saturday a form with two lines filled in correctly was seen at the middle gate, in a competent hand, about a road that is not ours.**"

Renn Vale did not sit down either.

"**Go on, and tell this room what you told the sheet.**"

"**And on Friday a woman on this landing said that the cheapest standing anybody has ever handed a man is a reason to go and look at things, and a man said he did not want it, and I entered that he did not want it.**" Dalla Sen put the chalk on the anvil end of the trestle. "**And this morning a sheet came down the middle passage with three things on it, the first of them being that we do a piece of work. It is done. The timber is already on our floor. The operator is named and it is not anybody in this room.**"

The finding she entered next is the one the hand-off will carry: that the first post this boundary has ever had arrived in somebody else's handwriting, was four days up the middle passage, and was not asked for.

"**That entry is a knife with the handle in your hand,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and everybody in this room can see it, and I am not going to be the one who says nothing about it.**"

"**The half against it is that the first thing on the sheet is that we empty a room nine children are taught in, the second is that a stranger counts us while it is empty, and the third is that the stranger pays for the timber. That is the most competent document anybody has put in front of this boundary in thirty-four weeks, and I am not going to pretend otherwise to make the rest of it easier.**" She picked the chalk up again. "**And the half against the half against it is that this is work this building would have had to do, and somebody four days up a passage did it first, and the difference is not competence. We were going to do it badly, and late, and on our own money.**"

Nobody improved on that, and Iven Tallo asked the only question anybody asked for about ten minutes, which was about the timber.

"**Where it came from, and how long it is,**" he said.

"**Standing stores at the middle gate. Six shores, seasoned, four years, on a rack for a district that did not ask for them. I chose them myself, and I am telling you I chose them, because a man who says the office provided them is a man who does not know what he is looking at.**"

"**Then what you are looking at.**" Iven Tallo had come over from the anvil end without anybody having noticed him do it.

"**Four years, dry, feet cut square, and no iron in them, which is a fault and is also the reason I chose them.**"

"**The fault, and then the reason, and then what you would have done with iron. I am a carpenter and I want to know if you know.**"

"**A shore with no foot plate crushes its own mark into a floor and takes the floor with it when it lets go.**" Karr did not look away. "**The reason is that I have four hours and six people, and the other way of doing this job needs a plate I would have to forge, and I would rather crush a floor on a common landing than send a man back four days for iron. With iron I would have had foot plates, two, a spreader on the head of the sill, and a shore a person could take out with a wedge in four minutes instead of forty. A man who wants to criticise the choice has three days and a district store and can have the iron, and I would rather be wrong in four hours than right in three days.**"

"**That is the correct answer and I have never once given it,**" said Iven Tallo. "**And I am not going to say the rest of what I am thinking, and this room is not getting it out of me this morning.**"

Then the work was done by six people who had not been asked, in nineteen minutes, and Marek Venn found that the most useful thing he could do with his whole morning was to stand out of the way of it and be right about where not to put a shore.

The nine children were in the room at the kitchen-line end at the first bell on Friday, and were not in it this morning, and will not be in it on the next. The four rooms of the network were held as one space by an arrangement nobody in this building had written. The air engineer stood at the top of the nine steps for all of it with her dyed cloth out, and said four numbers, and did not say a fifth.

The shores went up on the common landing side, which is the one side a shore can be put on, because the landing is the only place in this building with a floor at the right height, and because nobody had ever thought of the landing as a place a wall could be propped from. That is a sentence about a floor and not about a hole in a wall.

Marek Venn put his left hand on the head of the third shore and held it while four other people set the foot, and the left hand did not close, and the hold was coarse and it worked, and nobody watched him do it and he was not thanked.

The only instruction he gave in nineteen minutes was that no shore was to be set on the kitchen-line side of the wall. Karr asked him why, and he said because a shore on that side goes through the room nine children are taught in and a shore on the other side does not, and Karr said *that is correct* and moved on, and that was the whole of what he said for the morning.

And then the count.

"**What you are about to do, and what it is for, and to the room and not to me,**" said Dalla Sen. "**Because a count taken from outside this building ends up in somebody else's book, and I am not having that happen to a room in a corridor.**"

Karr turned round and said it to the room.

"**We are going to count everybody in the four rooms and the number goes in the district book. It is a number of bodies at a moment on a morning. It is not a permission, it is not a rate, and it is not going to be anything else. Tomorrow it is wrong, and that is the correct life of a figure of that kind, and I am telling you so now so that nobody in a year says a Warden sent down a number about these rooms and held that people live here.**"

Sefu Aran stood at the top of the nine steps through the whole of the count with his hands behind him and said nothing, and Dalla Sen entered that the elected keeper of this boundary stood at the top of nine steps while six strangers counted the people in his rooms and did not open his mouth. Nobody objected. She asked whether the boundary may enter a competing figure, and Karr read the second numbered thing again out loud, which is the answer, and she entered it.

Then the air engineer, who had been at the top of the nine steps for nineteen minutes, said the sentence she had written in a room a fortnight ago, in her own words, in the middle of a working morning, with a stranger's count-book open on a table eleven feet from her.

"**My condition is that I will not certify a building that has said no to nothing.**" Nera Oduya did not come down the steps. "**And this boundary has said no to nothing this week. It has not been asked to say yes to anything, and it has said yes to a piece of timber that was on the floor before anybody could refuse it, and I do not know which of those is a yes.**"

"**The other half of that,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**The half against it is that a boundary handed timber it did not ask for and a count it did not ask for has not said yes to anything, and a person who says so has found a way of keeping the sentence she wrote, and I do not trust myself with a sentence this month.**" She put the cloth away. "**So I am not asking this room to think I have been cheated out of my own condition, and I am not going to be relieved about it either, and I would like that entered in that order.**"

Dalla Sen entered it in her words, both, in that order, and entered that nobody was consoled, and entered that the air engineer has not been asked about her first condition or her second by anybody in this room and that she has not asked.

Then Bel Ordry raised the thing nobody had raised, and he raised it carefully, in the shape of a man who has learned to raise things in this building.

"**Plainly,**" said Renn Vale, "**and do not soften it.**"

"**There is a person in this building whose air a bigger office has just had counted by a stranger's crew, and who is going to be asked to sit at a table about a district, and I am not going to be the one who says that out loud if nobody else will, so I am going to say it.**" Bel Ordry had his hands at his sides. "**That is not leaving. I want that said. Nobody is going anywhere. But a person who certifies air for a district a Warden's instruction has just been written about is a person who is going to be asked to be in a room with a seal on it, and the asking will not be a question.**"

Dalla Sen did not ask for the rest of it. She waited.

Nera Oduya looked at the fourth bell and then at the count-book on the table and said nothing at all for long enough that the pump got a stroke in, and then she said:

"**No.**"

Dalla Sen entered it in her words, and entered that it is the whole of it and that she gave no reason, and entered that nobody asked her for one, and entered that nobody asked her whether she is going anywhere either, because a man with a page in a coat has already said in a room that that is not the question being asked.

Nobody asked her for one. Mero Senn said the sentence once and did not say it again, and the room let it stand.

And then Renn Vale came to the trestle and did the thing that had been owed since the fourth day, and she did it in front of the Warden officer and did not apologise for it.

"**In front of a stranger,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**Because a comparison read out in a room with a stranger's paper on the table is not a finding, it is a position, and this room has been waiting four days to make a position and I am not going to let it make one by accident.**" She squared the printed leaf, squared the page in the coat beside it, and ruled one line under both with a piece of chalk. "**There. That is the whole of it, and I have written nothing under it, and the line is not in the plan and it is not on the leaf and it is on this table only.**"

"**Say what is owed,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**A comparison, read out loud, in a room, with both documents in it and a second person checking it, said by somebody who is not a keeper and not a clerk and not a man with a page in a coat, in a room that has not had a stranger's seal in it this week.**" She put the chalk down. "**And that room does not exist yet, and I have known that since Saturday and said nothing.**"

"**The half against waiting,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and it is yours to say, because you are the one doing the waiting.**"

"**The half against it is that a room which waits for a clean table makes the finding on somebody else's schedule, and a Warden-General's office has a schedule, and I have seen what a schedule does to a boundary's week.**" She did not look at Karr. "**And the reason I am still doing it is that the other half is worse: a finding made with a stranger's paper on the table is a finding the stranger repeats, and this boundary has spent thirty-four weeks refusing to be repeated.**"

Dalla Sen entered it in her own words: that a comparison is owed, and that two documents are lying on a table under a chalk line with nothing written under the line. And she entered that she has been asked four times this month whether a thing may be joined to another thing and has said no four times, and that she is now putting two documents on one table on purpose, and that this is a different act and she is entering the difference.

At the fourth bell the anteroom's terms were said. The word was said by a person and repeated by the clerk, and the count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change, and the pump was attended, and the outer service door was the one a body went out of, and the fifth term was in a room with both the people it names in it. Nobody read it back, and the clerk entered that a term said with six strangers standing in it is a term a room has to say out loud and not a term it can stop saying.

And the pot was at eight days, the cook said the arithmetic out loud in the middle of all of it and would not say the word, and four households at a second gate still had nothing, with no figure attached to that gate by anybody.

At the sixth bell the clerk entered the day's number and the total in the book and read both of them out loud, and the count was read at one hundred and fifteen, and she walked the board at eighteen entries. One today and two hundred and forty-three since the keeper signed. The boundary is at three hundred and sixty and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, and the two columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen and stays seventeen. She read them out because the sixth bell of a morning when a stranger's count-book has been closed on a table is not the moment to read a total quietly, and because a boundary that has been counted by somebody else is entitled to hear its own figure said in its own room.

Tams Quill had the chalk in his left hand and the right one open and flat on his knee, and he did not read the eighteen in the order they were in, and everybody in the room heard that he was not going to.

"**What you are doing instead,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**One thing, and then the eighteen.**" He put the chalk at the first one. "**The Warden crew's count is not going on this board. That is a number made by six people who are going back up a road on Wednesday. A number on this board is a number this building agreed to, and it can be asked about by somebody who is in this building, and that is the whole of my reason.**"

"**The other part of it,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**And the column is not headed, and it is not headed on a morning when a stranger's seal has been on this table, and I have nothing to say about that and nobody has asked me.**" He read the eighteen. "**Eleven nulls. Then a claim, a mark, a row, a plan, a condition, a store and a brace. Nine in the ordinary column and nine in the one with nothing over the top of it. The claim's condition has not been met and the mark is not coming off.**"

She entered it in his words: that he gave a reason for the nineteenth entry and did not give one for the column, that the board was eighteen before six strangers came through the door and is eighteen now, and that a standing in a district book is not a nineteenth entry on a slate.

The brass survey rule in the pocket of the coat on the hook had said nothing for twenty-six weeks, and the wrapped plate on the shelf above the same bench had been unopened for thirty-five, and neither of them was in a room and neither of them moved.
