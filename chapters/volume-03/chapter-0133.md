# Chapter 133 — Two Lines With A Space In Them

**Open for the corridor on the third day of the twenty-sixth week, a Wednesday, and the school ran for the fifteenth morning in the room at the kitchen-line end, and a courier came up the passage with a sheet of paper in her hand and three people of her own behind her, and she did not come to see anybody.**

**The water was still coming up in three places and had stopped coming up in one of them, and nobody in this building knows which one it stopped in, because the one it stopped in is under a bed joint in a room nobody is using.**

Jo Verrick said the standing term out loud at the first bell in the doorway of the room at the kitchen-line end, the way she has said it every morning since a physician gave it to him and he gave it to her, and then Mero Senn read it out of his own hand against hers, and Ilsa Norr said yes to the reading, and that is the whole of what the practice is and it is a decision on a page in a clerk's hand and it is now standing on a term that belongs to a person and not to a room.

"**Hand on the bed, out loud, in a room, yes or no, and whether anybody is going to be in here. And if the answer is no twice, take the nine of them out and do not wait for me or for anybody in this building.**"

"**The strip is holding,**" said Jo Verrick. "**I have been in it a fortnight and the crate board has not gone and the two flags have not gone and the stone has not moved since the Monday it went over, and the ninth stone is where it was, and I want that said in a room and I want it said by me and not by a man with a rule."

"**And the bed under it is a hand and a half and I can put my arm in to the elbow, and the arm goes in because there is nothing there to stop it, and that has not changed in a fortnight, and a fortnight of a hole not getting worse is not a repair and I am not going to be praised for it.**"

Nine children went in at the fourth bell, and one of them went in sitting down, and the ninth hearth stone had a strip of crate board under two flags of its own and the light in the room was off nine stones, and the cook did not light that hearth.

---

**Talla Roke came up the middle passage on the third day of the twenty-sixth week with a wooden tally in her hand and three people behind her, and she said what she was before anybody asked her, and she said the thing she had come to say inside about a minute.**

She was twenty-two and she had a courier's coat with the road on it and a sheet of paper folded twice in her inside pocket, and Bevin Tace and Osha Kell came up behind her with a hand-cart and Merrit Amm came last and did not stop at the anteroom step.

"**Talla Roke. Courier. My route, my three, my terms. I am not here about the water and I am not here about the school and I am not here about a boy, and I would like all three of those entered before I say anything else, and the third one especially, because there is a boy on a bench at the top of your nine steps and he is my brother and he is not why I am here and nobody in this room is going to use me to get to him.**"

"**The last of those three is the only one I am actually worried about.**"

"**Say what you came to say,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**The form on the board at the gate on the market road has stopped being a form. It is a pass. That is the whole of it and I will give you the rest of it in about a minute because the rest of it is why I am standing in your anteroom instead of on a road."

"**Here is what happened. There is a gate on the road between this boundary and the middle gate, and at that gate there has been a printed sheet on a board for as long as anybody can remember, and the sheet has seven lines and two of them are for a name and for the person responsible for the load, and a clerk there has never had a reason to look at it, because it is a sheet about a load."

"**And then there was water. There is water in your corridor and there is water on a road that I do not have to explain to you. And a clerk at that gate looked at a piece of paper and put a hand on it, and the hand says: if you want to go along this road with more than what you are carrying, then these two lines get filled in."

"**That is all. Nobody wrote a rule. There is no order and there is no notice and there is nothing with a date on it, and it is being done by a competent person who is doing their job in the way their office does their job, and in six weeks it will not be a clerk doing it, it will be the way that road works."

"**And a route that cannot be used by an ordinary resident is not a public route. That is my rule and it is not mine, it is anybody's, and I will say why I hold it: I have run a road for two years for people who do not have a permit and a post and a name anybody can send for, and the day that road needed a line filled in, it stopped being my road and became a line on a board.**"

Dalla Sen had the clerk's chalk out and Nera Oduya had the market form off the anteroom table and face up in front of them both, because it had been face up on that table for a fortnight and had been read in four seconds by a woman from another office, and moving it was a thing a room could do.

"**Read the two lines,**" said Talla Roke, to the top of the nine steps, "**and I am asking you because a man told me on a road that you read numbers and that that is the whole of what you do, and I have never once in three weeks believed a sentence that short, and I am going to find out today."

"**Out loud, and say what they are.**"

Pell Roke had the sheet in his good hand and turned it the right way up. He did not go down the steps and he did not go over the waist and nobody asked him to, and the anteroom was quiet in the way it gets quiet when a fourteen-year-old is being asked to do the thing he is actually for.

"**One is a name,**" he said. "**And one is the person responsible for the load. And there is a space for each of them and both spaces are empty, and I do not know anything about that and I read numbers and that is the end of what I do.**"

"**Thank you,**" said Talla Roke, and she said it at the same volume she had said everything else and she did not go up the steps and she did not look at the bench a second time, and Dalla Sen entered that the room made nothing of it, which is itself an entry, and entered that she entered it on purpose.

---

**Then the two sentences went into a room, and the room found out that a boundary cannot get anybody out of here on its own paper.**

Bel Ordry brought the plan up out of his coat at the second bell in four parts and did not put it on the table, and Talla Roke looked at it and did not touch it and asked what it was.

"**Say the reason out loud before you open it, because that is this building's habit and I would like to be in it for one of them,**" said Bel Ordry, and then he told her: a boundary that keeps a plan and does not put a room on it is a boundary that has decided it does not have to know, and a room with nine children in it had no row on this page until the third day of last month, and the number in the row is a permitted occupancy and not a count of anybody, and a boy of fourteen read that column out loud in a room and said ten.

"**And that is a page that does not leave this building,**" he said. "**That is not a figure of speech. It is in my coat, it is in four parts, two of them in the kitchen and two in a school store under a shelf, and it does not go out of here, and I am the man who keeps it, and I have said out loud in a room that a page in a coat is worth exactly as much as the man in the coat.**"

"**Then you have nothing,**" said Talla Roke, "**that a person at a gate can be given. And I am not being cruel, I am being fast, and I will tell you why I am being fast."

"**The only road out of this boundary for a person who is not one of the five of you is the market road, because the middle passage goes up to a middle gate and a middle gate has an office at it that you have written a charter to and a charter with no name on it is a piece of paper that they have to find a person to put a name on. And the market road has a gate on it, and at that gate there is now a form with two lines and a space in each of them."

"**So the question for this room is not *should we fill in the form*. It is: whose name goes in it, and what title goes against the load, and whether the answer is that no ordinary resident of this Hearthhold can walk out of it without a line filled in by somebody in this room. And that is the question, and it has been the question since the sheet went on the board, and nobody here has been asked it until this week.**"

Renn Vale said the thing that the room had been holding all morning without knowing it.

"**A title. The answer is a title, and that is how a cell with two people in it works out that a title is not a name, and we worked that out because a man in a room four days up this passage wrote *the person who keeps the anteroom's terms* in a field book and a woman from another office could not take it out."

"**And the other answer is a name, and a name on that form is a name on a page about a load, and this boundary has spent twenty-six weeks finding out what a name on a page about a room is for. It is a handle. That is all it is. And a handle is a thing you can pick a person up with.**"

"**Say the third answer,**" said Talla Roke.

Marek Venn had been on the anteroom step for all of it and had not said anything, and he said it now in the shape of a man who has run out of better answers and not in the shape of a man who has a good one.

"**There is a third answer and it is bad and I am going to say it because a room that has only got two answers is a room that picks one of them by Tuesday."

"**The form wants a name and a person responsible for the load. This Hearthhold has forty-one people in it this week, more than usual, because there is water in a corridor. If this boundary fills in that form, then the load is the load of a building and the person responsible for the load is a person, and the person is chosen by us, and every one of the forty-one is on our side of that line by nothing except that they were here."

"**And the moment that line is drawn, this boundary is a place with a person in charge of a load, and I have spent the whole of my working life in the other kind of place. So the third answer is that we do not fill in the form, and the road is not a public route, and the forty-one of us are not going anywhere, and I would like that entered in a clerk's hand with the two and a half feet of water in the picture and not in a nicer light than it deserves."

"**I am not saying the form is wrong. I am saying I can see exactly what it is for and I cannot work out how anybody holds one of these without a person under it.**"

---

**At the sixth bell the clerk read the six things and the day's number and the count, and the two in the column with nothing over the top of it did not move, and the count in the same column did.**

"**Two today and a hundred and fifteen since the keeper signed. The boundary is at two hundred and thirty-two and a quarter, and it adds by the day the work was done, and the two columns reconcile, and they have reconciled for twelve weeks. The crew are at fifty-nine and a half and have not moved since yesterday and the reason is in my hand and it is that somebody said the terms out loud."

"**The pot is ten. The jars are two and I was told four threads this morning and I have entered the four threads because I was told them and the number of jars is a number I hold and not a number I was given, and I would like that distinction to survive this week."

"**The count. Wen Pask is sixty-eight days and sixty-eight nights into a count that is his own, and I read it and nobody said anything and I entered that nobody said anything again, because I am a clerk and that is the whole of what I am for and it is a strange thing to be for."

"**And I am entering one thing that is not one of the six and that I have no category for. A courier came up this passage with a form in her hand and a rule of her own, and the rule is *a route that cannot be used by an ordinary resident is not a public route*, and I have written it into this room's book in her own words because it is a sentence somebody in this building has needed for six weeks and did not have, and I would like it in the book in case anybody tries to take it off her by saying she made it up.**"

Not one person in the anteroom said a kind thing to her and one woman who might have said one did not, and Talla Roke watched the clerk write it down and said, "**Say that again slower when you get to a part I do not understand and I will not be insulted, and I will not correct you.**"

And at the seventh bell she said the last of it in a room with nine people in it, and it was about a courier and not about a rescue and not about her brother and not about the spring.

"**I am going to be here while the road is shut and I am going to be here on a market day, and I have not asked anybody for anything and I am not going to. My three and I have water of our own and we are not in your pot and we are not in your count."

"**And when we do go out of here, we go on my mark and not on yours, because my mark on a paper means it is not a guild's road, and a person walking on mine is not walking on a line somebody in a counter put in for them. That is the only thing I have got and it is not mine either. It is three people's.**"

**The market day was the sixth day of this week and a courier had said in a room that she would be here for it, and the form was still on the table, and a room with a packed sill and a propped hearth stone and a cold hearth had nine children in it and a girl of nine in it, and the water had stopped coming up under a bed joint in a room nobody was using, and nobody knew which of the three places it had stopped in.**
