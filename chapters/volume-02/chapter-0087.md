# Chapter 87 — Forty-Eight and a Half

The keeper asked for the two hours himself, on the second morning of the seventeenth week, in the anteroom, in front of the clerk, at the first bell, on a day when they could be refused, and he did it by name to two men rather than to a crew, which is what the rule allows, and Tams Quill made him do it in that order.

"Terms," said Tams Quill. "Out loud, all of them, before anybody says yes."

"Read them off the slate," said Renn Vale, from the landing, and Dalla Sen read them off Renn's slate, and they were hers and they were the seven terms and the eighth, and the eighth read: *if it turns out to be a door, it is a door in a building I keep and nobody goes down it.*

"And under them," said Sefu Aran, "**and these are mine, and they are the work and not the route, and if I have said them wrong the count is to stop me now.**

"The work is: to stand at the top of the four treads, at the rail, on the far side, with a lamp, in the pump's window, and to look, and to say what can be said, and to come back. Two of you, two hours, in the ordinary column, with a person's name against it in my writing saying *I would like your morning.*

"Not one foot past the rail. Not one foot past the rail on the far side, and the far side is the side with the four treads on it, and the reason I am saying the far side is that if I do not say it then somebody will come back in two hours having gone down and be unable to tell me why they went down, and I would rather have a short report than a long argument.

"And nobody is on a watch and nobody is on a rota, and the school is not coming, and the corridor is shut for its own reasons, and that is the day to do this, and the six minutes after the lever comes off are the six minutes, and there is no crossing this morning and nobody has to be anywhere but the rail."

"Repeated," said Tams Quill, from memory, in his own words, and Dalla Sen read them back and Tams said they were right.

"Two things before anybody says yes," said Renn Vale, from the landing, and the anteroom waited, because the two things are always the price.

"**One: that work is at the rail and not on the stair, and a rail is not a step, and nobody goes past the rail, and the eighth term stands and is not touched by this. And two: that is the first named work anybody in this building has asked of a person in fifteen weeks that is not a chore on a kitchen page.**"

"And what is the other one," said Tams Quill.

"And the other one is me," said Renn Vale. "I have had nothing in every work in this Hearthhold since the fifteenth week. A named work with a person's name against it in the ordinary column is a holder in everything but the spelling, and this room found that out about a piece of paper in one week and I am not going to have it happen to a man in a fortnight.

"So I want what I am about to say entered in my own hand and read out, because I would be the one to stop it and I am not going to be the one to stop it quietly.

"**The stair has been outside every work in this Hearthhold since the fifteenth week, and it is now inside one, and it is inside one for two hours on a Tuesday, and nobody has paid anything and nobody is going to. The price of this is not a thing I can name. The price is that the eighth term and this are now both in the book, in two hands, on the same subject, and they are not in agreement, and a person in a year is going to have to read both of them and be told which of the two is the promise.**"

"Two hours," said Marek Venn, at the bench. "And I am going down because I have to be the one who goes down, and not because the air engineer asked and not because anybody in this room thought of me, and if nobody has thought of me then the count will have my name on the work and not the doing, and I have been a man in this building for fifteen weeks who gets thanked for being in a room, and I would like one morning where the two are the same thing."

"You have had one," said Tams Quill. "The eighth of the thirteenth week. I wrote it in the column with no heading in my own left hand and I have never read it out and I am not going to now, and you know exactly which morning I mean and so does the room."

---

They went down at the second bell, in the pump's window, with the terms read at the school door, and two of the five went. The other three did not, and the anteroom had learned in the thirteenth week not to say anything about it, and it did not, and one of the three had said on the Thursday that he would rather be useful on the landing than be a fourth pair of hands on a rail, and the other two were on a litter and on a crutch, and nobody asked why those two had gone rather than two others, and the two who went were the man who is leaving and the man who writes the figures down.

Bel Ordry had the bar against the throat's north-east cheek at the second bell on the same morning, which was the seventh, and he came up the nine steps with his hands white to the second knuckle and *nothing*, and said the word at the frame before he had his coat off.

"Three places, same cold, same bar," he said. "**And that is the most useful thing I have written down in a fortnight, and I would like somebody to write the word nothing next to the word thing in the same line, because in a year a reader is going to want to know whether the seventh morning went with the other six or against them, and the answer is that a nothing on the seventh morning does not go either way. It is a seventh morning.**"

"Noted and read out," said Dalla Sen, "in his own words. The six mornings stand as they stand, and nothing in the seventh has changed them and nothing in the sixth changed the first five."

---

Four sentences came up the nine steps at the fourth bell and it took two minutes and it is in the book in both men's words, and it is four sentences long, and three of them are that they do not know, and that was the point.

"One. The four treads are cut from the same dressed stone as the landing and they are dry, and there has been no water on them that either of us could see, and there was a lamp held at the rail and not put over it.

"Two. The rail is not dressed stone. It is round, and it is newer than the stone, and neither of us could see how it is fixed into the floor, and we did not lean on it, and Tams said out loud on the landing that we were not going to lean on it and I said out loud that we were not going to lean on it, and the two of us said it at the same time and neither of us had said it to the other one before.

"Three. **The air coming off those treads is cold and it is moving**, and the air engineer was on the landing with us and she says it is the room's own air and that she cannot say from a landing where it is going, and she said that before either of us asked her anything, and she said it twice.

"Four. We do not know what is at the bottom of them, and we are not going to find out, and I have been asked to say what can be said and what can be said is that."

"The third one is the whole of the batch," said Nera Oduya, from three steps, and she had been on the landing with them the whole of it and had not gone past the rail either.

"It is a moving cold air off a stone stair that nobody has walked in nine hundred years, and I have been asked for a plan since the seventh week and I have wanted a document to tell me something since before that, and **this is the closest this building has come to a document telling me something, and what it told me is that there is a draught on a row of a page with no air on it at all, and the draught is the only thing anybody has ever found in that room, and it is not on the page, because there is no column for it.**

"And I am not going to say what it means. I am going to say three things I will not do. I will not call it ventilation. I will not call it a shaft. And I will not put a rate on it, and I have not published a rate in fifteen weeks and I am not going to start in a cold month on the strength of a cold draught on a stair that a page located nine hundred years before I was born."

---

On the way up, in the service bend, on the way back and inside the terms and inside the work, Marek Venn stopped and put his left palm flat on the dressed stone of the throat's far wall at the height of a man's chest, which is the height Bel Ordry's bar is put at, and which he had worked out in the eighth week by asking Bel Ordry and not by guessing.

Four degrees. The loudest thing in the room. His hand and the stone.

Nothing.

He held it for as long as he had ever held it anywhere, and he listened past his own hand the way he has listened since the seventh week, and the cold in that throat was four degrees and the difference between his skin and the stone was a hand's breadth, and it was, as it had been for a fortnight, the loudest thing in that room.

Then he came up the nine steps and put the slate down on the bench and said, "Count. Ordinary column, and my own hand, and the date, and it is the ninth."

"Ninth," said Tams Quill, and took the board, and watched him write it, and then said, "Now I am going to move it."

"Tams—"

"I am going to move it and I am going to say why, for the fifth time, and it is the last time I am going to say why, because the reason has not changed in three weeks and the reason is that a null in the cold is a different instrument reading a different room, and that is in the book in your words from the fourth day of the eleventh week.

"So the ninth goes in the column with no heading, with the temperature on it and the place and the reason. **Six in the ordinary column and three in mine, which is nine in all.** I have taken one out of the first and put it in the second, and that is the whole of what a move is, and it is the third time I have done it, and **every one of the nine says the same thing, and there is not one entry in any column of that board that anybody in this room is allowed to build on.**"

"Entered," said Dalla Sen, and read it out, and read out the arithmetic of the nulls — **six and two before today, and six and three after, which is nine, and I have done it twice** — and then somebody at the back said the word that has been wanted to be said in this building for fifteen weeks, and the anteroom went quiet about it.

"**Null.**"

"It is null," said Marek Venn. "And I put my hand on that wall for forty seconds in the cold on a day I was asked to look at a stair, and I got nothing, and I have had nothing nine times, and the number has gone up eight times since the second day of the twelfth week and the thing I am able to do about a wall has not changed at all, and I want it read out that I went down on a named work and that the work was not the wall and that I put my hand on the wall anyway, and that the count let me, and that is the only reason I am not standing in a corner about it."

"It was not the wall," said Tams Quill. "It was the stair. And the work asked for was the rail and the lamp and the four sentences, and you did all three, and I have entered the work as finished and I have entered the wall separately and I have let you have it, and the condition I put on letting you have it is the condition I put on it three weeks ago and it is that it goes in as a null and not as a reading, and it is a null, and there is no reading in this building and there is not going to be one this month."

---

Then he read the two columns, and it was the fifth bell on a Tuesday and the anteroom was full and had been over its number since the fourth bell and six of them were down the nine steps listening, which is what this Hearthhold does.

"**Forty-eight and a half**," said Tams Quill, and put the chalk down, which he has done four times in fifteen weeks. "Forty-six and a half on the Friday of the fifteenth week, and two this morning, and it is the first work anybody in this building has asked this crew for in a month and it is a man standing at a rail looking at four steps he is not going down, and I want the room to hear the number of hours that represents. **It is two. It is a quarter of what Isel Ferrow gave to a shutter route in one morning.** And it is the first line in the ordinary column in a fortnight with a person's name against it in somebody else's writing, and that is what the column is for."

"And the other column," said Bel Ordry.

"**I am not going to read the other column today.**" Tams said it flatly and the anteroom waited and he did not fill the wait with anything, which is a thing he has never done. "It was last read on the last day of the fourteenth week at ninety-five and a quarter. It has not been read since. I have not walked that board in three weeks and there are hours on it that I did not put there, and **a man with a board does not quote a figure he has not walked, and I would rather this room thought I was slow than have it find out once that I am fast.**"

"It is read on the Friday," said Dalla Sen, "and I have entered the day on this page so that there is one promise in this book with a day on it, and it will reconcile or it will be corrected, and I am not going to guess at it either."

"Fine," said Tams. "Then the two things I have to read are not numbers."

---

"The two refusals," he said, "because they are next to each other in the book on purpose and the room has not looked at them together, and I have.

"One is mine. A column that does not go up is the first evidence this Hearthhold has produced toward the end of our own obligation, and I am the man who could stand up and say *we are finished*, and I would not say it on a figure, and a man who could end an obligation with arithmetic and does not do it has to say that he knew he could.

"Two is the keeper's, in his own hand, a month ago. **The crew's obligation is not discharged. Not yet.** And the reason is not the work; the work is finished. The reason is that he will not release five people into a cold month on the strength of one morning, and he asked for it to be entered with a condition instead of a date, and the condition is *the first day of the month the pot stops falling.*

"And those two are not the same refusal, and I want the room to see that they are not, because they have been sitting next to each other for a month and everybody in this room has assumed they are one thing.

"**Mine is a refusal of arithmetic. His is a refusal of a number.** Mine says *I will not do it on a figure and I could.* His says *I will not do it on a number and I will not say when.* Mine closes the door and leaves the hinges on. His props the door and puts nothing on the hinges at all, and the nothing is what the pot is."

He put the chalk down again and looked at the board.

"And I have done the arithmetic of his condition, because I am the man in this room who does arithmetic of conditions, and I have been sitting on it since Friday morning, and I am going to say it and then stop, and it is the keeper's condition and not mine, and I have had four days with it and I have used all four deciding whether to open my mouth."

"The pot cannot stop falling," said Tams Quill, "**while the only road water comes up in this Hearthhold is a gate that has been told in writing to stop waiting on us.** Twelve days, and it has gone nineteen, sixteen, fifteen, fourteen, thirteen, twelve, and it has never once gone up, and there is no second source and no well and no rain on a fill over an eleven-foot shaft. And the review is set for the first day of the month the pot stops falling. And this boundary spent four days writing a document that makes the pot harder to stop. **You set a condition and then your own notice moved the ground under it, and nobody has told you, and the man who worked it out is the man with the board and he is telling you on a Sunday because there is no other day.**"

The wheel went round under the floor and the check hissed on its one note and the anteroom was extremely quiet.

---

Sefu Aran did not come off the wheel.

"Right," he said. "**The count is right and the condition stays.**"

"Sefu—"

"The condition stays, in my own hand, with the words *the first day of the month the pot stops falling*, and it is not going to be moved because a figure went the other way, because the day a keeper moves a condition because a number came in wrong is the day the book is a set of moods and a set of moods is worth less than the wrong number. And I would rather be proved wrong by a number in a month than let off by a man who felt bad on a Sunday.

"And I am going to say the other half and I am going to say it once. **The notice did not make it harder.** The notice made it honest. For six weeks the pot has been falling for a reason nobody in this room had said out loud, and the reason was a yes that never existed — a plank in a store four days up a road that a man had been holding against a dry lock since before the collapse, and every one of us had been carrying that plank in a book as the only thing this Hearthhold had ever been able to name.

"And the notice took the plank away on Friday, and the pot has gone from thirteen to twelve, and it went to twelve because a woman was in a clinic and a boy of twenty-two was on a frame and the pump was stopped before its cycle, and **not one day of that cook's number has the notice in it.** Not one. I have looked. I have looked at it in the kitchen's own book and it is Wednesday and a clinic and a frame, and the release is Friday, and the arithmetic holds.

"So I gave away the only yes this boundary ever had, and it has not cost me a day of a cook's count, and I would like the room to sit with that, because every person in here has been waiting a month for me to be wrong about it and I would rather be told now than find out in a month."

"Entered," said Dalla Sen, "in the keeper's words, in his own hand, with the time on it, and the count's finding is entered above it, and the two are on the same page, and there are now three things next to each other in this book and the third one is a condition and a number and a man who felt bad on a Sunday, and I am not going to rule them and I am not going to choose between them."

---

"Last thing," said Tams Quill, and he turned the board round, which he does once, "and it is the column with no heading, and it is the best thing in this building and I have never once said so and I am not going to now.

"It has got in it a leg and a bundle and twenty days and twenty nights, and a half hinge that belongs to a man two rooms above the clinic return, and a line I refused and have not given since. And as of the first day of the sixteenth week it has got a page in it, because a page went up the middle passage at the second bell and there is no column in this book for a page.

"**That is the first thing that has ever gone in there that is not a person.** And the column can hold it, and it has held it, and the day I head it, a page becomes a unit, and a unit is a thing a room can be praised in, and I will not have a page praised. And I will say what the refused line is, once, so that nobody in a year thinks I forgot it: it is a line for a man who is under eleven feet of dressed stone at the near landing and is not coming up, and there is nothing of him in this building to put anywhere, and it was refused in the fourteenth week and it has stayed refused and it will not be filled by a feeling.

"And the harder half. Everything else in that column I have seen with my own eyes. The leg, the bundle, the hinge, the twenty nights — I have watched all of it happen. **I have not seen that page since it went through a door, and nobody in this building has, and the honest name for that is *out of our sight*, and I am not going to write that either, so it goes in with the others and I will know where it is.**

"And I will say what the refused line is, once, so that nobody in a year thinks I forgot it. It is a line for a man who is under eleven feet of dressed stone at the near landing and is not coming up, and there is nothing of him in this building to put anywhere, and it was refused in the fourteenth week and it has stayed refused and it will not be filled by a feeling."

"And that," said Bel Ordry, from the side, "is the whole of what has happened in this Hearthhold in the sixteenth week, and it is a page going out of a room and a hand coming back with nothing and a column that will not be headed, and I have been a man with nothing in my hands for fifteen weeks and I have never once been able to put it as well as a man with a board who has just done it."

At the fifth bell Dalla Sen read the arithmetic of the pot, which was **twelve days**, and the lip's range, which was a finger and a half to a finger and three-quarters and worse on a cold morning, and Renn Vale's seventh term, and the ninth line, and the blank on the review page with the two sentences on it instead of a name, and the kitchen's book with its forty-one writes of the word water and a third column in it that was still empty, and the new page on a butcher's sheet in a teacher's hand with two columns filled and one empty, and nine nulls, and a bone that had been refused a line, and a person who was still under that lip.
