# Chapter 185 — The Seventh Thing

The sixth day of the thirty-fifth week is a Saturday, and it is the day of the week on which the market road is used by ordinary people with no form on any board, and there is no market in this building and nobody in it went anywhere to see one. The school ran for the thirty-fifth morning in the room at the kitchen-line end. The corridor was open. A road hand came up the nine steps at the second bell with a wooden tally and said what she was before anybody asked her, which is the only thing about her that has never varied.

"**Board's still there,**" she said. "**Middle gate. New board over the old one, form still on it, both lines still filled in, seal number's the same number. I did not write it down and I am telling you that as a fact about me and not as a fact about the board.**"

"**And the counter,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**The counter is the other thing and I have thought about whether to bring it up for two days and I have decided to bring it up.**" The road hand turned the tally over in her hand and did not put it on the table. "**The woman whose hand it is was not there this week. There is a man at that counter and he is not the post-holder, and he is not on either line of the form, and I have carried paper at that counter for six years and I know what a stand-in looks like from about nine feet.**"

Nobody said anything. The pump got four strokes in.

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen. "**Entered that the form is unchanged and the seal number is unchanged and the board is unchanged. Entered that the person whose hand filled it in was not at the counter this week and that a different person was, and that we do not know what that means and I am not going to find out and I am not going to ask. That is the ninth time I have entered that in this book, and the count of them is not a number I read out.**"

"**Then the finding,**" said Bel Ordry, from the step.

"**The finding is that this boundary has spent six weeks angry at a woman and has now been told that she was not standing at her counter, and that is not a relief, it is a smaller target. A boundary that is angry at one person for six weeks does not stop being angry when the person goes away. It finds somebody else.**" He put his hand flat on the back of the bench. "**And I would like the clerk to enter that I said that, and that I have been the man keeping the page on that anger for six weeks without once asking what it was for.**"

"**I noticed it happening in myself this morning,**" Dalla Sen said, "**and I am entering that, and I am not going to write down what I did about it.**"

Then Dalla Sen took a sheet out of the book and read out loud what she had written on it the night before, in her own hand, and read it out again in the second person's hearing so that a second person could take it against her.

"**The terms are six. One: five of the six work on the common landing side of the party wall, and the kitchen-line side is not worked and is not entered by anybody in this room. Two: every person whose hands go on that wall says out loud, in a room, what they are holding and what stops it. Three: nobody is sent to the low end of the corridor, and the page at that end stays as it is. Four: nobody goes through the wall, and nothing goes into the line of nothing in it. Five: the person who watches that line is somebody who is not the man who built anything on that wall and not the man who found it, and that person can be told to stop by a person in a room. Six: none of this goes on the board.**"

She put the sheet face down on the trestle.

"**And where a name goes in the fifth, I have left it empty,**" she said, "**and the reason goes here, and the reason is that the room already knows who keeps the corridor, and a name in a column in this building is a thing on a column, and a thing on a column is a thing somebody can be sent for.**"

Then she wrote the six out again in front of the room, from memory, without touching the sheet.

Emrys Callow took the sheet and read the six back against what she had said with her mouth. They were the same.

The clerk entered that a stranger read a clerk's terms back to her in a room and found nothing to change, and entered that this is the second time this week a person outside this building has checked this clerk's hand and found nothing, and entered that she finds that more alarming than if they had found something.

Marek Venn had been standing at the end of the trestle through all of it with his book shut and had not once been asked for anything, and he had spent the morning getting steadily better at that and steadily worse at it. There is a particular kind of quiet a man can learn in five days, and it is the quiet of a man who has established that the room does not need him, and it is the most dangerous condition he knows of, because the room never does need him, and what it needs is for him to want to be in it for the wrong reason.

He had wanted, since the second week of this book, to be the man who found out. This week he had found out three things, and had been the wrong man for two of them, and the two he had been the wrong man for were the two that mattered. The face of the wall and the head of the shore belonged to a woman who reads finished things and a foreman of nine years. What belonged to him was a drawing on a table that he had not burned and had not folded away, and a sentence he had composed on Thursday and had not said.

So Marek Venn stood at the end of the trestle with his hands at his sides and his book shut, and he let the room be a room he was standing in. That is the first thing he has managed in this building in thirty-five weeks. Nobody in the room noticed it. Dalla Sen entered it, because she enters that.

"**Then that is terms,**" said Callow, "**and I have never in nine years had six of them written down by the people I am working for. I have had them in a foreman's head and I have had them in a book in an office and I have had them on a piece of paper a man could lose, and this is the first time anybody has written down that the kitchen-line side is not entered and then let a man read it.**"

"**Then there is one thing left in it,**" said Renn Vale, "**and I have been not saying it since Friday afternoon, and I have decided to say it now, and I am going to say it badly and then somebody can help me.**"

She came down the four steps, all of them, and stopped on the anteroom floor, which she does not do for anybody, and which she has done once in this book, and the once was in the same week.

"**The fifth of the six names a kind of person and not a person, and a kind of person is not an instruction anybody can carry out,**" she said. "**I have read it twice this morning and there is nothing in it I can put my hand on, and if I am the one who watches that line then somebody is going to find out from me, and if nobody finds out from me then a line of nothing about two fingers wide in a party wall is going to be watched by nobody for as long as this building stands.**"

"**You are the one,**" said Iven Tallo.

He had been at the anvil end for the whole morning with the chisel in his right hand and his left open and flat against his thigh, and he came over and put the chisel down on the anvil, which is what he does before he says a thing he means.

"**And I am going to say the bad part first, because I have had it for two weeks and I have not said it out loud in a room. On the fifth day of the thirty-third week a woman asked me in here what a person in this building is supposed to do when a piece of beech lets go, and she answered her own question, and the answer was that it has to be a person who is not the man who built it. And then I asked the next question, which is who that person is, and nobody answered me, and I have not asked it again in two weeks.**"

"**You did not ask it a second time,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and I have entered that four times this week about three different people.**"

"**I did not ask it a second time because I did not have an answer and I am a man who is good at building things and I have spent thirty-five weeks in this room finding out what happens when I hand somebody a thing and walk away. So I will say the answer I have now and then I will stop.**" Iven Tallo looked at the woman who keeps the corridor. "**A person who is not the man who built it, and not the man who found it, and who can be told to stop. That is the whole of the answer and it is not mine, it is hers, and I have been holding it for two weeks because holding it was easier than handing it over.**"

"**Then it is handed over,**" said Renn Vale.

Nobody interrupted her, which was now the third time this week and which she was aware of.

"**I will watch it,**" she said. "**At the first bell, on the days the corridor is open, and at the second bell on the days it is shut, and I will say what I can hear out loud in a room and I will not put a rule in it, and I will not put my hand in it, and I will not know what it is about and I will not pretend to.**"

"**And who stops you,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**Ilsa Norr, and the physician, and a room, in that order, and any one of the three does not have to give me a reason and I do not have to accept one,**" Renn Vale said. "**And I am not one of the hands that put anything on that wall, and I did not draw a hole in it, and I did not find the line, and that is the sentence the term was written for and it has now been said out loud in a room, which is what a term is for.**"

"**The seventh thing,**" said Bel Ordry, from the step, and he said it quietly and he was not being clever and everybody in the room knew it.

Renn Vale did not answer it for about four strokes of the pump.

"**I keep six things,**" she said. "**A shore, a road, the lead terms, a corridor, a landing, and this Hearthhold's second way out of its busiest room. I have said that in this room in a fifth of the weeks in this book and it has never once been a boast, it has been an inventory, and an inventory is a thing you check.**"

"**It is not an inventory this morning,**" Bel Ordry said. "**An inventory does not decide. A woman in a room who decides to take a seventh thing onto herself is a woman who has become a post, and a post is a thing a person fills and a thing a person can be moved out of, and there is no page in this building where anybody's hours go and I know that because I have looked for one for thirty-five weeks.**"

"**Then here is what I am, and put it in the space where a reason goes.**" Renn Vale put her hands flat on the trestle, on nothing anybody had written on. "**I said no to a seventh thing in this book when a man offered me a door with nothing wedged in it, and I said no because I did not know that door. This is the seventh thing and it is not a door, and I cannot say no to it, and I am not going to pretend to myself that the reason I cannot is better than the reason I could.**"

"**What is the reason,**" said Nella Bray, from the kitchen-line doorway, where she had been standing for half an hour without anybody asking her to.

"**The reason is that the room behind that wall is the room nine children are taught in,**" Renn Vale said. "**And there is no version of me that hears a sound in that wall and waits to be asked.**"

Nobody improved on that, and the count was not made into anything.

Talla Roke came up out of the dark at the bottom of the steps and up nine of them with a courier's bundle that was almost nothing. She had come up this passage in the thirtieth week and had been in this building twice since, and she walked in at the fourth bell the way a person walks into a kitchen. She said who she was and what she was carrying before anybody asked, and then she said the other thing, which is that she is not here about a boy in this building and has never been.

"**I heard on a road that a piece of timber came out of a wall on a Friday morning,**" she said. "**And I asked the woman I asked what a boundary that takes a piece of timber out of its own wall on a Friday morning does with the man who set it, and she said she did not know and would not guess, and I came down on the next open day to find out.**"

"**Then you are a day late and I am sorry for it,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and the man who set it is not in this room and is four days up a passage and is going to be told by a foreman and not by us.**"

"**Then the terms I have been sent to ask about are the right ones,**" Talla Roke said, "**because a term between two crews is the only thing a foreman can carry up a road and a man in an office can read without a seal, and I have been carrying papers for eleven years and I know which ones move and which ones sit.**"

"**You are not here about the terms,**" said Renn Vale.

"**I am here about the fifth of the six,**" Talla Roke said, and she put her bundle down on the flags, which nobody in this room does, and which nobody in this room stopped her doing. "**Because on the fifth day of the thirty-third week I asked a question in that room and a carpenter asked a better one after me and neither of you answered it, and I have thought about it on a road for two weeks and I have got the answer and it is wrong.**"

"**I want the answer and not the question,**" said Iven Tallo, "**because I have had the question for two weeks and it has not improved.**"

"**Your answer is that it has to be a person who is not the man who built it, and that is right,**" Talla Roke said. "**And the answer to the answer is that the person has to be somebody who can be told to stop, and that is right too, and it is still not a plan, because a person who can be told to stop is a person somebody can stop, and the somebody is whoever is holding the pen, and this morning the person holding the pen in this building is a woman who has not written that word once in a book in six weeks, and I have read that book on a road, so I know it.**"

"**That is fair,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**It is not fair and it is not unfair, it is just what I found,**" Talla Roke said. "**Here is the half of it I came to say. You can be told to stop by two people, and neither of them will be able to reach you. Not because they do not want to. Because at the moment that wall does the thing it has been doing, the two of them will be on the other side of nine children.**"

Nobody in the room had a better sentence than that, and Dalla Sen entered it in Talla Roke's words and entered that it was said by a person who had been on a road for two weeks and not by anybody who works in this building, and entered that she was not going to improve it, and entered that she had wanted to for about four seconds and had caught herself.

"**And the other half,**" said Renn Vale, "**which I am entitled to and which I have not given yet, and it is this. I have been keeping the second way out of that room for thirty-five weeks, and it is a word, and a carpenter in this room said on the third day of the thirty-third week that a person does not go out through a word, and nobody has answered him, and I am not answering him this morning either, and I am not going to let anybody in this room hear me fold a new thing into that sentence and pretend the old one is settled.**"

Then the message up the passage came onto the table, and it came out of the clerk's book and not out of anybody's sentiment, and it went onto the table underneath a thing nobody in the room had said all morning and everybody in the room had been carrying since Thursday.

Yesterday had been a fifth day.

That is the whole of it. The day in the first column of that printed schedule came yesterday. The corridor was open. Nine children were in a room that is not shut. A man was expected to walk into this building and look at three rooms.

He did not come.

On Thursday a woman who works at a counter four days up the middle passage stood in this room and told this room that a boundary which has never said no is a boundary somebody will arrange instead of asking. She was right about that on Thursday. Nothing has been arranged yet.

Nobody knows what an office does on a Saturday when a boundary does not attend. A clerk at a counter knows what a counter does on a Saturday, which is that a form is put in a tray and the tray is looked at on a Monday by a person with a bad morning. That is a fact about counters and it is not a fact about a district and it is not a finding, and Dalla Sen entered it in her own words with the words *not a finding* in the line, and entered that she is the ninth person in this building to be careful about that phrase and the first to use it about her own work.

"**Then we should not be pleased,**" said Renn Vale.

"**I am not,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and I would like it entered that the clerk is not, because a boundary that is pleased about a day nothing happened on is a boundary that has begun to want days nothing happens.**"

Dalla Sen put her hand flat on the page and read out what she had written on Friday and not sent.

"**A piece of seasoned shore four years old, set on the common landing side of a party wall on the first day of this week under a Warden-General's instruction, has lost about a finger of its length at the head. Its foot has not moved. The head is being crushed for want of a spreader under the head of the sill. It was taken out in eleven minutes. There are five and a half on that wall. The figure in the district book is not corrected by us and is not contradicted by us.**"

"**Read the last line of it back,**" said Bel Ordry.

She read it back. "**The figure in the district book is not corrected by us and is not contradicted by us.**"

"**And the half against sending it,**" Bel Ordry said, "**and I am the man who keeps the page, so I am the man who has to say it. A true fact about a wall that four people in a district know and nobody has written down is a thing that kills somebody in a year. And the only road a fact takes into a district's book is somebody else's paper, and somebody else's paper has a day in it and a column in it, and a day in it is a schedule, and a column in it is a name, and a thing that is scheduled is not a thing that is refused.**"

"**Then it is not getting refused either,**" said Renn Vale. "**Send it. Send it because it is true and because a foreman is carrying it anyway and because if we hold it back and the man who chose the wood finds it out from a book in a district then this boundary has learned to hold things back from offices, and that is the first sentence of a very short road to a room with a name over the door.**"

"**Then it goes,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and I am going to write the day on it, and there is a date available to me on one instrument in this building and it is not this, and so I am going to write the day of the week and not the date, and I would like a person in a year to be able to tell the difference between a message and a form.**"

Talla Roke said it from the kitchen-line doorway with her bundle still on the flags at her feet, and she was going up the nine steps again inside the hour, and nobody in the room remarked on the shape of the sentence because everybody in the room has been saying some version of it all week.

"**Say what it costs.**"

"**It costs a piece of timber going up a passage on somebody else's day,**" said Dalla Sen. "**And it costs this boundary the last thing it had, which was not having said anything to anybody, and it costs the six on that landing a morning of their four. And it is the cheapest thing anybody in this room has ever sent anywhere, and I have put that in the book next to a sentence about a pot at seven days that a cook said out loud this week and would not attach a word to, with nothing in between them.**"

Then Tams Quill had the chalk in his left hand and the right one open and flat on his knee, and he read the eighteen, and gave the third refusal of a nineteenth entry he has given in a fortnight, with a reason for this one, which the other two did not have.

"**The terms are not going on this board,**" he said. "**And that is a different reason from the two I gave for the other two, and I have got this one.**"

"**Then give it,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**A brace is on here and a store is on here and a plan is on here, and every one of those is a thing in this building that a person in this building can be asked about,**" Tams Quill said. "**A term between two crews is not a thing in this building. It is a thing two rooms agreed about. And the day one of those rooms is not in this building any more, the term is still on here, with nobody left to ask.**"

"**That is a reason,**" said Dalla Sen. "**Enter it in his words.**"

"**And the column is not headed,**" he said, "**and I have nothing to say about that, and nobody has asked me.**"

By the sixth bell the clerk read the day's number and the total out loud, both, and the count was read at one hundred and twenty, and the board was walked at eighteen, and nobody in the room said one word about a hundred and twenty until Tams Quill said it, and then a clerk said it back to him.

"**A hundred and twenty,**" Tams Quill said.

"**It is a number of days,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and it is not a number of anything. I have been writing it in a column with nothing over the top of it for twenty-five weeks and there is nobody in it and there is nothing under it, and I have said out loud every week that it is not going to be the next one, and I would like that said in the space where a reason goes and not said again this week.**"

She entered it. Three today and two hundred and fifty-seven since the keeper signed. The boundary is at three hundred and seventy-four and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.

The crew are at seventy-one and there has been no sixth asking all week, and the reason for each of the five days is in the book. Today's reason is that a room wrote six terms down with a space in the fifth of them, and a clerk would not fill the space. A clerk does not ask five people to carry a piece of work on the morning she has refused to write a word in a place where a word goes.

The pot is seven days and the seam gave one on Thursday and not two, and the four households at a second gate have nothing, and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody. And the count. Wen Pask is one hundred and twenty days and one hundred and twenty nights into a count that is his own, and it is not going to be one hundred and twenty-one, and she has said in twenty-nine weeks running that she is going to keep writing it, and nobody has thanked her.

A coat has been on the hook by the door for thirty-five weeks, and there is a brass survey rule in the pocket of it that has said nothing for twenty-six of those weeks. Above the same bench a wrapped plate has been unopened for thirty-five. Neither came into a room this week. Neither moved.

And in the party wall, the line of nothing went on being the width of two fingers, unwatched, unmeasured and unnamed, with a woman who can be told to stop owing it the first thing in the morning, and a term in a clerk's book with a space in it where a name would go, and a message about a piece of timber going up a passage on somebody else's day.
