# Chapter 226 — The Loss Standing, And An Audience

The second day of the forty-third week is a Tuesday, the corridor is shut, and the school did not run and lost nothing, and at about the fifth bell four people in this anteroom opened four things that had been closed since Friday, because leaving them shut was not a decision either, and the clerk entered all four as four decisions, and entered that a room which has taken a loss has to put its furniture back before it can work inside it.

Jo Verrick had the frame at the first bell, a finger and two-thirds, and the party wall was a quarter of an inch, the forty-sixth reading, and the same as forty-five, and the string was where the string was. She put her thumb under it and the thumb came out of it right, and she did not give the four refusals, and she gave the reason instead, in one sentence, in the same words for the tenth time, and the clerk entered it and entered that it was the same sentence.

Renn Vale put her ear to the wall at the first bell, four times over, and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, and the run of mornings out of mornings is thirty-one.

The terms were said at the first bell, all six, and the third of them was said without anybody being asked whether it is right, and this morning the saying of it was the thing that happened in the anteroom, because Mikka Hale was in it.

She has not been in the anteroom at the second bell since the beginning of the forty-second week. She has been in the kitchen-line doorway, which is four feet inside the anteroom and is not the anteroom, and it has been a standing arrangement for forty-two weeks that the cook is either in her doorway or in her kitchen, and a clerk entered that on the second day of the forty-second week, because the room has been asking itself in a book whether anybody in it is a person who can be found, and a cook who is never in a room is a cook who cannot.

"**I am in the room,**" Mikka Hale said, when the third of the six terms had been said, "**and I would like it entered that I am in the room, and entered why, and I am going to say the why myself because the clerk would put it in the space where a reason goes and it does not go in the space where a reason goes.**"

"**Say it,**" Dalla Sen said.

"**On Friday a woman stood at the end of that table and said a plan of hers protects four rooms in this building, and I have thought about the four rooms all weekend and I have worked out which four they are, and one of them is this one and one of them is the room at the kitchen-line end and the other two are the two rooms off the far end where six people sleep.**" She had the cloth over her arm and she did not move it. "**And on Sunday it was in a room in a corridor that a woman who reads walls was in, and on Monday it was in a book, and this morning a man at the far end of a cook's room asked me a question about it and I answered him, and the answer I gave him was a number, and the number was mine, and I gave it to a man who is not this building's.**"

Nobody in that anteroom moved, and the pump made a stroke and then made another.

"**So the water-carrying has left this building twice this week without me doing anything,**" Mikka Hale said, "**and I am not going to be standing in a doorway on a morning when it is going out again, and I would like the clerk to enter that as the reason and not as a habit, because a habit is a thing I have and a reason is a thing I have to have.**"

"**Entered, in the cook's words, in the space where a reason goes, and entered that she is in the room,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that a term which is said in a room is a different instrument from a term which is said in a doorway, and that I have known that for forty-two weeks and have not had a cook in a room to make it true.**"

The pump was started, two people turning it, and it ran from the second bell to the fifth, and the four vents were open and the air in this room was coming from the corridor and out of the same hole it has been coming out of for nineteen weeks, and the man at the anvil end who had not been asked for anything said the thing that everybody in the room had been thinking and nobody had put in a shape.

"**Then the plan is dead,**" said Emrys Callow, "**and I am not going to be the man who says it in a soft voice, and I would like the clerk to enter that I said it flatly and that nobody argued with me. The plan ran from Friday morning until about the fourth bell yesterday. The far side of that door is shut. The far side of that door is going to be shut. There is no version of Monday in which nine men go back down there, and so there is no turn-over, and so the chamber keeps filling, and so there is nothing in this building's week that touches that water again.**"

"**Say the half against that,**" Dalla Sen said.

"**The half against is that a plan is not a thing that is alive and a plan is not a thing you can kill,**", and he did not sit down. "**There are four vents in this building that got shut on Friday and got opened again at the fifth bell yesterday and the reason they got opened again is not that the plan failed. It is that they were in the way of a thing this building wanted to do. A man in this room should not be able to shut four vents in a school and be told two days later that shutting them was not a plan, he should be able to shut them and then be the one who opens them, and that is what happened, and I do not know what I am disagreeing with.**"

"**Entered, in a foreman's words, and entered that he was not asked,**", and she did not look up.

"**Then let us say what is standing,**" said Bel Ordry, from the step, and he had a page out of his coat and he did not read it, which is the thing he does four mornings in ten. "**The loss is standing. A pump on a line this boundary has no authority on has not been turned over and will not be, this week or the next, and a chamber four days away is filling, and no person in this room can do one thing about either of those, and a clerk wrote a man's name against it on Monday morning and read it back out loud, and that entry is the only instrument this building has produced in a week.**"

"**Say the half against that,**", from the middle of the floor.

"**The half against is that the entry is not an instrument, it is a receipt,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and a receipt is what a room keeps so that it can say later that it knew, and there is a great deal of that in this building already and a receipt for a loss is the most useless object in this world. And the other half of the half against is that a man who is given a receipt in a clerk's book stops being able to say that he was never asked, and I do not know whether that is a loss to him or to us, and I have been in this building forty-two weeks and I have never once had to think about it.**"

Nobody in that anteroom improved on that, and the clerk entered it in the words of the man who said it, and entered that he was not asked, and entered that a claim on a trestle is neither granted nor refused and that a receipt in a book is a different thing from a claim and that she is not going to put them next to each other.

Then the woman at the counter put her slate down, and it was the first time in two days that anybody had put anything on that table that was not a plan, a term, a refusal or a debt.

"**I have been at a counter for nineteen years,**" said Nella Bray, "**and I am going to say a thing in this room that is about my trade and not about this building, and I am going to say it because it is Monday and it has already happened once and I would like it on the page before it happens a second time.**"

"**Say the thing,**" Bel Ordry said.

"**A fact that is said out loud in a room is a thing that is out of the room by the following day.**" She did not look at anybody. "**Not because people are wicked. Because a room like this one is a room where something interesting was said, and a man at a counter is a man who has a form and a form has a line, and the line wants filling in, and the line is *what was said in the anteroom*. I have watched a thing travel from a gate to a middle gate to a file in nine years and I have never once watched it travel wrong. It travels right. That is the difficulty.**"

"**Say the half against that,**" Dalla Sen said.

"**The half against is that a plan is a different thing from a fact and that the plan is going to travel slower than the fact, and that is worse, because the fact is what people are going to be asking about and the plan is what this building is going to be trying to sell them,**", and she had the slate out from under her arm. "**A woman at a gate does not want to know about four vents. A woman at a gate wants to know whether there is water. And there is a difference between those two sentences and it is the whole difference between a building that keeps something and a building that has something it could give away.**"

"**Then say the number of days it takes,**" Bel Ordry said.

"**One,**" Nella Bray said, "**and I am not going to make it a day of the week.**"

"**Then it has already happened,**" said Bel Ordry, "**because a woman read a plan out loud in this room on the fifth day of the forty-second week and a man at a counter in this district is going to be asked about it today, and this building has not put one word in writing anywhere outside this room, and that is not a defence and it was never going to be one.**"

"**It is not a defence and I was not offering it as one,**" Nella Bray said, "**and I am going to say the cost of what I have just said out loud because a room deserves it. I have said that in a day. The day after I said it, this boundary has a fact about water in it at a second gate, and it did not choose that, and it cannot call it back, and the two people in this room who are going to be asked about it are a keeper and a cook, and neither of them is a keeper of water and one of them has told a room she will not have a post.**"

Then a woman came out of the kitchen-line doorway with a cloth over her arm and stopped, and the room had not asked her, and Dalla Sen entered that nobody had asked her, and entered that she was about to say the thing a cook says at a gate in this building, and entered that the clerk had noticed that she was about to say it and had not asked her to and would not have.

"**How long is it,**" said Mikka Hale, "**before a thing this room has said out loud turns up at the bottom of four sets of steps as a question somebody is asking me?**"

Nothing came out of anybody in that room.

"**I have been asked three times this month,**" she said, "**and twice by women I know and once by a man who wanted to know whether I had been in the room. I have said no three times. I am going to say it a fourth time and I would like the clerk to enter that I have said it four times and that the fourth time is the one I would like written down, because it is the first one where the reason has changed and I do not want the other three thrown out and I do not want this one to be a reason for the other three.**"

"**Say the reason,**" Dalla Sen said.

"**The other three were about me,**" Mikka Hale said. "**This one is about them. On Sunday a woman came up nine steps to say this building had done a great deal to a room she asked it to leave alone, and she said the reason four households have nothing is that a boundary decided a long time ago it would not have a post. And on Monday this building was handed a loss by a man and it took it, and it wrote his name in a book against it, and it did not have a post to refuse it with and it did not need one.**"

She put the cloth over the other arm.

"**So the fourth no is this: the reason there is nothing at the bottom of those steps is that this building has decided it has nothing to hand out, and a building that has nothing to hand out does not get to say it has nothing, because the only reason it has nothing is that it has never looked. And the reason it has never looked is the post, and the post is refused, and I refuse the fourth time, and the clerk is going to enter that a cook has said no four times and that the fourth no is not the same no and that I do not know what to do with that either.**"

"**Entered, in the cook's words, and entered that she was not asked, and entered that this is the fourth refusal and that the clerk has entered the first three and does not know that they were the same refusal and is not going to be told by anybody in this room which of them was, and entered that a cook has now said in a room that a building which has never looked has no standing to say it has nothing, and that this boundary has been saying that it has nothing for two volumes in a sentence that this room has never once tested,**" said Dalla Sen, and she stopped writing, and she did not read it back.

At about the fourth bell a woman at the far end of the room at the kitchen-line end said a sentence into the room that nobody had asked her for, and then went out at the far door, and the corridor was shut and the far door is not the corridor and that is the whole of the difference between this building and a building that is honest about its doors.

"**Say what was said,**" Bel Ordry said, "**or say that it was not said, and I will take either.**"

"**She said it out loud in a room and there are two of us in it and I am not going to pretend I did not hear it,**" said Mikka Hale, "**and I am also not going to say it again, and I want that entered.**"

"**Entered that a sentence was said and is not being repeated, and entered that the clerk asked for it to be entered, and entered that a cook did not give it,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that the second half of that is a refusal and it is mine and I am not giving a reason for it because I have not got one that is mine.**"

No one in that room took it up., and the clerk entered that nobody had.

Then, at about the fifth bell, a woman who reads walls put her hand flat on the party wall about four feet out from the join and said the thing that nobody in that room had thought about for a week.

"**I am going down the wet stair at the second bell tomorrow,**" said Jo Verrick, "**and I have not told anybody and I am telling this room now, at the fifth bell, an hour before I am going to bed, because if I say it now then nobody in this room can say that I did not say it, and if I said it tomorrow morning at the first bell then this room would have a morning in which to talk me out of it, and I have known me for nineteen years and I know what I would do with a morning like that.**"

The anteroom was quiet for about as long as a pump takes to make three strokes.

"**I am going to find out how clear that run is,**" she said. "**A woman read a fall out of a cistern nine days ago and told this room it goes down at the same rate for its whole length and that she could see it at the cistern end and at the chamber and could not see the part where it comes in under this floor, and that nobody in this room knows how clear it is, and that she was not going to guess. She was right not to guess. And there is a person standing in this room who can stand in that water and put a lamp on the bottom of it and look, and I have a folding rule in a belt that I have not taken out in fifty mornings because I have been giving a reason every morning instead of four refusals, and the reason is *a wall is not mine*, and the reason is a good reason and it is about me and it is not about a wall.**"

"**Then say the reason you are going,**" Dalla Sen said.

"**Because a chamber four days away is filling and nobody in this building can do one thing about it,**" said Jo Verrick, "**and because I have been in eleven flooded rooms in nineteen years and I have never once been in one where the way out was the same height all the way, and somebody read that off a wall a week ago and this room has not sent anybody, and this room will not send anybody, because of a post, and I would like to go on my own two hands before somebody in this building has to send me.**"

"**Entered, in the words of the woman who reads walls, and entered that she was not asked, and entered that no work was asked for and no hours were asked for and no person was named as being able to be told no,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and entered that a woman with a rule of her own on a wall has said out loud in a room that she is going to be the one who goes, and that this is the first time in fifty mornings that the woman who reads walls has said she was going to do a thing, and that I have entered it and joined it to nothing.**"

"**Then the clerk is going to say one thing and I am going to say it now while he is in the room,**" Dalla Sen said, "**and it is not about the woman who reads walls. There is a man at the end of this trestle who has got a stage and has not used it since he cut a splayed head and a shim in this building and watched a brace go up over a door, and I have watched this room for forty-two weeks go round him every single time a thing gets built, and every single time it has been the right thing to go round him, and I am not going to stop going round him, and I am entering that a clerk noticed it and did not stop it and that the noticing is a thing about the clerk and not about him.**"

Marek Venn had been at the end of the trestle since the first bell with the shut book under his forearm, and he had done the thing he does about four mornings in ten, and what it turned up this time was not a thing anybody else in the room had been carrying.

It was that the stage was worth having again, for the first time in a fortnight, and that there was nothing to put it on.

A frame across the head of that wet passage would still take the pressure off the head of that door, and he had worked that out two weeks ago and had not said it, and the reason he had not said it was in this room in a book on the sixth day of the forty-first week and it was the right reason and he would give it again. And this morning the door was shut and the four vents were open and the plan was not a plan, and a woman was going down a wet stair in the morning to look at the bottom of a nine-hundred-year-old channel, and there was no frame in the world that would help her with that, and there was no frame in the world that would turn a pump over on a line this building has no authority on.

He had spent five volumes being a man who answers a cost with a thing he can make. Two weeks ago he had found out that there is a second kind of man, who hears a cost and lets it stand, and he had not known what to call the difference. On Monday a clerk wrote a man's name in a book against a loss he did not decide, and a cook said a fourth no that was not the same no, and a foreman said flatly in a room that the plan was dead, and a woman read a line off a counter and put a day on how fast a fact travels out of this room, and none of those four things could be answered with timber.

And the thing that the woman at the counter had said was the one that stayed. A fact is out of the room by the following day. He had been sitting in this room for nine weeks believing that the danger was a room that said something in a room. The danger was a room that said nothing in a room and had a pump, and a fall, and a door, and four rooms of people, and a chamber four days away, and none of it in writing anywhere, and all of it true, and all of it on its way to a gate by the end of today.

You cannot build a brace against that. He understood that at about the fifth bell of a Tuesday, sitting still, with a book under his arm, and it was the first thing he had understood in nine weeks that was not about a structure.

At the sixth bell the clerk read the day's figure and the total out loud, and did not read the count, and gave the reason, and the reason was that a room which has just been told that a fact is out of it by tomorrow is owed a total it can verify, and that the total is the only instrument in this building that has never once been anybody's opinion.

"**Three today and three hundred and ninety-nine since the keeper signed. The boundary is at five hundred and sixteen and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.**"

The count went in underneath that on its own line, in the ordinary hand, and was entered and not read, and the reason was given, and it was that a cook has said a fourth refusal out loud in a room this morning and a woman at a counter has put a day on how fast this room talks, and a clerk who has entered both of those is not going to read a number she has been reading for sixty weeks, and that is a reason about the clerk and not about the count. Wen Pask is one hundred and seventy-one days and one hundred and seventy-one nights, sixty weeks of a clerk saying out loud that she is going to keep writing it down. It is not going to be one hundred and seventy-two.

The board was not walked, and the reason was entered, and the reason was that a man at an anvil said the plan is dead this morning and a clerk wrote a name in a book yesterday, and a board is a list of what this building found, and a plan that was alive for four days and a receipt are not on it, and a clerk is not walking it.

The crew are at eighty and did not move, and the reason for the day is in the book, and the reason is that a foreman said in a room at the first bell that there is no version of this week in which nine men go back down there, and that four and a half hours of his own are outstanding and there is no settled work in this building to put them on, and the clerk entered that she wrote both halves of that and believed neither of them entirely.

The pot is three days, and the seam gave nothing this morning for the fourteenth morning running, and it is at four threads, and nobody measured it and nobody explained it, and the fire in the room at the kitchen-line end has now not been lit for thirty-two weeks, and the four households at a second gate have nothing and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody, and a cook has told this room that a building which has never looked has no standing to say it has nothing, and nobody in this building has any idea where to start looking.

And at the seventh bell the anteroom was nine steps and a trestle and a book with four new things in it and a page in a coat that was not read, and four vents open that had been shut for two days, and nine men at the far end of a cook's room, one of them limping less than he was at the first bell, and a woman at the far end of that room who had said a sentence out loud that two people had heard and that nobody was going to repeat, and a woman at the kitchen-line end with a cloth over her arm who had been in the anteroom at the second bell for the first time since the beginning of the fortieth week, and a woman who reads walls with a folding rule in a belt that she has not taken out in fifty mornings and is going to take out tomorrow, and a fact that was somewhere in this district already by the fourth bell and had not come here yet.
