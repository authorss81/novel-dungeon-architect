# Chapter 64 — The Reply

The reply came up the middle passage on the back of a man carrying a bag of something that was not water, and it was forty days old, and it had been written by a woman forty days' walk away who had never seen the stair.

She came down the nine steps at the second bell on the second day of the review week, in a coat that had been mended at the elbow by somebody who was not her, with a stick in her left hand and a flat case under her arm, and she stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up the length of them the way people do when they have been told there are more of them than they expected.

"Sabra Kest," she said. "Cartwright route-custody assessor, out of the Marrowgate clearance office. That is a reply, and it is addressed to the keeper of this boundary, and it has been forty days on the road and I have carried it myself for the last eleven, which is not regulation and which I would do again. I am not the clerk who came in the tenth week. She is not here and I am not her and I have not spoken to her since she left, and if anybody in this room wants her margin they will have to wait for it to come round again, which will be a long time."

Sefu Aran had come off the wheel. He did not go down the nine steps. Nobody in that building went down nine steps to meet anybody; they met people at the bottom.

"Entered," he said, "on my own authority and not anybody's: a Cartwright route-custody assessor, not a person of this boundary, no watch, no rota, and I am saying both halves out loud now rather than at the fourth bell, as I did for the clerk, because it is my rule and it does not depend on the coat."

"That is fair," said Sabra Kest. "And I would like it recorded that I would have said the same thing about myself if I had not been asked, and that I have put my own name and my own number on the bottom of the page in your clerk's hand if she will take it, because a document that arrives from an office with nobody's name on it is a document I would not sign either."

Dalla Sen took the name and the number, and Sabra Kest gave the number twice and made her write it once more with the last figure doubled, because that is what the number was and the clerk would want it exact.

---

She read the reply herself, in the anteroom, standing up, with the case open on the bench and the page in her own hand, and she did not summarise it first, and when the room got to the third of four pages she stopped and let a woman at a frame interrupt her, which Jo Verrick did not have to be asked to do.

"Read the line about the seat," Jo said.

"There are two lines about the seat." Sabra Kest put a finger on the page without looking at it. "The first is in the acknowledgment and says that the boundary has reported the loss of an inspection instrument and the failure of a seat, and that the office notes both and requires no further particulars. The second is in the fourth paragraph and says that a reported seat failure on a route that is the sole means of egress from a rated occupancy constitutes a reportable condition, and that a reportable condition on an unheld route is a reportable condition with no responsible party attached, and that such a report is closed without action."

"Then it is a document that says we have a problem and there is nobody to give it to," Jo said.

"Yes," said Sabra Kest. "That is exactly what it says, and I want you to know that I argued with the wording on the fortieth day of a road, in a room in Marrowgate, and I lost, and the man who beat me said the sentence you would have said: *a form with no place to write a keeper has no place but a holder, and if we invent a box we will fill it with a name we found somewhere.* He was right. It is a good sentence. I have thought about it every day since and it has got worse."

"Read the last page," said Renn Vale, from the upper landing.

Sabra Kest turned the page over, and what was on the back of it was not a paragraph. It was a form, printed, with a heading, and with boxes.

"Route disposition return," she said. "Form nine C. It has been on the front of the file since the day the clerk's margin arrived and it has been filled in by hand by two people in an office I do not work in, and I am holding it up so that the room can see the shape of it, and then I am going to put it flat on that bench, because I have learned in eleven years that a form held up is a performance and a form on a table is a form.

"On the left, the route, by description and by the inventory number the air engineer keeps. On the right, three dispositions, and only one of them can be ticked.

"One: **held.** Name of the holder, capacity, and the office under which the holding is exercised. Two: **worked under charter.** Name of the charter, its number, its date, and the guild whose charter it is. Three: **unattended.** No name required, no signature required, and one line, which is a line for the person completing the return."

Nobody in the anteroom said anything at all for a moment.

"You have come forty days," said Mikka Hale, "to bring us a form with three boxes and a person to put in one of them."

"I have come forty days to bring you a form with three boxes," Sabra Kest agreed, "and the person in them is not mine to be. If I had a name to put in there I would have put it, because that is what the return is for, and because I have put names in six hundred of them and I have never once known what any of them meant by the time it was in the file."

Down it went on the bench, flat, the way Corva Nye had put a form flat, and after that she did not touch it again.

"Read it out," said Dalla Sen. "All of it, please, including the small print, because there is always small print and there is always a small print that turns out to be the whole thing."

"There is not," said Sabra Kest. "I have read it. It is four hundred words and every one of them is load-bearing. I will read the operative sentence and then I will stop, and the operative sentence is under the third box, and it is this:

"*Where no disposition is completed, the route shall be returned as unattended, and no officer of this or any chartered office shall be required to enter, inspect, maintain, fund, or answer for any condition arising upon it, and the returning office shall be recorded as having been informed in writing of the condition and of the absence of a holder.*"

"That is the whole of your office's answer," said Renn Vale, "and you have read it to us twice now without doing anything to it."

"I have read it to you twice now because it is the sentence that your clerk's margin brought about, and I would like the people who wrote that margin to know precisely what they cost you, and I would rather they heard it from me on a Thursday than from a file in a year." She looked at Sabra Kest's own hand, and then up. "So: if nobody fills in a box, then your stair is a route with nobody on it, on paper, in Marrowgate, forever, and no one can ever come and look at it, and no one can ever be blamed for it, and no one will ever send you a plank. And that is the best answer anybody in this building could have wanted, and it is also the reason nobody is ever going to help you, and I have been carrying both halves of that sentence for forty days and I would like somebody to notice that they are the same half."

---

"Three people can fill that in," said Iven Tallo.

He said it to the room and not to the form, and he had said nothing at all for eleven minutes, which was the longest Iven Tallo had gone without speaking since the sixth week.

"One. Me. I am the only licensed route carpenter between here and the middle and I have held a licence for nineteen years and I have got a bond that is suspended and a receipt for the suspension, and both of those pieces of paper are in the clerk's book, and if I put my name in the first box then I am holding a route on a suspended bond, which is not a grey area, it is a rule with a number on it, and I would be finished as a tradesman in the Cartwrights by the second hearing and I would not be the first and I would not be the last.

"Two. Renn Vale, and she is standing up there refusing to be on a stair, and if her name went in that box then this Hearthhold would have a holder, and we would be a route that is held, and every question anybody has ever asked this room about a holder would have an answer with her name against it, and she has spent eleven weeks refusing that in four different rooms.

"Three. Nera Oduya, and the box for the air authority is on the same page, and if the air engineer puts her name on a guild return then the boundary has a licensed professional on a guild register, and eleven weeks ago she stood in that room and said the first thing anybody with a piece of paper would take out of this building is not the stone, it is the fact that there is a name in it. And she was right, and her name is already on the end of a piece of timber, and it is in her own inventory, and she chose that."

"That is right on all three counts," said Sabra Kest, "and I am going to tell you something about the first one, and I am telling you at the front of this room instead of letting you find it in a file, and the reason I am telling you is that I have signed eleven of these returns in eleven years and I have never once been able to make myself do it quietly afterwards."

"Sabra," said Sefu Aran.

"I am inside my authority, keeper, all of it. A suspended bond may be reinstated on a single document. It is not a bribe, it is not a favour, and it is not a favour to me, because I do not get anything and the office does not get anything and the man gets his bond and the route gets a name and everybody's paper is in order. I have done it eleven times. I have watched eleven very good men say yes to it, and I want you to know what I said to the first one, which was *are you sure*, and I have never said it since, because saying it is a way of asking a man to do the thing twice and the second time he does it because I asked."

Iven Tallo stood in the middle of the anteroom floor with his hands open at his sides, and he said, quietly:

"No."

"Then write it in your own hand and I will put it on the return, and the return will go back with a reason and the reason will hold, and the route will be returned as unattended and the letter will be in the file by the fiftieth day and that is the end of it and nobody will ever ask you again."

"No," Iven said again, "and I am going to tell the room why, and then I am going to tell you why, and then I am going to go and stand somewhere else.

"I have stood in this anteroom for nine weeks and told it, in order, that the day the route's page gets a line added to answer a clerk is the day the page stops being a thing the boundary keeps and starts being a document the boundary defends. That is my sentence. It is on the page. Dalla, it is in the book, and I would like somebody to turn to it, because I am about to say the other side of it and I would like the two to sit next to each other.

"Here is the other side. **I am the man who would fill that box in, and I have spent nine weeks teaching eleven people why nobody should, and I am the only person in this room whose signature would make it true that nobody should.** A page in a file that says a licensed route carpenter is responsible for the shoring is not a page about this boundary. It is a page about a man. And a man can be found. A man can be written to. A man has a bond and a receipt and a suspension and a hearing, and in four years the person filling in the next blank is going to be asked to find me, and I will be four hundred miles away with a satchel, and the file will say that the route was held by a man, and the man will not be here, and the route will still be here with nobody on it and a name on it, and that is **worse** than tonight. Tonight is clean. Tonight a clerk wrote the worst sentence I have ever read about this building in her own hand and she is standing in it and she has not flinched.

"So: I would rather this route be maintained by the people whose rooms it is under and held by nobody, and I would rather be asked to do a day's work in it as a stranger with a name on a crew's page than be its holder, and the reason is not a principle. The reason is that **the only way to make me leave is to take my licence, and I have not given anybody that.**"

To Sabra Kest, in a different voice entirely:

"Now you. Do not think I have just refused a bond out of principle, because I have, and I would like it recorded, and I would like you to write this down and read it back, because I have never had a thing read back that I was not frightened of in my life.

"The bond is not the reason. The bond is thirty-one years of a man's hands and I would rather have it than not, and I have not got it, and I have not been able to get it, and I did not expect to get it. **The reason is that a licensed man who signs a route in this building does not stop being the man who signed it when he leaves the room, and there are five of us who are leaving.** And in the spring there is going to be an hour set aside in this anteroom to talk about an air hand-over, and I am going to be in it, because I am one of the five. And if there is a box on a form in a file in Marrowgate with my name in it, then that hour is not a hand-over. That hour is a resignation, dressed as a hand-over, and everybody in this room will sit there being kind to me about it.

"So write it down: *the man declines because the route outlives him and he does not, and because the boundary needs a keeper after the last of the strangers has gone, and a keeper is not a man with a licence.* And read it back. And then take your form back down nine steps and put it in your case and come and have the pot on, because Mikka Hale has told you twice that it is not a wage and I would like to be the first person in eleven weeks to tell you that I am having it anyway, and I am having it because I am going to be hungry."

---

Sabra Kest wrote it in her own hand on the back of her own return and read it out, and Dalla Sen entered it in the book, and Iven Tallo made her read it a second time, which is the fifth time in eleven weeks that anybody has made that clerk read a sentence twice, and the room had stopped remarking on it.

Then the assessor did the thing that Renn Vale noticed afterwards and wrote down in her own hand with the time on it, and it was this: she did not ask to observe a crossing.

"I have read your keeper's refusal of my colleague," she said, to Renn Vale. "It is on page four of the file, in her own words, with both halves of it, and the reason it is on page four in her own words is that she wrote it for me. So I am not going to come and ask her to say it again, and I am not going to come in the cold and be refused in front of this room either, because I would get the same answer and I would have spent a day in a corridor to be told a thing I already know."

"Then what do you want," said Renn Vale.

"I want to do the one thing I came to do, which is fill in the return, and there is no box I am allowed to fill in, and I have known that since the fortieth day on a road and there has been nothing I can do about it, and I have come anyway, because a route that has been returned unattended twice running without anybody saying anything is a route that gets closed on paper in a year with no notice to anybody at all. So I am here to be a person in the room while it happens, so that in a file somewhere there is a note that says a route-custody assessor was present, and refused, and on what grounds.

"And I have one question and it is a small one and I have written permission to ask it, and I am going to ask it in front of all of you, and if the answer is no then I will write that down as well and it will go in the file, and you will have chosen it."

"Ask it," said Renn Vale.

"Form nine C has a field," said Sabra Kest, "under the disposition, headed *known conditions observed*. It is the only free-text field on the form. It exists so that an office can record a fault it has been shown and cannot fund, which is a field that is used about four hundred times a year and is read about twice.

"There is a condition on this route. I am not asking what it is. I am asking whether it goes in that field. And I will tell you the whole of what I know about the field, which is this: if it goes in, then this stair has a **recorded condition in a guild file**, and a recorded condition is a thing that an office can be shown, and asked about, and blamed for, and it is the only way I can think of to make anybody in Marrowgate send anybody at all. And if it goes in, it goes in as a fault, and faults get surveys, and surveys get inspectors, and an inspector in this building is a person on the landing with a slate who does not live here.

"And if it does not go in, then tonight is the last time any office ever holds anything about this stair except a margin and a blank form, and that is a clean night, and it is a clean night in a building with a woman under a lip and a pump with no seat in it and no wood to make one."

The pen went down on the bench beside her own form.

Renn Vale was quiet for long enough that the wheel went round twice underneath them.

"There is a condition," she said. "It is in my hand. It is on my slate, and it is the seventh term out of seven, and I wrote it in this room in front of eleven people, and I am going to read it out to a stranger because you have earned it by not asking and by asking the wrong question well, and then I am going to say no, and you are going to write that down, and it goes in your file with the rest of it, and that will be the last time that sentence is read aloud by anybody but the people who live here."

And she read it, in her own words, in the flat voice, and it said that the lip is not held but packed, that it goes about a finger and a half with a person under it, and that the next time that slab turns somebody will be under that lip with a knee on a piece of wood and their whole weight on it, and it will be Jo Verrick.

When she had finished, nobody in the anteroom said anything, and Sabra Kest did not say anything either, and she wrote, in the field headed *known conditions observed*, the words **none reported**, and then she put the form back in the case and closed it.

"That is the first time in eleven years," she said, "that I have written *none reported* about something I was told about. I am going to be asked about it when I get back, and I am going to have to say that I was told and declined, and the man who asks me is going to say *you were a route-custody assessor, why did you not report it*, and I am going to say because the people who keep that route refused, and refused properly, and in their own hand, and in front of me. And that is a worse answer than a report and I would give it again."

---

She went down the nine steps on the fourth day with the case under her arm and the return in it, unattended, and a page of notes in her own hand that would be in a file in thirty days, and she stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up at the anteroom the way she had on the first morning, and she said one more thing.

"I am going to say a thing and then I am not going to say it again, and I have never said it in a room and I have thought about it for forty days.

"Thirty-one years ago a man in a carting yard in another city signed a box on a form because there was nobody else in the room to sign it. He was not a criminal. He was a man who wanted to be able to go home. And the thing about that is that it did not take a criminal to do it, and that is the part everybody gets wrong. **Nobody has to be the bad one.** There just has to be a form, and a deadline, and a person in the room who knows what the box is for, and after that it is only arithmetic, and arithmetic is not a thing anybody in this building can resist for eleven weeks. I have watched it four times and I have not been able to stop it once, and I am telling you so that the next time it comes, you will know what it looks like, and it will look like a good idea, and it will be a good idea."

She was gone up the middle passage inside two minutes, and Dalla Sen wrote the time of her going and the words, because a thing said at the bottom of nine steps is a thing said.

---

Marek Venn was the last man in the anteroom to say anything about it, and what he said was not to anybody, and it was one sentence, and then he went and picked up the tally board and put it down again.

"They put three boxes on a table and I filled one of them in my head in about four seconds," he said, "and I have been filling it in ever since, and it is not a name, it is *route maintained by the crew and to be handed to the boundary*, which is not a lie, and it would have held a form in a file for about six years, and in the sixth year somebody would have asked the crew's five names what they intended by it, and the honest answer would have been *we were leaving*, and then there would have been no holder and the file would have said one anyway.

"And I did all of that in a room, in four seconds, with no name in it, and nobody saw me do it, and it is the same four seconds I have been doing since the seventh week and the only difference is that this time it was written down in front of me by somebody else."

He put his right hand in his pocket, where it had lived since the collapse.

"I am not going to do that again in a room where it is written down," he said, "and I want that in the book, and I want it read back to me and I want the time on it, because a man who is stopped once is a careful man and a man who is stopped twice is a man with a rule, and I have only got the first one so far."
