# Chapter 84 — Not In Agreement

On the fourth day of the sixteenth week the corridor was shut and could not be opened, which is how the boundary got the argument it had been putting off since the first day of the thirteenth week, and the argument got it because the alternative was to be on a stair and look at four treads, and the road was closed.

Here is what a boundary can do with a day it cannot go anywhere on.

At the fifth bell Dalla Sen did a thing she had wanted to do for ten days and had not had a reason to do, which was to read two sentences against each other on the same line, and the two sentences were in the clerk's book already and had been in it since the sixth day of the thirteenth week.

One of the two was Renn Vale's eighth term, in Renn's hand, read out and left in the book: *if it turns out to be a door, it is a door in a building I keep and nobody goes down it.*

The other was a word in a column on a sheet of paper on a dowel in a locked store, and Dalla Sen could only read it out, because the page had not left the store and was not going to: ***none.***

"There," she said. "**A boundary now has two ways of saying *not on any day*, and one of them is nine hundred years old and in a store and one of them is a woman's on her own landing, and they are not in agreement, and nobody has asked them to be, and I put that in the book in the fourteenth week and I am reading it out because this is the morning this room is going to need it.**"

Nobody in the anteroom had ever heard those two things next to each other before, and the whole of the room's attention went quiet in the way it goes quiet about four times in fifteen weeks.

"They are not two ways of saying the same thing," said Renn Vale, from the upper landing, without coming down. "**They are not the same kind of sentence and anybody in this room who puts them side by side and says *and they are in agreement* is doing arithmetic on two different things.** Mine is a promise. I have said to a woman on a landing that nobody in this boundary will go down a door in a building I keep, and I have put my name to it and I have said I will keep saying it, and a promise can be broken by a person, and that is what makes it worth anything to the person it was made to.

"And that is a *condition* on a piece of paper. *None.* It is not a promise to anybody, because there is nobody on that page, and that is the thing about it that I have been not saying for ten days. **A page with a word in a column and no person in it is not keeping to a promise. It is describing a room. And a room described in nine hundred words by nobody does not get a promise, it gets a habit, and a habit lasts until somebody stops and there is nobody to notice.** So the two are not in agreement because the two are not both sentences, and the page is not a promise, and I will not have this room frightened of a piece of paper the way a room gets frightened of a person."

"And is the page right," said Bel Ordry, "or is your slate right?"

"I have no idea," said Renn Vale. "**That is the first thing I have said in this building in fifteen weeks that I have not known, and I would like the clerk to enter it, because I have had a slate with seven terms and an eighth on it for five weeks and I have been treating it as though knowing a thing about a door made me responsible for it.**"

---

Nera Oduya came down two steps from the top of three, which is as far as she comes, and stood there for about a minute without saying anything, and the room waited, and then she asked one question and it was not the one anybody expected.

"Read me the count on the fourth row again."

"Ten," said Nera. "And it is the highest number on the page, and this anteroom holds ten including one litter for no more than two hours, and the two tens are the same figure and not the same number, and I have known that since the second day of the thirteenth week and I have not said it, and I am saying it now and I am not saying it because it is a meaning. I am saying it because a woman who is going to argue about a row needs the rest of the page, and the rest of the page is the only thing anybody in this room can honestly look at. **One of those tens is what a room has held, nine hundred years ago, in a building we have never been inside. The other one is what a room may hold, in this one, with the door open and the pump running and a woman on a litter. They are the same figure and there is no reason on this earth for them to be, and a coincidence is a coincidence, and I have not got a month to spend on a coincidence.**"

"And the ninth row has a one," said Ilsa Norr.

"**The ninth row has a one and it is the only number on that page that is not an occupancy.**" Nera said it flatly and from where she was. "Every other count on that sheet is *how many people a room holds.* A classroom, a kitchen, a hall. That is what a count is when it is next to a room. And the ninth row is a one, and a one next to a room is either one person living in it or one person put in charge of it, and this building has spent two weeks arguing about which, and the argument has been about the *when* column and the work column, and **nobody in this room has asked whether we are even reading the same column.**

"A page that keeps nine rooms with counts and conditions and no names anywhere is a page about occupancy. A single figure that is a person and not a head-count is a **staffing**, and a staffing is a different kind of number, and if you put a staffing into an occupancy column then whoever wrote it either made a mistake nine hundred years ago or did something nobody in this room has thought of doing yet. And I am not going to stand on three steps and tell you which, because I do not know, and **I have wanted a document to tell me something since the seventh week and the thing I have got is a document that cannot tell me which and that is not going to change because I want it to.**"

"Then it is two things," said Bel Ordry, "and we have had one of them since the thirteenth week and the other one since this morning, and the first one is a room with a person posted to it and no work, and the second one is a column that has a staffing in it and no heading, and I do not need to be told which of those is worse."

"No," said Renn Vale. "**You do not, and the reason is that both of them are this boundary.**"

---

The argument that decided the morning came from the school door, and it came from a woman who had not been able to say that the school was hers for a week, and it came out of her the way it had come out of her the first time, standing up, in front of people, before she was ready.

"Right," said Ilsa Norr. "**Then copy it.** That is the honest answer and nobody in this room has had it because everybody in this room is frightened of the page.

"We have started a schedule this week with three columns and one of them empty, and I have watched a room full of careful people stop dead at an empty column, and the reason is that the empty column frightens us. And a row with a count and a condition and no work in it is what a schedule *is*, nine hundred years or nine weeks, and the dowel has eight of them and six of the third column empty, and it has been sitting in a store being a mystery for a fortnight, and it has not hurt anybody.

"So: when this schedule is finished — and it is not finished and I am not going to pretend it is — it gets a row for that room. One in the count. *None* in the condition. Nothing in the work, because we do not know what the work is, and **a schedule that admits a room it has not worked out is the most honest object a boundary can make**, and if the ninth row is what we think it is then the ninth row is the row where this boundary tells the truth about itself, and it does it in a column, in our own hand, where somebody can find it."

"No," said Nera Oduya.

Ilsa Norr did not sit down, because she had not stood up in order to be answered from three steps, and she said, "Say why, and say it as long as you need to, because I have made this argument to myself four times since Sunday and I would like to be beaten properly."

"A blank row in an old document is a mystery," said Nera, "and a blank row in a new document is an **omission**, and a stranger reading an omission does not think *we have not worked this out.* A stranger reading an omission thinks *there is somebody in that room and they are not being paid*, and that is not a thought a stranger has to work at, because **we have said it out loud in this room, a boy of fourteen said it, and it is the obvious reading, and it is the reading a clerk forty days away with a stamp and no reason to come and look would arrive at inside a minute.**

"And then we have published the accusation before anybody has made it, and we will have published it about a room that is four steps below a landing, behind a rail, that has a count of one and a condition of *none* and no work, and there is not one person in it, and the page we publish will be in a hand and it will be readable and it will be true, and being true will not help, because **the accusation is not about the room. The accusation is about us.**"

"And what do we do about it," said Renn Vale, from the landing, and the whole room turned, because that was the question and everybody in the anteroom had been waiting for somebody to ask it out loud since Monday.

"**Nothing**," she said. "And I will say the nothing in my own words and the clerk will write it in my own words and read it out, and it is the second time in this building I have written *nothing* in a hand and both times it has been about a room.

"A room four steps below the far side of the fixed first landing, behind the rail, is not on this boundary's rota, is not in this boundary's work, is not on a page a person can be paid against, and is not walked by anybody, and will not be walked by anybody this month. **Nobody goes down. I am not asking to be thanked for that and I am not asking anybody to give it up.** And the day somebody goes down, the keeping begins, and it will not stop, and I have said that out loud in this room twice and I am saying it a third time because a page has been read at me in a kitchen and a man has read a one out of it and a woman has asked for the row to be copied, and a room that is nine hundred years old is not going to make me careful. I am already careful. **I am the careful one. That is not a compliment, it is the job.**"

"Entered in your own hand," said Dalla Sen, "and read out again, and the ninth line is *none* and the ninth line is not *held*, and nobody goes down the four treads, and that stands."

"And three other things," said Renn Vale, "and the second one is the one that costs.

"I am not having that stair marked. I know somebody was about to say it and I know why it is a good idea, and Bel — a man with a good idea is not a reason, and I know what the idea is because I watched three people arrive at it while I was talking. If we cut a mark in that stone to say *do not go*, then we have made a claim on a room, and a claim is a thing a person can be asked about, and I would rather there be nothing there at all. **No mark. Nothing on the stone. The only thing that says do not go is a slate and six people and a woman who will say it out loud every week of her life, and that is the whole of the arrangement and it is not enough and it is what we have.**"

"And the last thing," she said, "is that a boundary is allowed to do nothing about a room it has found, and to write down that it has done nothing, and to name the person who decided, and I am that person today and nobody is to relieve me of it before the end of the month."

---

Marek Venn had been in the room for the whole of it and had not said one word, which was not like him, and Dalla Sen noticed and Nera Oduya noticed from three steps up, and at about eleven minutes past the sixth bell he put his slate down on the bench, blank side up, and left it there, and Leena Orr, from the litter, said the only sentence she said all day.

"You have said nothing for forty minutes and that is not nothing, that is you deciding not to be the man who settles it, and I would like it noticed, because you have been the man who settles it in every room I have been in since the sixth week and today you did not, and I do not know whether that is growth or whether you have run out of things to be sure about, and I would like somebody to check it in a week."

"I will," said Marek Venn. "That is the correct use of a question and I am going to write it in my own book and I am not going to show it to anybody, which is the first thing I have done in this building in a week that I would want written down, and it is not going to be."

Then, from the top of three steps, Nera Oduya said a sentence into the middle of the floor and the room heard it and nobody argued with it, and it is in the book.

"**Then let us look at it.** Not go down. Stand at the top and look, on a day the school is not coming and the pump is in its window, with the terms read at the school door, and come back and say what can be said, and if what can be said is *we do not know*, then that is what goes in the book, in the room's own words, on a page nobody can carry out of this building, and I will read it out at the fifth bell for as long as it is true.

"And I am not asking the crew. **I am asking nobody today.** I am going to ask a man with a board, in a room, in front of the clerk, on a day when he can say no, and if he says yes then it is a named work and it goes in a column and it costs the boundary two hours of somebody who is leaving in the spring, and that is the cheapest thing anybody in this room will spend this month and I am not going to spend it out of a sentence on a Sunday."

The corridor stayed shut for the fourth and fifth days, and the school ran one day out of seven in the sixteenth week, and Ilsa Norr entered that in her own words on the fifth day and asked for it to be read out once, and the fourth day's closure came back off a page in the clerk's hand and into a running account with a number on it, and at the sixth bell on the fifth day Sefu Aran said out loud that the road would be open again on the next morning, and that the standing three days in seven would stand after that and would be argued about in the eighteenth week, and he gave the reasons, and the reasons were a cook's number and a school that had lost a week of itself in three days, and every person in that room knew that was the least respectable reason available in this building and it was the reason they were going to use it, and he said so too, out loud, twice.
