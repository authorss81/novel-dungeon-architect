# Chapter 178 — A Form Filled In Correctly

The carrier came up the nine steps at the second bell on the sixth day of the thirty-fourth week, a Saturday, and stood on the anteroom floor, which is not what a man with a road's business does, and put a slip of paper on the trestle and took his hand off it. The school did not run, because a sixth day has not been a school day for a long time and nobody has put it back.

"**Middle gate. I came down the passage on the sixth day because it is one of the three days in seven this corridor is open and a man with a load's business waits for an open day like everybody else.**" He kept his hands where they could be seen. "**There is a board at the middle gate that was not there when I went up. It is a new board, and it is nailed over an old one, and there is a form on it, and both of the two lines on that form are filled in.**"

Dalla Sen did not touch the slip.

"**The two lines,**" she said.

"**A route-holder's name, and the name of the person responsible for a load.**"

"**The names.**"

"**A post-holder of the cleared-risk office for the ninth district, and the same name again in the second line.**"

Nobody in the anteroom said anything for about two strokes of the pump. Bel Ordry put his hand on the coat where the page was, and then took it off, and Dalla Sen took up the chalk and then put it down again without making a mark.

"**All of it,**" she said, "**before I write anything, and in the order a clerk would give it, because I am going to have to write this down and I would rather write down a clerk's reasons than mine.**"

"**The form is for a road. Not yours, and not the gate you use. The section on the form is the ground between the end of the middle passage and the middle gate, and it is called a road on the form, and it is a working road, which means a road a person carries a load along.**" He looked at the slate and not at the room. "**And the second line is filled in, and that is what makes it a working road, and the clerk filled it in on the ordinary day with an ordinary pen, and there is a seal number on the corner of it and the seal is the insurance office's and not the Cartwrights'.**"

Bel Ordry leaned over the trestle without touching it.

"**Whose hand,**" he said.

"**I do not have the clerk's name. I have got the way of the hand.**" The carrier took a breath. "**It is the hand of the woman who took your form off your board in the fourth week of your twenty-ninth. I have carried her hand before, on a slip, when she set *a week is not a month* in a book. And I am telling you because you are going to hear a hand and decide what it means, and I would rather you heard it from me.**"

"**I have no name to enter and I am not going to invent one,**" said Dalla Sen. "**The same hand is the hand of the twenty-ninth week. It goes in as a fact and not as a reason and not as an excuse.**"

Renn Vale had come down to the trestle and was reading the slip upside down, which she can do.

"**What it does,**" she said. "**Not what anybody meant.**"

The carrier looked at the slip again and read it as though it were a list of timbers.

"**It does this. On that form a road with a named person responsible for a load on it is a road people carry loads along. That is the rule and it is the rule on the sheet at the market gate too, and I have seen it four hundred times.**" He put his finger on the paper without touching it. "**So the ground your people walk across to get to the market road is now a working road, and it has a named person responsible for what is on it, and a person responsible for a load is a person who can say what crosses it and what does not.**"

"**Then the part you did not want to give,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**A person who has been on that ground carrying a bundle of somebody else's pot is on a working road, and a working road can be closed to a bundle.**" He took his hand off the paper. "**And a person in a crew with a survey lamp and a line and a note-book is a person carrying a load.**"

Marek Venn sat down. He did it without deciding to, the way a person sits down when the thing that has been holding him up turns out to be a chair, and the book stayed shut on the table where he had left it yesterday.

The lamp, the line, the notebook, four people, three days, a survey of a void behind a party wall. Since Friday morning all of it had been a question of permission, and he had gone to sleep on Friday certain that the permission would be given. A competent clerk gives permission for a survey the way a competent clerk fills in a line, and a survey of a void is the most ordinary thing anybody has ever asked for. There was nothing in it that anybody could object to.

That had been the strength of it, and it turned out to be the whole problem, because a thing nobody can object to is a thing nobody has to allow either.

"**Your own words,**" said Dalla Sen. "**And as the man who drew it, not as the man who is disappointed. I have watched you answer a question you were not asked three times this week and I am not going to be the fourth to ask you one.**"

"**I asked the wrong clerk the wrong question.**" He opened the book and shut it again. "**I did not ask to be let in. I asked to be asked to survey, and those are the same sentence and they cost different things, and the one that costs is the second one, and I had it backwards for three days.**"

"**The half against,**" said Renn Vale. "**Nobody in this room is going to hand you a comfort this morning.**"

"**I did not know there was a difference until a sheet of paper came down a passage, and a man who cannot see a difference before it arrives has been lucky, not careful.**" He put his knuckles on the table. "**And the other half is that I would have asked the second question anyway if I had thought of it, and the reason I did not think of it is that the question I wanted to ask did not need me to be allowed into anything. I wanted to be a man who found out. I have wanted to be that man since the second week of this book.**"

Dalla Sen entered it in his own words and entered that a man in this room has said out loud for the second morning running that the thing he wants is to be the man who finds out, and that she is entering the twice and not the once because a clerk who counts a man's humility has misunderstood the arithmetic.

Iven Tallo had not moved from the anvil end, and the reason he gave for it was that a room had not yet got to the part of the form that concerned him.

"**Then here it is,**" he said. "**And the half against my part, because there is one.**"

"**A surveyor cannot take a lamp into a void that is carrying a wall, and I do not know what a void carries, and a survey of a void that is carrying a wall is not a survey. It is a reading, and readings have a way of being wrong that a person does not like.**" He turned the chisel and did not use it. "**And I have said for thirty-four weeks that I cannot tell you when a shim dies, and I have no more right to a void than I have to a shim, and this morning I have been the only man in the room who was not disappointed, and I would like it entered that a man who is not disappointed is not the same as a man who is right.**"

Nobody improved on that either, and Mero Senn said the sentence he says, once, out loud, and then said it again in a different shape, which he has done four times in thirty-four weeks.

"**A plan with a named injured person in it is not a plan,**" he said, from the doorway, with his bag against the wall. "**So say the name. Which person gets hurt if this boundary tries to find out what is in that wall without being allowed into it?**"

"**Nobody,**" said Marek Venn.

"**The second one, then. Which person gets hurt if a survey party of four goes into a void carrying a wall, on a day somebody is standing on the landing side holding a lamp and somebody else is holding a lamp and the fourth one is holding a note-book?**"

"**The fourth one,**" said Marek Venn.

"**Then the fourth one is the name, and the fourth one is you, because you have said you would go in, and I heard you say it on Friday and so did four other people in this room.**" Mero Senn did not move. "**And I am not saying you cannot go in. I am saying that the moment you go in, this building has a plan again, and the plan has a person in it, and the person is the man who finds out, and that is the plan I have refused to certify for two volumes.**"

"**The half against that,**" said Renn Vale, "**and now, because there is a room in here that has been kind to you all week and a room is entitled to the other half.**"

"**The half against it is that there has not been a plan in that wall for thirty-four weeks at all, and a void nobody has looked into is not a plan and it is not the absence of one. It is a thing carrying a wall that nobody is watching,**" said Iven Tallo. "**And if the man who finds out is the man who goes in, then he goes in with a line and a lamp and a person behind him who can pull him out, and that is not a man finding out. That is a rescue with a note-book.**" The carpenter put the chisel down. "**And I will not sign that, and nobody asked me to, and I have said it so that it is in the room before somebody asks me about it on a day when it is more convenient for them.**"

Then the carrier was asked the only question that had been waiting, and he answered it flat.

"**Has anybody said no to anything?**"

"**No,**" said the carrier. "**Nobody has said no. There is a form, and the form has been filled in, and a seal is on it, and the seal is a real one, and the clerk did her work and her work was correct. I have been up and down that road for nine years and I could not tell you one thing on that sheet that is wrong.**"

"**Then the thing that is true about it,**" said Bel Ordry.

"**The thing that is true about it is that I can carry a bundle of somebody else's pot up that ground on one day and not on the next, and neither of those two facts has anybody's name on it, and both of them come off a board in an office.**"

Dalla Sen entered that, in his words, and entered that no person was refused and that nothing was untrue and that the whole of it was done correctly, and entered that she is going to carry that sentence in this book for the rest of her life and would like a person in a year to read it and understand that it is not a complaint about a clerk.

Then Renn Vale did the thing she had told a room on Wednesday she was going to do on the sixth day of a week, and she did it standing up, with the slate under her arm and nobody standing near her.

"**You said on Wednesday that I would get an answer on the sixth day of a week and that it would not be the answer I expect, and that it was a schedule and not a threat, and I am aware of what I said when I said it, and here it is on a Saturday.**" She did not sit down. "**The anteroom's second way out is a word. It has been a word for thirty-four weeks. I have kept it and I have said it and the clerk has repeated it and the room has used it to empty forty people out of two rooms into one.**"

"**All of it,**" said Dalla Sen.

"**It is a way out of a room. It is not a way out of a building. That is the sentence I have known since the first week and have not said out loud, and I am not saying it because of the second way out. I am saying it because a carpenter said in this room ten days ago that a person does not go out through a word, and nobody answered him, and a keeper came back into this building a week ago and declined to answer him as first-week decisiveness, and a man has been trying to cut a hole in a wall for three days so that the word would have a place behind it.**" She put the slate on the trestle. "**So the answer everybody wanted from me on a Saturday was a second door, and I have not got one, and what I have got is a sentence I have been keeping for thirty-four weeks because a room that has got a sentence it does not say out loud has got a way out it can use without asking anybody.**"

Nobody said anything for a while, and the pump got two strokes in.

Dalla Sen entered it in her words, and entered that a keeper gave the answer she promised on a day of the week and that the answer was a sentence and not a door, and entered that she has been asked nine times this month whether a term with a word in it is going to be answered and has said nine times that it is not hers to answer, and that a woman who keeps six things answered it herself on a Saturday and nobody asked her to.

Bel Ordry had been looking at the slate on the trestle and not at her.

"**You have not said the other half,**" he said. "**And I will.**"

"**I know you will,**" said Renn Vale. "**A sentence is not a plan, and a man in this room has spent Friday and Saturday trying to turn it into one, and the sentence I have just given him is the reason he cannot, and I have known that since Wednesday and I have let him spend two days finding it out in a room instead of telling him at a kitchen table. That is a kindness I did him and he has paid for it and I am entering that I did it on purpose.**"

The sixth bell came round and the clerk entered the day's number and the total in the book and read neither of them out, and the count was read at one hundred and thirteen, and she did not walk the board. Two today and two hundred and thirty-nine since the keeper signed. The boundary is at three hundred and fifty-six and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, and the two columns reconcile. The counter is at seventeen and stays seventeen, because a counter is a thing a clerk stops looking at on purpose.

She entered the reason for not reading them, which is that a form with two lines filled in correctly in a competent hand has been in this room since the second bell, and a clerk does not stand up and read a total on a morning when the only total in her head is a number of people.

Nobody has been asked for a sixth piece of work, and the reason is that a permission was refused by nobody. A clerk who asks five people to carry a piece of work on the back of a refusal that nobody made is asking them to carry a mood.

The pot is nine days. And the count. Wen Pask is one hundred and thirteen days and one hundred and thirteen nights into a count that is his own, and it is not going to be one hundred and fourteen, and she has said in twenty-two weeks running that she is going to keep writing it.

And she did not walk the board, and the reason is that a form came down a passage with a post-holder's name in both of the two lines, and a board is a column with a heading on it, and a room that has just been shown what a heading can do to an ordinary person is not going to ask a man to read a heading out loud in the same ten minutes.

Nobody was sent anywhere, and the school door at the far end of the nine steps was shut and had nothing wedged in it, and the page where a person's hours would go was blank and had three conditions on it, and the third of them said that nobody was to be sent anywhere to make it true.
