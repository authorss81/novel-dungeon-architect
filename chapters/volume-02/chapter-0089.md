# Chapter 89 — The Middle Gate Answers

The woman at the counter came down the middle passage herself, on the fourth day of the seventeenth week, behind the courier by a road nobody in this building can put a date to, and she got to the bottom of the nine steps and sat down on it, which nobody in this building had seen anybody from the middle gate do, and which Bel Ordry afterwards said was the most alarming thing in the fortnight.

"I am not tired," said Nell Ferrin, when Dalla Sen asked her three times. "I am fifty-one and I have been on a counter since I was seventeen and I have spent thirty-four years standing up and I have got bad legs and a good back and this is the stairs. I am going to sit on your step and I am going to want the whole of that anteroom in about ten minutes, so you can put six of them down the nine steps and let me know when I have got the number I am allowed."

Her name went in the clerk's book on her own authority, and her business went in as *counter, the middle gate's store, nineteen years, and the woman who has read every paper on that timber to every man who has asked*, and Dalla Sen read that back twice because a clerk in this building had learned in eight days that the difference between a refusal and a withdrawal is the difference between a clerk and a courier, and a woman behind a counter is neither.

Her satchel had three things in it. The office's answer on a folded sheet with a clerk's mark at the top. A copy of the release, sent back, which nobody had expected and which Dalla Sen took with both hands. And a fourth thing, loose, which was a store's issue slip with a column on it headed *pails up* with a number under it, and a second column with no heading at all, and Nell Ferrin said the second column was not hers and had never been hers and had been on that slip since before she had the counter.

"Four days up a road," said Tams Quill, "on a different kind of paper, with a different office at the top of it, and a column with nothing over the top of it. **I have got one of those on a board in this anteroom and I have refused to head it for five weeks. Two institutions a season apart, the same disease, and the woman standing on the step has been looking at hers for nineteen years and has never once been asked.**"

"I have been asked," said Nell Ferrin. "By you. In the last minute."

They read the office's answer out at the fourth bell, and it was read by the clerk and then by the keeper and then by the woman from the counter herself, and it is in the book, and and it is the first document this Hearthhold has ever received.

---

"*To the Hearthhold boundary, in answer to your notice of the first day, and to a question put by a courier of this office on the sixth day.

"The Middle accepts the notice. It is a good notice. It is the first thing anybody has put in writing to this office about that timber in the whole of the time the office has kept the count, and the office did not know it was doing nothing and the office now knows it was doing something, and both of those are worth saying to a boundary and one of them is worth saying to a person.

"The Middle cannot put the water-lock in front of the people behind it. Not this season and not by the middle of it. A water-lock is a sill, a bed, a wedge of hardwood and two hands a day for four days. The sill is the four hundred paces. Your notice has come the hold off that timber and the timber is the Middle's, and the Middle has no carpenter with a bond, and the trade's rule will not let a man without one take a chisel to it, and the Middle is not going to break that rule to be kind to four households.

"So: **the hold is off, and the timber is ours, and the timber is at the second gate, and the work is ours, and the timber is standing in a store with a saw beside it and no one cutting it, and that is what your notice has done and the office is not sorry about it and is not going to pretend to be sorry about it either.**

"And the second answer, which is the one the office has thought about for two days and which the office would rather not have written.

"**The Middle has halved what it puts up that passage.** The office counts what leaves its own counter, and the count was twelve pails on a day your corridor is open and eight on a day it is shut, and the office has had it on a slip for a season and did not know what the slip was for. Now it knows. **The slip is what the middle sends you, and the office was counting its own side of a road, and every load of it was a delivery that was not a delivery. It was a boundary expecting a plank.**

"And a boundary that has said in writing it is not coming has ended the reason for the carrying, and the carrying has stopped, and it will not start again on our word, and the office is not going to pretend to four households that a notice read twice at a counter was a help.

"The count now is **six.** Six on a day your corridor is open and six on a day it is shut, and the difference between that and what you had is carried by the four households of the second gate, who have their own water and their own four sets of steps and who have had them since the cold closed and who did not ask the office for any of this and are not going to be told about it by the office.

"And the office's last line, and the office has thought about whether to write it and is writing it.

"**You asked us to stop holding the timber and put the water-lock in front of the people behind it. We have stopped holding the timber. We cannot put the water-lock in front of anybody. If anybody at the second gate is owed an answer by anybody, it is not us and it is not you, and the office would be obliged if a boundary which has been careful with a piece of paper for a fortnight would say so out loud, in a room, to its own people, before the first of the month.**"*

---

There was a silence of a length that Dalla Sen entered, because she entered silences in this building when they were about things.

"That is the best piece of writing that has come down that road since we came up a shaft," said Tams Quill, "and I want it entered that I said so, and I am aware of what I am, and the office did its job and it did it well and it has just told us to go and say something out loud in our own room, and **I am not going to pretend that a competent office being competent is the same as a friendly office, and it is not, and I have been in this building fifteen weeks and I have never once wanted a thing more to be a friendly office and I would like that written down as a fact about me.**"

"Half the carriage," said Sefu Aran, from the wheel. "Right. Dalla, read the count back to me off the work, and then read the terms, and I want the room to hear the two next to each other before I say anything else about them."

Dalla Sen read the water-carrying: **every day in seven; twelve pails on a day the corridor is open and eight on a day it is shut; eight pails is two crossings of four and never one crossing; by named people, at named hours, at a walk; priced by the people who carry it; received by this boundary and not held by it; and on the morning the slab under that lip turns, the work stops and the lever is not touched by anybody.**

"**Right,**" said Tams Quill, before Jo Verrick could say the number, because he had got there first and he wanted the room to have the shape of it before the figure. "The slip counts what comes up the middle passage. The work counts what reaches the kitchen. For five weeks those were the same number and this week they are not, and the difference is what the middle has stopped sending, and nobody in this room has found the other half of it, and there is no other half of it, because this boundary has one road up it and no well and no seam anybody has tested for drinking water and no second pair of hands in a place to get any."

"**Six,"** said Jo Verrick, from the frame.

"Six is one crossing of four and one crossing of two," said Tams Quill, "and that is two crossings, and the work says eight pails is two crossings of four. **So the page is wrong.** Not the arithmetic — the arithmetic has been right for a month. The page has been right about the world and the world has changed under it, and the difference between those two things is a piece of paper read twice at a counter four days up a road, and **the page is not wrong about six. The page is wrong about there being nothing else, and I would like the room to hear where the wrong is, because the wrong is in a place nobody has looked.**"

"Four at a time is Renn's and it is not a preference. It is four because four is what the corridor will take at a walk with a bearer on the near lip and a woman on the string and a plinth read, and six is not a whole number of four, and **a plan that cannot be divided into its own terms is not a plan with a smaller number in it, it is a plan that has been broken by somebody else from a long way off without touching it.**"

"Then it is one crossing of four and one of two," said Renn Vale, from the landing, "and the second one is two, and a party of two is not harder than a party of four, and the terms are four at a time, which is a maximum, and a maximum of four permits two, and I am the woman who wrote the maximum and I am not going to have the office of a road four days away set the head-count of a crossing at this building's air gate by accident.

"So: **six, in two crossings, four and then two, every day in seven, at a walk.** And the keeper has to change his own page in his own hand, and the change has to be read out, and it is the second time in a month that a document a person has written has been made wrong by somebody who never touched the building."

"It is the first time in my life," said Sefu Aran, "that I have had to correct a work in the middle of the week because a competent office did its job properly and told the truth. **Struck in the keeper's words: twelve and eight, and in their place six.** Read it out, and read the seventh term out with it, because the seventh term is not a number and it does not change and the room should have to hear the two together."

"And the water does not stop coming in the spring," said Mikka Hale, from the frame, where she had been standing since the second bell with her hand flat on the wood, "and I want that said in this room in the words I am going to use.

"Twelve days. It has gone nineteen, sixteen, fifteen, fourteen, thirteen, twelve, and it has never once gone up, and every one of those drops is in a book with a reason and a time on it and two hands. And from tomorrow the water is **two thirds** of what it was on an open day and three quarters on a shut one, and it is half of what a fortnight ago it cost a road to get here, and **the number on my board is not going to change on the day it halves, and Jo Verrick is going to tell you the week it does, and it will not be this one, and I would rather you heard the arithmetic than the number.** It changes on the day the pot is thinner, and that will be a Friday, or a Sunday, or a Thursday like today, and I am not going to be asked to guess which, and nobody in this room is going to be comforted.**

"But I am going to say the other half of it and I have had it ready since the Wednesday, when the number went to twelve and a page came back up a road with a stranger's hand on it, and I did not know the two of them were in the same month.

"**Nothing has been promised to those four households by anybody in this room, and nothing has been refused to them by anybody in this room, and they have not been in this room.** Four of them: two brothers, their sister, and one house that only carries. And the whole of what they have from this boundary in the seventeenth week is a second page read twice at a counter. And that page is honest and it is the best document we have ever produced, and it contains one instruction, and the instruction is *say so out loud in a room to your own people before the first of the month.*"

She stopped there, and did not say the rest of it, and the room sat with it.

Then Nell Ferrin said, from the step, in the flat voice of a woman who has read a great many things out loud:

"**She is right and she has stopped half a sentence early because she is being careful with me, which I have not been in nineteen years and I have noticed.** So I will finish it for her, because I am the only person in this room who is going back up that road.

"**The second gate is told about this boundary by a piece of paper and not by a person.** The paper is ours, and it goes up a road with a woman behind a counter, and there is nobody in the four days in between who is anybody's to anybody. And that is exactly what the keeper of that lock asked in five words on a piece of painted board, and he asked it on the Saturday before last, and we are answering it on a Thursday with a sentence telling us to go and answer it ourselves.

"I am fifty-one and I have bad legs and I did not ask to come and I am going back up this road on Friday morning, and **I have not got four days either, and nobody in this room has, and that is not a thing anybody can fix this week and I am not going to be one of the people who says it can.** And I will say the thing nobody in this room is going to say, because I am a stranger and it costs me nothing: **you have got a page that says you will not come, and a page that says you have one work, and a page that says there is nothing else, and all three of them are true, and there is not one of them that a person at the bottom of four sets of steps can be helped by.**"

---

Quieter than any of it, and later in the day, was a page with three columns on it in a teacher's hand on a butcher's sheet, and it happened because Nell Ferrin had been in the room for two hours.

She had not said a word about the schedule. She asked about it once, at the second hour, and she asked the way a woman asks who has been on the other end of a counter for nineteen years, which is by asking what it is *for*.

"And it has three columns," she said, "and two of them have things in them, and one of them does not, and it has got no name on it, and it is not a month old, and it is in a schoolteacher's hand on the back of a butcher's sheet, and I have filed nine hundred documents and I want to tell you the one thing I know and it is the only thing I am qualified to say in this room."

"Go on," said Nera Oduya.

"**Every document I have ever filed says what somebody must do.** A notice, an order, a warrant, a return, a form — every single one of them is a sentence with a person on the end of it that is going to have to be obeyed by somebody. Yours is the first one I have ever filed that says what somebody will *not* do, and that is why it got read twice at my counter, and I have thought about that for two days and here it is: **a refusal is the only sentence in an office that everybody can act on.** You can act on it. I did. I could not act on any of the other nine hundred and I did not want to.

"And the trouble with it, and I am the woman who reads these out, is that your page is a page that cannot be used against you — and the reason it cannot be used against you is that it does not say what you *will* do. So a man at my counter who has to answer a question about you has nothing to answer *with*, and I had to send four days up a road to find out that, and I did not have to, and I would not have had to if your second page had one more column in it."

The anteroom was extremely quiet.

"So I am going to say the thing and I am going to say it as a woman behind a counter and not as a person of this boundary, and if it is wrong then it is a stranger's sentence and it can go in the book as one.

"**You want a fourth column. Put in it what this boundary will do.** Not in a column of names — you have already found out what a column of names is, don't argue with me about it, I have read the row. Put in it the *work*. A count, a condition, and a thing that gets done, and a fourth column that says *this boundary undertakes.* And write the date on it and not the season, because a date is not a promise, a date is a fact about when you said it, and I have watched your clerk write one thing without a date on it and I would like her to write two more with dates on them so that the one without is on purpose and not an accident of a woman being frightened."

Nera Oduya came down one step, which was the fourth time in seventeen weeks, and said:

"No, and it is the answer I gave in the anteroom on Saturday and I am giving it again with a better reason in front of a woman who has filed nine hundred documents.

"**A column that says what this boundary will do is a column of obligations. And an obligation written on paper is an obligation somebody can call in.** In three months there will be a bad week — there is going to be a bad week, the pot is at twelve days and it is only going down and the road is shut four days in seven — and on that bad day somebody forty days away will read a page in a schoolteacher's hand and say *you undertook.* And there will be no version of that anybody here can refuse, because they did undertake, in writing, in a form that can be carried out of this building.

"And a page that promises is a page that has already broken."

"Then what is the fourth column for," said Bel Ordry, "if it is not for promising?"

Renn Vale answered it from the landing, and she said it slowly, and she had clearly been carrying it since the middle of the second hour.

"**For refusing.** And I know what that sounds like and I know what it is, because I have been the woman on this landing who says no before the argument starts for five weeks, and I will say it out loud: *I am the last person in this building who should be trusted with a column of refusals on a page a stranger can hold.*

"But listen to the reason, because it is the reason and not the woman. A column of *will not* is a list of things this boundary has promised not to do. And a promise not to do is a promise like any other. **Give it to a judge, or a hearing, or an office with a stamp, and it becomes *this boundary undertook not to* — and then every single one of them is something somebody can ask this boundary to explain, and explain, and justify, in a room, with a stranger in it.**

"And this building's refusals are not the kind of thing that can be put on a page and read out. The wash rooms are kept and there is no work in them and **no name is ever to be put against them by anybody including by accident in four years, and that is a woman's own condition and it is the reason a woman in this building is not on a rota, and it cannot go on a piece of paper, and it must not, and I will not have it copied into anything anybody can carry.** And the ninth line is *none* and it is nobody's and it is not on this page and it is not going to be. And I have said no to a form and to a slate and to a list and to a night room and to a deck, and every one of those was right, and **not one of them can be written down in a column and handed to a man with a stamp, because the moment it is written it is a thing that is owed rather than a thing that is refused.**"

"So the fourth column is empty," said Nell Ferrin, from the step.

"The fourth column is empty," said Renn Vale, "**and it is going to be empty, and I am not going to be allowed to head it, and neither is the air engineer, and neither is the clerk, because the whole of what a column of refusals needs to be safe is for it to have nobody's name on the head of it, and this boundary has had two weeks of learning that a name on a head of a column is a holder.**"

Nell Ferrin got up off the step, which took a while, and looked at the butcher's sheet on the anteroom table where Ilsa Norr had left it, at three columns and a line under the third, and she said:

"Then you have written a document with three columns that anybody can use and a fourth one that nobody can fill, and the fourth one is the one I came down here to tell you about, and I have been in this room two hours and I have not been asked to sit in it once.

"**That is the most like a boundary I have ever stood in and I have been doing this for nineteen years and I have never been able to say that about any of them.**"

She went back up the middle passage, and it took her most of the day, and Dalla Sen entered that she had been in the anteroom two hours and had not been asked to sit and had not asked, and that the middle gate's counter is four days up a road and forty days on from the top of it to a file, and that the plan on a butcher's sheet now has a fourth column with no heading and nothing in it, and that a woman had told them why, and that the two columns that can be filled are the two the oldest book in this building has got, and the two that cannot be filled are the two it has not.
