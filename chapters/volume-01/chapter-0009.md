# Chapter 9 — The First Short Route

By late morning, the service shaft behind the maintenance room was not a passage so much as a long argument with the hillside.

It narrowed to a litter’s width, dropped in shallow steps, and opened at intervals into small stone shelves where old pipes had once carried air. Marek had the copied map braced against his left forearm. Iven and Oren carried Leena’s litter. Tams followed with the cracked lamp and the remaining water skins. Pell moved with a crutch made from a strip of the sound short upright, his left ankle bound above the swelling.

The bypass was physically continuous. Every turn showed the next wall, the next bracket, the next stretch of worn stone. Nothing opened beneath them and nothing appeared where they had not walked. When a seam crossed the shaft, the group crossed it in the same order, one person at a time, with the litter held level.

The pressure pulse arrived as a change in the draft. Warm air pushed past them, then reversed. Tams lowered the lamp and listened to the stone.

“It is breathing,” he said.

“It is moving air,” Nera’s voice answered from ahead.

The speaker stood at a barred air gate on the far side of the shaft. She was compact, dressed in a dark work coat, and held a strip of dyed cloth between two fingers. A second person stood beside the gate, older, with a broad wooden wheel set into the wall and a route keeper’s woven band at his throat.

Marek recognized the signal from the boundary stone before he recognized either face.

“State your water and air,” the older man called.

“Six living,” Marek answered. “Five liters from our descent, plus one liter verified by condensation. One woman with a recently reduced right shoulder. One boy with a left ankle injury. The lower chamber is flooding.”

The older man looked at the litter, then at the water skins. “Sefu Aran. I keep the boundary. That does not mean I open it.”

The woman tested the strip of cloth again. “Nera Oduya. Air and water works. I do not speak for the residents.”

The two introductions made the difference plain before either of them had explained it. Sefu’s hand rested on the civic wheel. Nera’s hand rested on the air valve.

Marek reached the gate and held up the copied map. “We need the failed pump. It is the only way to keep the lower chamber from taking the bypass.”

Nera looked at the map, then at the diagram Marek had copied from the old refuge. She did not touch either.

“Your air plan,” she said.

“It is a record of the room we tested.”

“It is a closed box with a beautiful floor.”

Leena’s head turned on the litter. “That is a surprisingly exact description.”

Nera’s gaze stayed on Marek. “You use one shaft for intake and exhaust. When the pressure changes, the room takes its used air back. At six people, with smoke, that is not a room. It is a delayed suffocation.”

Marek felt the old defensiveness rise. He had marked the plan. He had tested the failure. He had not known enough to redesign it before the room began taking people.

“The plan is historical,” he said. “It is not the route we are asking you to approve.”

“That is not what you wrote.”

He looked at his own copied lines. They were uneven, but the mistake was clear. The same shaft served as supply and return. The failure was not a matter of opinion.

Nera turned to Sefu. “The pump air chamber has to stay isolated from this route while we work.”

“And the gate?” Sefu asked.

“Stays closed until the air test and the room test.”

Sefu nodded once. The movement was not agreement to admit them. It was an agreement about the order of work.

Nera pointed through the bars to the lower pump gallery. “The service canister is here. Split gland seal, bronze-fiber facing, drive-dog key. It belongs to the pump crew. It is not a gift, and it is not yours until the work is recorded.”

Iven stepped forward. “I can fit it.”

“You can identify the failure,” Nera said. “That is different from repairing it.”

“I have fitted split glands in worse water.”

“Then you can explain each step to the person who has to live with the next one.”

Iven’s mouth tightened, but he nodded.

Nera unlocked a narrow side hatch rather than the main gate. The group could see the pump through it. The pump gallery opened beneath the anteroom through a grated service well, and a long timber lever reached up through the bars so a person in the anteroom could work the pump without entering the flooded bay. The old reciprocating frame stood on its plinth exactly as they had left it, its broken gland shining with damp. The flood gate held behind it. Water moved below the threshold.

Nera set the service canister on the sill. Inside lay a replacement gland split into a bronze outer face and a dark fiber lining, packed flat, plus a short steel key with a square head.

Iven inspected the pump. “The seat is damaged. We need to clean it before the seal will hold.”

“Then clean it,” Nera said.

Iven and Oren worked at the gland. Marek directed from the left, using the brass rule to check the alignment of the retaining plate. His right hand stayed against the frame. Tams rigged a timber lever from one of the case-panel offcuts, and Pell called the lever’s position from his crutch, keeping his weight off the injured ankle. Nera isolated the air chamber and checked the dyed cloth over the gate.

“State each step as you do it,” Nera told Iven. “The next person may not have your hands.”

Iven cleaned the seat, showed Oren how the fiber lining should fold, and named the retaining plate before he tightened it. “The person after me will know what was done and why,” he said. It was not agreement with Nera’s distrust. It was an answer to it.

“Twenty no-load strokes,” she said. “Count them where everyone can hear.”

Iven fit the gland. The bronze face seated over the piston rod; the fiber lining folded into the groove in a continuous ring. He placed the retaining plate and drove the key into the crank hub. For a moment, the key resisted. Tams shifted the timber lever, and the square teeth seated with a hard click.

Marek felt a pressure shift under the pump plinth. The old line carried weight through the common foundation, but the broken pump now had a new load path. The sensation lasted less than a breath.

“Stop,” he said.

Everyone stopped.

“What is wrong?” Nera asked.

“Relative change. The pump is no longer sitting only on the old plinth.”

Iven looked down. “The gland face is tight enough to pull the frame.”

Marek shook his head. “I don’t know that. I know the frame moved toward the wall when the key seated. Check the feet.”

Oren checked with the pry bar. The rear foot had lifted a fraction from its bed. They packed it with a wedge from the same case-panel offcut. The pump frame stopped moving.

Marek looked at Nera. “You could have seen that.”

“I saw the air side. You saw the load side.”

“Your correction still matters.”

Her expression did not soften. “Good. It should.”

They primed the pump with short, unnumbered strokes while the outlet check stayed shut. Tams and Pell pulled the timber lever together, keeping the motion even. Air and a little water moved through the suction side with a rough gasp. Iven watched the gland. At the fifth priming stroke, a bead of water appeared at the lower edge.

Nera stopped them.

“Fix the seating.”

Iven tightened the gland face by a fraction. The bead stopped. They bled the pressure back down and reset the lever.

“Those were priming strokes,” Nera said. “The witnessed run starts now.”

With the delivery check still closed, Tams and Pell began the counted test. The pump moved with increasing smoothness. At the tenth stroke, sediment made the delivery check chatter even though no water was being drawn. Nera told them to keep the count and watch the noise. The gland remained dry.

They finished the remaining strokes without stopping. When the twentieth stroke ended, Iven released the lever. The pump stopped, then gave one final shudder.

“Twenty no-load strokes,” Tams said.

Nera checked the dyed cloth. Its color had not changed. The air chamber remained isolated from the occupied route. The flood gate was dry.

“Now the draw-down,” she said.

Marek had a marked stone beside the pump recess. The standing water covered the lower step. Tams read the level aloud while Iven and Oren worked the lever. Nera watched the gate and the air cloth. Pell watched the water. Tams counted the minutes against the lamp’s burning time.

The pump drew from the flooded sump. It did not touch any drinking water.

The first minute passed with little change. In the second, the water broke around the pump foot. By the third, the lower step emerged. At five minutes, the marked stone showed a drop of fifteen centimeters.

The gland remained dry. The air cloth stayed the same color. The flood gate did not leak.

Then the delivery check chattered again, a hard irregular knock under the steady pumping. The water stopped falling as quickly, though the pump still moved.

Nera raised a hand. “Stop.”

The crew eased the lever back. The pump’s temporary success was visible in the lowered water and the exposed stone, but the check’s noise had changed the meaning of the result.

“Sediment is lifting against the seat,” she said. “It will not hold unattended. It may fail again before the next shift.”

Marek looked at the dark pump, the dry gland, and the lowered sump. “Then it is a temporary attended tool.”

“Exactly,” Nera said. “Not a permanent route and not a promise that the lower chamber will stay dry.”

“Write that on the work sheet,” Leena said from the litter.

“Someone will have to maintain it,” Iven said.

“Someone here, or someone at the boundary,” Nera replied. “That is a question for the people who live beyond this gate, not a favor to strangers.”

Nera opened a Hearthhold maintenance ledger and wrote the failure, the replacement seal, the twenty strokes, and the five-minute draw-down. She drew a line beside the word temporary, then handed the ledger to Sefu. The screw jack, pry bar, spare wedges, and the remaining spare timber had to stay beside the pump gallery; carrying them up the narrow shaft would have blocked the route and left no one free to work the lever. They marked the cache with a Hearthhold maintenance stroke and left it under Nera’s inventory.

Sefu had not opened the civic gate. He stood with one hand on the wheel and watched the water through the service hatch.

“You have tested the pump,” he said. “That is not a reason to admit six people to the Hearthhold.”

“We are not asking to cross into your homes,” Marek said. “We are asking for a route to keep the lower room from taking us.”

“A route can still become an occupation,” Sefu said. “It can also bring the people who made it back to claim the room that made it possible.”

Nera looked toward the hidden rooms beyond the gate. “The air works are maintained here. The water works are maintained here. People live beyond it. They do not owe you a crossing because your pump is useful.”

Marek accepted the distinction. “Then tell us what test would make the anteroom usable.”

Nera glanced at Sefu. Sefu looked toward the four workers assembling beyond the gate. “A room test,” he said. “Not an empty inspection. We will count everyone who enters. Nera will watch the air. I will watch the door.”

“And if it passes?” Iven asked.

“It passes only for the conditions we write,” Sefu said.

Marek looked through the bars at the service shaft, the pump, and the water they had drawn down. The short route was real. The pump was installed, witnessed, and tested. Neither fact was permission.

Nera turned back to the gate. “Bring the anteroom’s residents in,” she said. “Twelve occupants—eleven adults and Pell—one stretcher, and one hundred and twenty kilograms of gear. We test for thirty minutes with the pump running. If anything reverses, the upper service landing receives everyone.”

Sefu’s hand tightened on the wheel. “And the outer door remains open.”

“For the escape route,” Marek said.

“For the route that lets us close the room if it closes on us,” Sefu replied.

Sefu opened the outer service door a shoulder’s width. Four Hearthhold workers came through with a lantern, a repair ledger, two baskets of tools, and a folded canvas sheet. They did not step into the deeper rooms. Sefu counted the threshold and kept one hand on the civic wheel.

“Litter first,” he said. “One person at a time.”

Iven and Oren carried Leena across while the others followed. The group entered the anteroom, not the Hearthhold Middle. Nera wrote the pump test on the maintenance ledger and wrote beneath it, *air mechanism only; admission pending*. She handed the ledger to Sefu.

“Do not turn my air approval into their permission,” she said.

Marek watched the hand on the civic wheel. The route was short now, and its next condition had a name.
