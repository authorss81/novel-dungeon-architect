# Chapter 221 — The Windlass On The Table

The fourth day of the forty-second week is a Thursday, the corridor is shut, and the school did not run and lost nothing, and something that weighs more than a person came up nine wet steps at about the second bell and was laid on the trestle in the middle of the anteroom in front of a room that had been talking about it for five days without once having it in the room.

It was a windlass. A drum of dressed stone on a spindle of the same stone, about the length of two men's arms, with a bar through one end and sockets worn on one side only, and beside it a channel that had been cut for a door and had taken the weight of that door for nine hundred years without being cut for anything else.

Jo Verrick had the frame at the first bell, a finger and two-thirds, and the party wall was a quarter of an inch, the forty-first reading, and the same as forty, and the string was where the string was. She put her thumb under it and the thumb came out of it right, and she did not give the four refusals, and she gave the reason instead, in one sentence, in the same words as Sunday and Tuesday and Wednesday, and the clerk entered it and entered that it was the same sentence for the fifth time and that the woman had said in advance that it would be.

Renn Vale put her ear to the wall at the first bell, four times over, and reported what she could hear, which was nothing, four times, and the run of mornings out of mornings is twenty-six.

The terms were said at the first bell, all six, and the third of them was said without anybody being asked whether it is right. Ten including one litter for no more than two hours, and it did not change, and there is a stone drum on a table in this room now and the anteroom has two ways out and one of them weighs more than a man. The outer service door is the one a body went out of. The fifth of the six is in a room with both of the people it names in it, and he named it and read nothing. The sixth was the pump, and it ran, and the clerk entered that she entered it and wrote not a word beside it.

The pump was started, two people turning it, and then two of the crew of nine who are not this building's got the windlass up the last of the stairs and set it down, and neither of them said anything, and nobody thanked them and nobody asked them.

Then the air engineer stood at the end of the trestle with a piece of chalk in her hand and no dyed cloth anywhere about her, and nobody had called her and she was not asked, and Dalla Sen entered that she was not asked.

"**I am going to say it a second time and I am going to say it in the same order and I am going to say the cost first, and I am going to say it with the thing on the table this time instead of in my head, because on Friday the cost was in my mouth and the mechanism was in my mouth and neither of them was in anybody's hands.**"

Nobody in the anteroom said anything.

"**Four closed. The low vent off the corner of this anteroom. The broken louvre over the kitchen line, which is a hole and not a vent. The seam vent off the far end, because there is a head of water on that side of the party wall four days in seven and the standing phrase in this room says three and the two of those are in a book and I am not going to argue about either of them this morning. And the riser at the top of the nine steps, which I want shut for about an hour and no longer.**"

"**And two opened,**" Renn Vale said, from the middle of the floor.

"**And two opened. And with those four shut, everything that chamber pushes out has one way to go, and the one way it goes is up the shaft that serves this anteroom and the room at the kitchen-line end, and the air those two rooms breathe gets pushed at them by a body of water nine hundred years old instead of being drawn through a hole in a corner. That is the plan. It has not changed since Friday. I have not improved it, I have not had time to improve it, and I have had five days to think of a better one and I have not had one, and I am going to say that in a room because a woman who has not had a better one and does not say so is a woman selling something.**"

"**Then say the cost,**" Dalla Sen said.

"**The cost is the door, and you have all got that, and I am going to say it once more and then I am never going to say it again in this building unless somebody asks me, and if I do say it again somebody is to stop me.**"

She put two fingers on the stone drum.

"**A windlass and two people and about four minutes, and the far side of that door is cut off while it is shut, and everything on the far side of it is cut off, and that includes a pumping station and a drain and anybody standing in either of them. If there is anybody on the far side of that door when it shuts then the cost of my plan is that person, and not a room, and not the air, and not the water. I told this room that on Friday in the first person before anybody asked me, and this morning I have put the instrument on the table and I am not going to soften it by having it in front of me.**"

Then the man at the end of the trestle put his hand on the drum, and nobody had asked him, and the clerk entered that nobody had.

"**I have not turned it,**" Marek Venn said, "**and I am not going to, and I have looked at it for about four minutes and I am going to say one thing about it and then I am going to stop talking, and I would like it entered that I said one thing and stopped rather than that I had nothing.**"

"**Say the thing,**" Bel Ordry said, from the top of the nine steps.

"**The pawl sits on the wrong side of the drum for the way you are going to turn it,**" Marek Venn said. "**Which is not a fault, it is a direction. Somebody cut it that way and somebody worked it that way for nine hundred years and the sockets are worn on one side only because that is the side they leaned on. When it comes across, it will not want to be wound. It will want to be pushed, and it will take the two of you about four minutes, and at the end of the third minute the drum will be past the point where the pawl has anything to say and the fourth minute will be the two of you holding a thing that is heavier than it was at the start.**"

"**Say the half against that,**" Bel Ordry said.

"**The half against is that I have never seen this instrument work and that I have told you about a machine I have not seen, and that a man who can tell you a thing is wrong and cannot tell you the right thing is a nuisance,**" Marek Venn said. "**I do not know how heavy it is. I know a pawl is on that side because the sockets are on that side. That is all of it and it changes nothing and I would rather have said it than sat here having worked it out and kept it.**"

"**Entered, in his words, and entered that he was not asked, and entered that it changes nothing,**", and she did not look up.

Nobody improved on it. There is a standing arrangement in this room that a true thing said for nothing is a true thing and not a gift, because the day this building starts paying for information is the day a man with a page in a coat has to decide what to charge, and it has not happened in forty-one weeks and it was not going to happen on a Thursday because a man could not help himself.

And then a man who had come four days up a middle passage with a piece of paper in his coat put both hands flat on the trestle and said the thing that had been in him since the previous afternoon, and the room went quiet in a way it had not gone quiet all morning, because it was not a sentence. It was arithmetic.

"**Say it out loud,**", from the top of the nine steps.

"**There is a pumping station on that line,**" said Seth Wray, "**and it has not been turned over in nine years, and there is a chamber beside it with four fathoms of water in it, and that chamber is filling, and the reason I know it is filling is that I have been in it up to my chest and I have watched the salt on the far wall go from about the height of my hand to about the height of my elbow in nine days, and I have not put a figure on it because I am not a man who puts figures on salt.**"

He turned round and looked at the stone drum on the table.

"**That turn-over has to happen while that door is open.**"

Nobody in that anteroom said anything for about as long as the pump takes to make four strokes.

"**Say the rest of it,**" Dalla Sen said.

"**After it is shut, nobody gets to that pump. Not me, not you, not a man in a coat with a shut book under his arm. That door has been shut since before anybody alive was born and whatever is on the far side of it has been going on without anybody for nine hundred years, and the moment that stone goes across, the far side of it stops being a place anybody can go.**" He put one hand on the drum and left it there. "**And nobody in this room can tell me how long a turn-over takes. Not her, and she will not guess, and she is right not to. And nobody in this room can tell me how long she needs that door shut. Those are two numbers. I have been carrying one of them since Friday and the other one arrived in this room on Friday afternoon and the two of them have never been in the same breath in this building, and I would like them put next to each other, and I would like somebody who is frightened of arithmetic to be the one who does it.**"

"**Then say the half against that,**", and he did not look up from the stair.

"**The half against is that this is not arithmetic, it is a pump,**", and he did not put the slate down. "**A turn-over is a crew of men with bars on a spindle in a room you cannot see, and the length of it is the length of the work and not a length anybody can read off a wall. I have turned over nine of them in twenty-three years and I could not tell you the shortest of them in weeks. That is not me being slow. That is a thing nobody knows, and it has not been known for as long as there have been pumps, and every man who has ever gone down to one has found out at the same time as everybody else.**"

He took his hand off the drum.

"**So there is one thing left that I can do and I am going to say that I am going to do it, and I would like it in the room before I do it, and I am not asking anybody for anything. My nine go down to that station at the fifth bell this afternoon. Not because anybody in this room has asked them to. Because the turn-over has to start while the door is open and tomorrow is the last morning the door is going to be open, and if it is not started tomorrow then it is not started, and if it is not started then this chamber fills, and a chamber that fills is a room that fills, and a room that fills is not a thing a ventilation plan is a plan about.**"

"**You are not asking us for anything,**" said Renn Vale, "**and you have just told a room that your nine are going down a wet stair on the strength of a length of time none of us can name.**"

"**I am telling you and I am doing it,**", at the trestle. "**Those are two different verbs and I have had them separate since Friday. I told this room in the fourth week of this volume that whatever this boundary owes us it is not a rescue, and a rescue is a thing you do to people, and if anybody in here says the word I will go back down tonight and I meant that as a fact and not a threat, and I am not going to soften it this morning because I have found out something inconvenient about a pump.**"

Dalla Sen entered all of it, and entered that he was not asked, and entered that the room had been told a thing before it was done rather than after, and then she stopped writing and sat with the chalk off the page for a while, which she does perhaps three times in five volumes.

"**I am going to enter one thing and I am not going to put a reason beside it,**" she said, "**and I am going to say out loud that I am doing that, because I have done it twice this month and both of those times I knew the reason and once I did not. This is the third time. There are two numbers about that door. One of them is a length of time nobody can name and the other one is a length of work nobody can name, and they are not the same length, and one of them has to be longer than the other for this to work, and nobody in this room knows which.**"

"**Say the half against that,**" Bel Ordry said.

"**The half against is that a clerk is not a place to put a thing because nobody else will hold it,**" Dalla Sen said, "**and I have been a place to put a thing for forty-one weeks and I have got good at it, and I am telling you now that this one is going to be picked up by whoever finds this page first and used, and the man who uses it will be right about the arithmetic and wrong about the people, and that is the ordinary way a true number becomes a bad plan.**"

"**Entered that, in my own words, and entered that I know what I have done,**" she said.

At about the fifth bell nine people went down the nine steps and along the passage and down a set of wet stairs, and one of them had a lamp and one of them had a length of black cord and none of them had a bell, and this boundary had said out loud in a clerk's book on the sixth day of the forty-first week that it was not going to give them a bell and was not going to give them a line, and it gave them nothing this morning either, and that was entered as a third decision and not as part of the other two.

Marek Venn sat at the end of the trestle with the shut book under his forearm and did the thing he has done about four mornings in ten, and this time it went wrong in a new direction.

What he had noticed was that there was nothing in him to refuse.

Nine weeks. He had given five reasons in a fortnight about not saying things and not being asked for things and not sending people, and he had built a groove in himself the exact shape of one sentence, and a cook had found the groove in ninety seconds with a cloth over her arm. He had spent the previous week renouncing the sentence and finding that his hand went straight back onto the book the moment the room wanted a figure. And this morning the room had got a good plan and a bad arithmetic and a stone drum with a pawl on the wrong side of it, and every single thing that had happened had been something he could have stopped, and he had stopped none of it, and there had been nothing in him to stop it with.

So he had done the only thing left, which was to say one true thing that cost him nothing and that nobody had asked him for, about a machine he had never seen working, and then stop talking.

It was the first time since he came into this building that he had put something into a room and got nothing for it, and the nothing was not a refusal and it was not a hearing. It was a foreman naming a pump and a room going quiet and a woman at a counter writing down a number nobody had said. He had spent a fortnight learning that a room that has been handed an offer instead of a cost hears the offer, and this morning he had handed the room a fact and the room had heard it the way a room hears a fact, which is to say for about four seconds and then it went back to the thing that frightened it more.

He put his hand on the book without any conclusion in it, and left it there, and found out later what it had been for.

At the sixth bell the day's figure and the total went into the book and neither was read, and the reason went in beside them: a room which has spent the morning being told that two numbers have to be compared and that nobody can compare them is not going to be read a total over by the woman who just wrote the word *unknown* in a findings column for the second time in a fortnight.

Six today and three hundred and seventy-eight since the keeper signed. The boundary is at four hundred and ninety-five and a quarter, it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen.

The count went in underneath that on its own line, in the ordinary hand, and was entered and not read, and the reason was given, and it was that a clerk who has spent the morning writing down the order in which people go down a wet stair is not going to stand up and say a number she has been saying for fifty-five weeks, and that is a reason about the clerk and not about the count. Wen Pask is one hundred and sixty-six days and one hundred and sixty-six nights, fifty-five weeks of a clerk saying out loud that she is going to keep writing it down. It is not going to be one hundred and sixty-seven.

The board was not walked, and the reason was entered, and the reason was that a board is a column and a clerk does not walk a column on a morning when she has written the word *unknown* into one for the second time in a fortnight and has been told by a man with twenty-three years of his own that a pump is not a number.

The crew are at eighty and did not move, and the reason for the day is in the book, and the reason is that a foreman has four and a half hours outstanding and a chamber filling four days away and has not asked, and the clerk entered that she had noticed the shape of that sentence before he finished it and entered that she did not write the noticing down.

The pot is three days, and the seam gave nothing this morning for the ninth morning running, and it is at four threads, and nobody measured it, and the fire in the room at the kitchen-line end has now not been lit for twenty-seven weeks, and the four households at a second gate have nothing and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody, and nine people have gone down a wet stair to a pump that nobody can say how long a turn-over takes, on the word of a man who was told there was water.

And at the seventh bell the anteroom was nine steps and a trestle and a stone drum lying on the trestle with its sockets worn on one side only, and the two of the nine who carried it up standing by the kitchen-line doorway with their sleeves wet to the elbow because the wet stair had been doing that to people for five days, and a man at the end of a trestle who had said one true thing for nothing and had not been thanked for it and had not expected to be and was not going to be, and a room with two numbers in it and no way to compare them and a windlass that had not been turned.
