# Chapter 36 — A Counter-Support for the Receiver

Marek put the word *counter-support* on the slate and four people in the room told him it was the wrong word, and he was glad about three of them.

The session was a session and not a review, which Dalla insisted on and made him write the difference of: a review reads what exists, a session writes what is proposed, and only a review can stop anybody.

"Nobody marks it," she said. "Nobody puts a mark on it. It is not a decision and it is not a route and it is not a thing anybody has agreed to. It is a page that says a man thinks something might be possible, and if a person in this room does not think it might be possible, the right thing is for them to say so while he is in the room and not afterwards."

"Give me the object first," Iven said. "Not the word. The object."

Marek's object had been a support under the lower receiving landing, a post that took the return the stair asked for. He had come in with it drawn, in his left hand, badly, and Jo had taken the slate and turned it round without saying anything, which was her way of making him look at it from the room's side.

Iven looked at it for about four seconds.

"That is a receiving support," he said. "And there is nothing on that landing to receive. I told you what the hollow is. It is a line-block, worn a hundred years deep by a rope, and the bright band on its front face is a stone somebody drove in to stop the line. There is no landing down there to land on. There has not been a landing down there since before I could read."

"Then the stair is asking for a line," Marek said. "And we cannot put a line in a hollow that is four inches deep in the bottom of a block that is under the far side of a throat we may not reach. So we answer it from this side. We take a standing load on this side at the same moment the slab goes, big enough that the difference the stair is asking for arrives from a floor that is already here."

"That is a dead-load bench," Jo said, "which is on your slate since Sunday and which we do not have stone for, and which is on a floor that is a lid over eleven feet of shaft, which I am not putting a hundredweight on, and which you are not either, and I would like it written that I said both of those things before anybody did."

"Write it," Marek said.

Jo wrote it, in her own hand, under the line, and read it back.

Then Leena did the thing she had been doing since the nineteenth day, which was to take the plan apart by asking who is where.

"Where does the load go, physically?" she said. "Not where it should go. Where does it stand?"

"Here. On the near landing, on the fixed stone, against the wall that the block is set into."

"Then it is pushing on the same block," Leena said. "The block that has a bright four-inch band on it from a thing somebody wedged into it forty years ago. Marek, you cannot put your bench on top of the question. The bench and the answer are the same piece of stone."

"Backed off. Set on the throat's near lip, outboard of the block."

"The near lip is the edge of the hole over eleven feet of shaft," Nera said.

Marek stopped.

"Yes," Nera said. "It is. And you have now drawn a plan that puts a standing load on the lip of the mouth the corridor's air comes up out of, in a room that I have told this boundary cannot be called a room until somebody can see down that shaft, and which you and I agreed in the thirtieth day was a floor we use while a machine lasts." She was not angry. She was doing what she always did, which was take a word apart in front of people. "Say the sentence that means it, because I want it said and not sketched."

Marek said it. "Then the bench stands on the lip of a hole I cannot see into, over a shaft the stair is breathing through, and if it moves, it moves the lip."

"Yes."

"That is a condition we would have to accept or design around, and I cannot design around it, so it is written down as a condition."

"It is written down as a condition," Nera agreed, "and I will put my mark beside it, and my mark will say that an air engineer's answer to a load on a shaft lip is to move the air, and that moving the air on that lip during a turn is the one thing I have stood in this room and said we would not do."

It went like that for three hours, which was two hours more than it should have taken, because Marek would not let a condition go by without somebody writing it in his own words, and because three of the people at the table had conditions of their own that had nothing to do with timber.

Renn's came first, because her rooms were nearest.

"You are working beside my door," she said. "Not in it. Beside it, with a lamp and a stand and a man on the step, eleven feet from where four people sleep, and one of them is not walking and the other three are, and I am not going to pretend I care less about the stair than I care about the stair. So here are my terms, and they are not requests, and I am putting them on the page myself."

She wrote them herself, in her own hand, and read them out.

No work at the stair between the second bell and the fourth. No work at the stair at all on the days the corridor is open, because a household in the corridor deserves not to have a lamp swinging over its head. Nothing left standing on the near landing between periods, and nothing to be climbed over. If any of the six of you is on that step, then a person whose name is written on the day's board is outside in the anteroom and I know who they are. And the fifth: the restriction on the stair is mine to re-tie, not yours, and if your plan ever needs the cord moved, the cord moves by Sefu's hand and mine and not by a man with a lamp.

"Sefu," she said. "Do you want that in your hand or mine?"

"Both," Sefu said, and signed beside it, and meant it.

Mikka's were about the kitchen, because the anteroom's ten includes the kitchen line on an open day and the crew standing in it discussing a project with a chalk is a thing that can happen too.

"Whatever it is," she said, "there is no pot off the fire while you are on that step, and there is no queue through the space where you will be standing, and if I need the anteroom's ten for a pot I will take the ten, and you will be on the step in the cold, and that is not a complaint, it is a priority."

Ilsa's were about the school corner, and they were the last and the shortest.

"Your school corner floor is a lid over a void that a wash room is built over," she said. "The crew put a kerb in it nine days ago. If the work you are planning is a hole through this building, and if any part of it comes up under my floor, then my floor is a work site and my school does not run and nine children are somewhere else, and I will not be asked about it afterwards. Ask me now. I am saying now: not under my floor."

"Noted," Dalla said, and wrote it in Ilsa's words, and it was on the page before anybody could soften it.

Oren's contribution was not a term. It was a demonstration, and he did it on the anteroom floor with Nera's draw cord and a stone, and it took him four minutes, and it explained more than the whole morning.

He tied the line to the plinth of the pump housing, which was the most fixed thing in the room, and put the stone out on the floor, and then pulled the line, and let it go, and the stone came about an inch.

"Now," he said. "That is a floor, and it is doing it, and it is not the floor we are talking about. The floor we are talking about is not on its own footing down there. It is on a lip over a hole, and the whole length of that lip is one stone with the block set into it, and if I pull at the near end of a lip that is one stone, I am pulling at the far end of the lip as well, and everything in between goes."

"Then we cannot stand a load on the lip," Marek said.

"You cannot stand a load on the lip," Oren agreed, "and I would put my shoulder under that lip, so you know I am not saying it because I do not like the plan. I am saying it because I have spent thirty years leaning on other men's lips and I know what it takes to bring one down. I could not hold it and I would not be asked to."

Leena wrote that down too, in her own hand, because it was a bearing condition and bearing conditions were hers by thirty days of standing where she could not stand.

And then the tally, which Tams had been waiting for since the first bell, and which he did out loud with the board turned round, in four columns, because that is what the board is for.

"Labor," he said. "The six of us. Two of us cannot be on a floor at all — a shoulder and an ankle — and Tams is on a hand and is a counter and a reader and not a body. So: Marek on the survey, Oren on the bearing, Iven on the only carpentry in this building. Three, and Jo is not one of them, because Jo is the frame and the wedges and the record and Jo has a shutter route."

"That is the labor line," he said, "and I am not finished, because Leena would have objected to being in it, and she is right to. Leena is the four minutes and the survey marks and she is not hours. Put her in the record and not in the column."

"Material." He turned the board further round, as though that would help. "A post. There is none. A line — the hand line is on a school-door head, the bell rope is Dalla's, the black cord is forty feet on a hook in the boundary stores and Sefu does not release it, and the only line that came free in this whole business was Nera's eight-foot grille draw cord, which is on her valve board and is going back on its hook tonight. A dead weight. The only mass in this building that is not somebody's wall is eleven feet of parapet slab with *removed for safety* on it in a ledger, and Dalla has already said the words *you may not build on it* out loud in this room."

"And a lip," Nera said, taking the board. "Read the fourth one right, Tams, because you are about to put it in the wrong column and I want it in the right one. The far wall of that throat is eleven feet below the near lip, and the light goes in and comes off the water at the bottom, and at the angle you would need in order to see a fixing you would be looking at water. That is not a material. That is a sight line, and the sight line is the whole of the problem, and it goes in the column where I put it."

"Time," Tams said. "I cannot do that one, and I am not going to pretend to. The turn is not on a schedule, so a work period is a start and a stop and no length, and I can tell you what a day costs and not what a day is."

"Then the fourth column is not air," Nera said. "You have been writing *air* at the top of it because that is the word for the thing I have been saying all morning, and I have been saying a word that means a *time*. A gap is a length of nothing. What I do is air. Write what the air costs and write it in a sentence I would say."

"It costs a period that begins without warning and ends at four minutes," Nera said, when Jo had a slate. "That is all. There is no interval. There is a start and there is an end and the end is sooner after the pump than not, and I will not put a number on it, and if anybody wants a number then somebody has to find a way to see down that shaft, and nobody in this building can see down that shaft."

Jo wrote it, and read it back, and nobody added to it, and the four columns were on the board and three of them said *none*.

Pell read the four columns back to the room from the bench while it was still warm, and read them the way he read a tally, in order and without comment, and the only thing he added was that a column that says *none* in three places out of four is not a plan yet, it is a list of things somebody has to go and find.

"That is fair," Tams said. "I will write it on the board as the heading, because I would rather it was written by a fourteen-year-old than discovered by somebody in a year."

Then Iven put the four ways round it on the table, one at a time, and killed them one at a time, and it took him twenty minutes and he enjoyed it and nobody minded.

"A cantilever from this side, reaching under the lip and bearing on the far side. That wants timber. There is no timber. And it wants a man with a wrist like mine holding a bar under a lip for an hour, and the man holding that bar is on the step and not on the near landing, which means the whole thing is one man and one bar."

"A line made fast to an eye in the far wall and hauled from this side. We do not know that there is an eye. Nobody has ever seen the far wall of that throat. The far wall is eleven feet down and under water some days and we cannot see it from anything."

"A dead load heavy enough to meet the difference from this side. There is no permitted mass, there is no floor to stand it on, and the floor we have would be the lip we have already been told we may not load."

"And the fourth," he said, "which is the one everybody wants, which is that you put a walking person across it and time it, because a walking person is the only thing that repeats the return, and a walking person is the exact thing we have corded off."

Nobody argued about the fourth. It had been said in a corner of this room before and had been written down and corded, and it was not a proposal; it was a piece of history.

And then Leena said the sentence that closed the morning, and she said it from the litter with the slate on her knees and the four minutes written on somebody else's page in two other hands.

"You have designed a support," she said, "that has to be stood by a person, in the one place no person may stand, and the reason you cannot put it anywhere else is that the thing it has to hold is under the floor, and the floor is the far side of the hole." She put the chalk down. "Marek, in six weeks I have watched you get this far three times. You get to the edge of a thing and then somebody hands you a piece of timber and a pair of hands and it goes. This time there is no piece of timber, and there are no hands, and the thing is on the wrong side of a line. That is not a hard morning. That is the actual shape of it."

* * *

Nera stayed at the valve after the room emptied, which she did most evenings, and which had stopped being a habit anybody commented on.

She had a slate of her own. She did not keep it on Jo's board and she did not give it to Dalla, and there were two reasons for that and one of them was private and one of them was a rule she had made in the second week and never explained to anybody.

The rule was that she would not put a pattern on a page she did not hold, until she could stand behind it. She had learned that the hard way, from another engineer's recorded air plan, on a day in the ninth week of a life that was not hers, and she had spent a long time afterwards working out that the fault had not been dishonesty. It had been that the plan was written by a competent man who had not been in the room with the air.

The pattern was eleven days old and it was on her slate in her own hand and it was this.

She had counted, every time she had been at that valve since the twenty-ninth day, the interval between the end of a pump interval and the next turn. Eleven days. Forty-one turns counted, because eleven of them she had counted from the other end and had to strike out, and striking out is the discipline.

Thirty-four of the forty-one came between one and nine minutes after the lever came off. Four came between ten and nineteen. Three came more than half an hour after, and two of those had come while the pump was still running, which had frightened her, and one had come while the corridor was shut and empty.

It was not proof. She knew exactly what it was: forty-one numbers, one pattern, no mechanism she could name, and a building that had lied to her before in a way she could not have predicted.

But she was not going to have anybody killed on the other side of an unproven pattern either, so she did the thing she had decided she would do, which was the only honest thing available to her, which was to stop pretending she had a window.

It was not a gap of nine minutes. She had told the crew it was a gap and she had meant it, and a gap is a thing that happens to you. What it was, in fact, was a period whose end was set by something on the other side of a wall she cannot see into, and the only thing she could tell anybody was that the end of it came sooner, and more often, after the pump had been running than not.

So the plan on Jo's board, which was a plan for building a thing, had to become a plan about stopping.

She wrote it on her own slate, in the plainest words she had, and read it three times, and did not put it on the board, and the not-putting-it was the hardest thing she did that week.

*Marek Venn: the plan cannot be a plan for making. It has to be a plan for stopping, and every step of it has to end with the near side as it was found, because the turn is not a gap, it is an end that arrives early, and a man on a step with half a thing made is a man on a step inside four minutes with half a thing made and no way to have help.*

Then she closed the valve to its attended stop, which is not the same as shutting it, and left the cloth on its hook, and went up two flights to the wheel to find Sefu, because a rule that arrives at a boundary second is a rule the boundary has already had to argue about once.
