# Chapter 192 — The Morning With No Day On It

The seventh day of the thirty-sixth week is a Sunday, and a seventh day is not a crossing day, which means that nobody can come up the nine steps this morning and nobody can go down them, and that is not a rule anybody in this building has ever had to enforce because it has never once been tested. There are eleven people in this Hearthhold and all eleven of them are standing in an anteroom with a hole in the corner of the floor, and the passage at the top of the nine steps is shut, and will be shut until the first bell tomorrow, and tomorrow the corridor is shut again anyway.

So there is nowhere for anybody to go, and nobody came, and the room has an ordinary working morning in front of it and no way at all of turning any part of that morning into an event. The school did not run. It does not run on a seventh day and has not for a long time and nobody in this room has ever proposed putting it back.

Renn Vale came down the four steps at the first bell with her slate under her arm and stopped in the middle of the floor.

"**I said on Saturday that I would say what I can hear,**" she said, "**and I said it on an open day at the first bell and on a shut day at the second, and today is neither, and I have been awake since before the bells thinking about that, and here is what I have decided and I am going to say it before anybody can improve it.**"

"**All of it, and in that order,**" said Dalla Sen, and she had her slate face down on the trestle, which she has not done on a working morning in a month.

"**There is no first bell on a seventh day for the watching, because a seventh day is not a crossing day and there is nothing crossing to be heard from the other side. So the watching is not done at a bell today. And I am not going to give it an hour instead, because an hour is a smaller version of a bell and this building has spent thirty-five weeks finding out that a small version of a rule is still a rule.**"

She put the slate down on the trestle, on nothing anybody had written on.

"**So here is the morning. Every person in this room who keeps a thing in this building looks at that thing, in this room, with everybody else able to hear, and says out loud what they found. And nobody writes it down.**"

"**That is my line,**" said Renn Vale, five seconds later, without moving off the middle of the floor. "**That is mine, you have just said it in a room, and I said on Saturday that I would not let a new thing be folded into an old sentence without somebody saying so first.**"

"**Then I am saying so first,**" said the other of the two of them, from the kitchen-line doorway, "**and I will not pretend I said it better than you. I said a thing on Saturday and I am going to do it, and I am going to do it in a room with no slate in it, and there is a difference between those two things and it is the only difference I have ever been able to put my finger on.**"

"**Then it is agreed,**" Renn Vale said, "**and I want it entered that it took two women four strokes of a pump and that neither of us gave way.**"

Dalla Sen did not enter it. She turned her slate over, picked up her chalk, and then put the chalk down on the anvil end where it was not within reach of her hand, and said why.

"**It is not coming near me this morning,**" she said. "**A clerk with a chalk in her hand writes. That is not a moral thing, it is a thing about my hands, and I have known it for nineteen years and I have never once been in a room where putting it down was the correct thing to do.**"

Then the anteroom's terms were said, by a person and repeated by the clerk, on a morning when nothing was going to be written, and the count was ten including one litter for no more than two hours and did not change, and the outer service door was the one a body went out of, and the fifth of those six terms was in a room with both the people it names in it, and the pump was attended. Dalla Sen entered that she had said the six out loud on a morning with no instrument in it, and that she had done it because the six are the only terms in this building that are not about a room, and a set of terms that is not about a room has to be said out loud or it is a rule, and this is not a building with rules in it.

Then they went round, and they went quickly, and it was not a ceremony and nobody made it one.

Renn Vale went first, because it was her morning and she had said so.

"**The corridor. Open, shut at the first bell, and the common landing is clear of packs for the first time in a week, and there is nothing on the landing that has not been on it before Friday. Five and a half shores, a spreader under the head of the sill, and the sixth place empty and not filled, and I am going to say the last part out loud because it is mine to say. The sixth is not coming back this week and it is not coming back on a cart, and the iron for it is four days up a passage, and the wall is not the wall it was on Monday and I will not pretend otherwise.**"

Ilsa Norr went next, from the doorway, with her slate in both hands.

"**Nine hearth stones in order, and the ninth is where it has been, and the bed under it is a third of an inch higher than it was in the twenty-fourth week and I have not found out why and I have stopped looking on shut days.**" She put the slate down. "**The room is not to be used and it is not shut, and both of those are still true this morning, and the fire has now not been lit for twelve weeks and nobody has asked me about it since the ninth week and I would like that noted by whoever is keeping the notes, which is her.**"

"**Entered,**" said Dalla Sen, from a long way from her chalk.

Jo Verrick went with the string and did not put it on the wall.

"**The frame is a finger and two-thirds. The wall is a quarter of an inch and it is the fifteenth reading and it is the same as fourteen. The join is a finger on this side and two fingers on the other side of the same join, and I have not put a rule in it and I never shall, and I did not need to on Friday because a man read it for me from the other side and said he could not tell whether the sill would hold and that I was the only person who could tell him, and I told him.**"

"**That is the arrangement working,**" said Callow, from the bench.

"**That is the arrangement working,**" said Jo Verrick, "**and it is the first time in five weeks that a figure I have read has been read by somebody who did not ask me for it.**"

Mikka Hale came to the doorway with the cloth over her arm and did not come through it.

"**The pot is three days. The seam is at four threads and it gave nothing yesterday and one on Friday and I will not put a number on how fast that is going. Six pails a day, every day in seven, in two crossings, four and then two.**" She turned the cloth over. "**And six people who are not this building's ate here yesterday and the day before, and I decided that on the Monday on my own, and I have not been asked about it once, and this morning I would like it entered that a cook has been left alone with a decision for six days and has not been asked about it and has not minded, and that those two things are both true and I do not know which one is doing the work.**"

Sefu Aran came down two steps and stopped, which is where he stops.

"**Four rooms held as one space, and the last one out of a room decides when that room is empty, and that is not on my page and it is not on anybody's page, and it has been true for longer than anybody in this room has been standing in it. I have been back nine days. I have not found out who decided it and I am not going to find out this morning, and I would like it entered that an elected keeper stood in a room for a quarter of an hour this morning and said four sentences and decided nothing.**"

Nera Oduya came down all three steps, which she does for air and for nothing else, and stood in the doorway of the room at the kitchen-line end with the dyed cloth out, and it was not out for long.

"**Four numbers,**" she said. "**And not a fifth, and the fifth one stays in my mouth, and I have said four and not a fifth in this room eleven times now, and I intend to go on saying it until somebody asks me for the fifth, and nobody has.**"

Nobody asked her for the fifth.

"**And I certify nothing and I refuse nothing,**" she said, "**and that is not a policy, it is a description of a morning, and a description of a morning is the only kind of sentence I have left.**"

Iven Tallo came off the anvil end and did not put the chisel down, which is what he does when he is going to say a thing he means, and he said it with the chisel still in his hand.

"**The brace is over the outer service door and the shim in it is a beech shim a finger wide in a hollow worn nine hundred years ago and its edges crush in about two years.**" He looked at the hand that is open and flat and does not close. "**A condition on it stands while two people agree the wall can take it. One of them spent it on Thursday in front of a room. The other one was not asked, and I am the other one, and I have not been asked, and I am saying nothing this morning, and I would like a clerk to enter that the man who built it declined to say whether the wall can take it while standing under it.**"

Dalla Sen entered that in his words, which took longer than most of the morning's entries.

Bel Ordry came down the step, all the way, and stood at the end of the trestle with his hands behind his back the way a man stands when he is not going to touch anything.

"**The page is in the coat,**" he said, "**and it is staying in the coat. It has been on a table in this room for six days with another document on it and a chalk line under both of them and nothing written under the line, and that is the closest this building has come to saying the thing it has not said, and the chalk line is still on the table and nobody has swept it up.**"

"**And the two other things in this room that nobody has looked at today,**" said Bel Ordry, "**because they are not mine and I do not keep them. There is a brass survey rule in the pocket of that coat on the hook by the door, and it has said nothing for twenty-seven weeks. And there is a plate wrapped in work cloth on the shelf above this bench, and it has been unopened for thirty-six.**"

"**Then they are looked at,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and found in the same state, and neither of them came into a room today.**"

"**Then the comparison is still owed,**" said Renn Vale.

"**The comparison is still owed,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and the room that can hear it still does not exist, and I said that in this room six weeks ago and I have not found out anything since that would let me say it does.**"

Marek Venn was last, because nobody had asked him to go and he went anyway, and he had been standing at the end of the trestle with his book shut under his forearm for the whole of it, and what he had in him was the eleven minutes from the second day of last week, when he had shut a book in under four seconds and noticed that it did not take him four seconds, and that was new.

"**The frame is a finger and two-thirds and it is a word said to a page,**" he said, "**and it has moved a twelfth of an inch in thirty-three weeks and it moved because a piece of beech came out of a school door and a shim crushed, and that is the same quantity and it is a return and not a decay, and I have known that for two weeks and I have not said it out loud because nobody has asked me and because I did not want to be the man who says it.**"

"**Then say it,**" said Renn Vale.

"**I have said it.**"

"**Then that is six of us and a clerk and a cook and a man who reads numbers and a foreman, and the seventh is a woman at a counter, and I am going to ask her the thing I have not asked her,**" said Renn Vale, "**because it is her trade and it is the only thing in this room that is.**"

Nella Bray had been standing in the middle of the floor for about a quarter of an hour without anybody having asked her to stand anywhere, and she had been looking at the hole in the corner of the floor.

"**I have stood at a counter for nineteen years,**" she said, "**and half of what I do there is not written down. It is not gone. It is not written down. And I am not going to tell you which half, and I would like that entered as a thing I declined to say, and not as a thing I did not have.**"

"**Then the arrangement,**" said Renn Vale.

"**A counter-schedule has a day of our choosing in it,**" said Nella Bray. "**That is the whole of what makes it a schedule. A day of our choosing, and then a column, and then somebody standing in it. This has no day in it. That is the difference and I am not going to pretend to you that it is a big one, because it is not, and I have got a counter's knowledge and it is telling me that a thing with no day on it is a thing that never happened, and I have no answer to that, and I am not going to invent one in front of nine of you.**"

Then the six terms were said, and they were said by the foreman, because the clerk had put her chalk on the anvil end and had not picked it up again.

Emrys Callow read them out of his own mouth, all six, without the slate, and Dalla Sen did not hold the slate for him and did not look at it.

"**Five work the common landing side. The kitchen-line side is not worked and not entered. Every person whose hands go on that wall says out loud in a room what they are holding and what stops it. Nobody is sent to the low end and the page there stays as it is. Nobody goes through the wall and nothing goes into the line of nothing in it. The person who watches that line is somebody who is not the man who built anything on that wall and not the man who found it, and that person can be told to stop by a person in a room. None of it goes on the board.**"

Then the five of his agreed to them out loud, one at a time, each of them giving his own name and nothing else, and Dalla Sen wrote none of it down, and the reason she wrote none of it down is that she was asked to by a foreman in a room and because she wanted to.

Then the clerk wrote one line, and this is the whole of what this building put in writing on the seventh day of the thirty-sixth week.

She wrote it in her own book and in her own hand and she did not put a day of the week on it and she did not put a day of anything on it, and then she read it back to the room.

"**That this boundary looked at its own rooms in a room with the people it concerns, and entered nothing.**"

"**And I am entering that,**" she said, "**and the reason I did not put a day on it is that a day of the week is how a counter-schedule begins. If there is a day on it then there is a next one, and if there is a next one then somebody chooses it, and then it is a schedule, and a thing which is scheduled is not a thing which is refused. So there is no day. And I would like a person in a year who finds this line to understand that a line about a morning with no day on it is not a missing day. It is the only instrument this boundary has made in thirty-five weeks that is about itself and not about anybody else.**"

"**And the half against it, which is mine,**" said Bel Ordry, "**is that a clerk who writes one line on a morning when the rest of it is unwritten has made the line the instrument. In ten years somebody will read that line and believe that this boundary did one thing and not nine, and the nine were not smaller, and I would like somebody in this room to understand that a single line is the most dangerous kind of document a room can make.**"

"**Then why are we making it,**" said Nella Bray.

"**Because a clerk who writes nothing at all is a clerk who says that the morning did not happen,**" said Bel Ordry, "**and I have spent this week learning that a fact nobody writes down is a thing that does not travel, and I am not willing to be the reason this morning stays in this room forever.**"

Bel Ordry read it over her shoulder, which he has not done since the thirty-fourth week, and he did not touch the page.

"**Then it goes nowhere,**" he said.

"**It goes nowhere,**" said Dalla Sen. "**It has no seal on it and it is not a form and it is not a notice and nobody outside this building will ever read it, and if a man in an office four days up that passage asks me in a year whether this boundary inspected itself I am going to tell him that I do not know what he is talking about, and I will not be lying, and I will also not be helping him.**"

The board was not walked on the seventh day of a week, and the reason was entered, and the reason was that a man who reads numbers had given a fourth refusal two days ago and had said in his own words that the third time anybody asked him he would want to refuse, and a clerk does not walk a board in front of a man who has just told her what he is waiting for.

And Pell Roke, who is fourteen and has been on the bench at the top of the nine steps with his ear against the stone for the whole of the morning and has not gone down the steps, said the only thing he said.

"**Nobody has ever asked me what the count is for,**" he said, "**and I have got a theory, and it is not a good theory, and I am keeping it.**"

"**Then nobody is going to ask you,**" said Dalla Sen, "**and I am entering that nobody asked you and not what the theory is.**"

The cost of the morning was then said out loud by the woman who keeps a corridor, because it was hers to say and she had said so.

"**Nobody has come back,**" she said. "**A piece of seasoned shore went up this passage on Saturday with a day of the week on it and not a date, and it has not come back, and it cannot today, because today is not a crossing day. And there is a sheet on a board four days up that passage with a line for the date it was observed on it, and the day on that line is a day somebody chose, and we decided on Tuesday not to find out which way, and we are not finding out today either. So this morning has not answered anything and it was never going to.**"

"**Then what was it,**" said Emrys Callow.

"**It was us,**" said Renn Vale. "**That is all it was, and it is not nothing, and I refuse to be the woman who says it is enough.**"

And then the anteroom emptied the way an anteroom empties on an ordinary morning, in ones and twos, and the pump ran its length and stopped. At the seventh bell the clerk entered the day's figure and the total and the count and read none of them. The only thing she was entering that anybody else will ever read is a line with no day on it, and a clerk who reads a total out loud over that is a clerk who has stopped believing her own hand.

Two today and two hundred and seventy-eight since the keeper signed. The boundary is at three hundred and ninety-five and a quarter, and it adds by the day the work was done, both columns reconcile, and the counter is at seventeen. The count was read in her own book and not out loud: one hundred and twenty-seven days and one hundred and twenty-seven nights, and it is not going to be one hundred and twenty-eight, and she has said in thirty-six weeks running that she is going to keep writing it, and nobody has thanked her for thirty-six weeks.

The pot is three days and the seam gave nothing yesterday, and the four households at a second gate have nothing, and no figure is attached to that gate by anybody.

And at the eighth bell, an hour after every bell in this building had been said and gone, a woman who keeps a corridor went and put her ear against the party wall on the anteroom side. At no bell. With nobody with her. She stayed there about the time it takes to say a sentence, four times over, and came back and said out loud, to an empty anteroom, that there was nothing.

She would be at the first bell tomorrow and the second. The wall on the other side of it had five and a half shores on one side, a brace of a school's own beech on the other, and nine children behind that. She had not put a rule in it and had not put her hand in it and still did not know what it was about. A courier who is not this building's had said out loud in this room on Saturday that the two people who can stop her would be on the other side of those nine children. Nobody had answered her.

She was not going to be the one to answer her either.
