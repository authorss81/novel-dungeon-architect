# Chapter 19 — The First Public Work Sheet

By the time the crew returned from the restricted stair, the maintenance draft had been folded and unfolded so many times that its creases had become part of the record.

Dalla laid it on the bench beside the old route charter. Dalla’s local record of the guild papers and Iven’s suspended-bond receipt remained on a separate board. The wrapped empty-seat plate stayed on its spread of work cloth at the far end, its symbol hidden, its evidence undisturbed. Between the plate and the route papers, Dalla placed a clean board wide enough for everyone to see.

“An agreement people can inherit cannot live in one person’s memory,” she said. “It has to say what the next person should do.”

Marek looked at the blank board. He had spent the day mapping a stair and returning to a room where the pump still knocked. The old route charter had one clear purpose: it identified the licensed line. The new work sheet had several. It needed to tell a resident maintainer what to do with a chattering pump, a crew member how to stop a task, an air engineer when to close a baffle, and a boundary keeper when the room should empty.

He began with a clean sentence: *The route is maintained safely until—*

Leena touched the board with two fingers of her left hand. “Until when?”

“The next review.”

“Name the review.”

Marek looked at the room. “At the next water change after two pressure turns. If a condition changes sooner, the review happens sooner.”

Nera tapped the air valve. “The pressure turns are not yet a schedule. Write ‘after a pressure turn’ and let the people at the cloth decide whether the route can resume.”

Iven took the chalk. His hand was steady, but Marek noticed that he kept looking at the suspended-bond receipt folded beside the ledger.

“The pump work needs its own sequence,” Iven said. “Otherwise the next tired person will think the delivery check can be opened while the frame is loaded.”

He wrote:

*Gland dry. Delivery shut before pressure relief. Check seat cleaned without scoring bronze. Frame foot packed, not raised. Pump primed before use. Count the strokes aloud. Stop for a wet gland, a reversed cloth, a new movement at the housing, or water at the sill.*

He paused before the next line.

“Add that I fitted the gland and key,” he said. “Not that I own the pump.”

Jo took the chalk from him. “Add that the person who cleans the check records the time and the cost.”

Iven added it.

Marek watched the craft become a sequence another person could follow without hearing Iven explain it. The work was still his until he gave it away. That was the difference between a craft lesson and a claim.

Nera took the board next. She wrote the air conditions in short lines.

*Outer service door open. Enclosed lantern only; no open flame. Pump chamber isolated during housing work. Anteroom air checked before occupancy. Clinic return watched during pressure turn. Service bypass hatch stays latched. Air reversal, wet mineral seam at the pump, or water at the sill: clear to upper landing.*

She put her name beside the air section.

Sefu read it and added a civic paragraph.

*No deeper passage without the consent of the people responsible for the rooms entered. Sefu may refuse or stop boundary admission. Nera may stop the air mechanism. A resident may refuse a work task or a room without being replaced by a crew member. Withdrawal stops new admission and work; people already inside leave by the outer landing.*

He signed beneath the paragraph and left the pen on the bench.

Dalla took it up again. “Payment and cost are not the same as permission.”

Jo’s name went beside the pump-cleaning line. “The first cleaning took half a day from my tool route. That cost is not paid by the fact that I learned the work.”

Mikka set a bowl of cooked grain near the edge of the board. “The kitchen can provide recovery water and a meal during a named work period. That is support for the shift, not a promise of permanent room and not a claim on anyone’s labor.”

“And Iven’s guild work?” Marek asked.

Iven looked at the frozen-bond receipt. “The guild has suspended the witness privilege. If the guild refuses payment, the Hearthholds are not required to pay it for them.”

Dalla wrote: *Iven Tallo’s work is recorded as a local maintenance contribution; guild reimbursement and personal compensation remain unresolved. No route ownership is created by the debt.*

Marek read the sentence twice. It was not elegant, but it prevented the work from being used as a reason to transfer the pump.

“Add a line for the crew,” he said. “Temporary maintainers receive food and water for scheduled work according to the local count. Their own drinking reserve remains separate. They may withdraw from a future shift. They receive no ownership and no permanent room.”

Mikka looked up. “They receive no permanent room, but Leena may use the clinic cot while her shoulder is treated. That is care, not residence.”

“Write that separately,” Leena said.

Dalla did.

The board was becoming crowded. Marek tried to add a heading for *route* and found that the term still carried too much assumption. He crossed it out and wrote *physical connection* instead.

Then he wrote *pump* and *air gate* and *maintenance shift* and *boundary record*. Each line had a limit beside it. The plate stayed covered on the work cloth. The guild papers stayed apart. The returning stair restriction had its own sheet, attached by a cord to the route record so nobody could lift the pump plan and leave the stair behind.

A knock came from the pump.

Jo looked at the delivery mark. “It is time.”

The room did not become empty. Renn took the door lantern. Dalla kept the ledger. Nera watched the air cloth. Sefu stood at the civic wheel. Oren and Tams took the pump frame and the relief chock. Leena remained on the clinic cot with the board balanced across her knees. Pell sat at the bench, calling the water level and the time.

Marek began to assign the work from memory.

“Jo operates. Iven explains. Oren and Tams take the load. Pell calls the mark. Nera watches the air. Renn watches the door. Sefu can stop the room. Leena records corrections.”

“Who is writing the result?” Dalla asked.

“I am.”

“You are not holding the board flat.”

Marek looked at his left hand, then at the two smooth stones holding the old sheet. His right fingers could not keep the chalk in a line, and his left wrist ached after the stair trip. He could dictate, but a dictated record was not the same as a record made by the people who would use it.

“Dalla,” he said. “You write the result. I will call the structural changes.”

Jo looked at the water mark. “And if I stop?”

“Stop the pump,” Nera said. “Do not wait for permission.”

“If the air cloth changes?” Renn called.

“Clear the room,” Nera replied. “The upper landing is the destination.”

“And if the pump fails while the crew is asleep?” Leena asked.

The question stopped the board. Everyone looked toward the anteroom’s dry floor and the pump gallery below it. The old working rating required the pump to be attended. A crew member could sleep through a change if the water stayed down, but no one had yet said who would listen to the delivery check.

Jo answered first. “The pump stops when the shift ends. If the water needs lowering, a resident or a named crew operator stays with it. No one sleeps while a pump runs without a person assigned to hear it.”

“And if the next shift does not come?” Sefu asked.

Jo looked toward the lower route. “Then the route closes for use until someone can take the watch. We do not let a temporary promise become an unattended machine.”

Nera added a line to the air section: *Pump may run only during a named attended shift. No overnight running without a resident or accepted crew watch.*

Marek had expected the agreement to require more from him. Instead, it required him to stop being the person who automatically carried the next shift. The distinction was practical, but it touched the old part of him that believed responsibility meant being the last person willing to say no.

He placed the chalk beside the board. “Then I will not promise that the crew can cover every future hour.”

Renn looked at him. “You can promise that you will name the hour you can cover.”

“That I can do.”

The pump began its shift.

Jo took the lever. Iven stayed beside the grate, explaining the first check without touching the moving handle. Oren and Tams lifted the frame at the marked points. Pell called the water level. Nera watched the air cloth and the wet seam. Sefu watched the civic wheel. Dalla’s pen moved across the page. Leena called a correction when the pump-parts box was placed too close to the escape line, and Jo moved it without arguing.

Marek watched the floor. He felt a brief pressure change beneath the bearing strip, then the ordinary vibration of the pump. He did not turn the feeling into a command. He stated what he saw: “The frame is answering the load without a new movement at the plinth.”

Dalla wrote it down.

The work sheet did not make the room safe forever. It made the next action less private.

When the shift ended, the relevant maintainers came to the board in turn. Nera accepted the air limits. Sefu accepted the boundary and stop conditions. Jo accepted the pump sequence and its cost. Renn accepted the door watch. Dalla accepted the record. Iven signed the craft section and wrote *not owner of pump* beneath it. Oren and Tams marked the lifting and relief positions. Pell marked his level call and the statement that he could not run or take unsafe loads. Leena signed the corrections with her left hand. Marek signed the structural observations with an uneven line made by his left hand.

The sheet was not a public charter. It named no owner, no permanent route, and no resident assembly that had not assembled. It was an interim maintenance agreement among the people who had chosen to keep this particular pump and anteroom usable.

At the bottom, Dalla left one line open:

*Next review: the second work-bell after the next complete pressure turn, or immediately if air, water, pump, or boundary conditions change.*

Marek looked at the open line. It was not a promise that the route would continue. It was a promise that someone would look again.

Sefu hung the board where the pump crew could see it from the grate. The wrapped plate remained on its work cloth. Dalla’s copies of the guild papers and Iven’s suspended-bond receipt remained on their separate board. The returning stair remained restricted beyond the service bend.

The route was still conditional.

The next shift now had a name, a limit, a cost, and a way to stop.
