# Chapter 17 — When the Air Path Changes

The pressure change began with a drop of water beside the pump.

Before the first bead, Mikka had asked Sefu to open the deeper gate for a named clinic check. Sefu had turned the civic wheel with Mikka’s consent and Dalla’s agreement about the shared return, while Nera watched the air path. The opening was temporary, limited to the clinic corner, and scheduled to close when the check ended. It was not blanket admission, and the patient remained in the room only because Mikka was there.

It was not a splash. A single dark bead formed at the edge of the service well, ran across the stone, and disappeared into a seam beside the housing. Tams was on the lever, Oren had his hand near the relief chock, and Jo was checking the delivery mark when the pump gave its next uneven stroke.

The water bead returned on the following stroke.

Nera saw it from the air valve. “Stop the lever.”

Tams eased back. Oren caught the load before the handle could rebound. The pump settled, and the bead remained on the floor, shining under the lamp.

Marek crouched beside the seam without putting his right hand down. The stone was cool. The pressure beneath it was not. For a moment he felt the same brief pull he had felt beneath the pump plinth, a relative change moving through the old foundation. It was gone before he could measure it.

“A wet mineral seam,” he said. “It is feeding the housing area under pressure.”

“Not feeding the pump,” Nera said. “The gland is dry. The seam is outside the air chamber.”

Pell called from the bench, “The lower mark is unchanged.”

“Good. Keep calling it.”

A sound traveled through the service shaft from below and behind them. It began as a distant scrape, then became the heavy turn of a wheel. The returning stair was moving somewhere out of sight, its rope and counterweight answering a pressure change they could not see. The sound was familiar from the stair map, but here it arrived through the route that had seemed to bypass it.

The air cloth above the air valve lifted and fell.

Nera crossed to it. “Outer door. Now.”

Sefu moved to the civic wheel. “Clear the anteroom to the upper landing.”

“Move the litter first,” Oren said.

Marek looked toward the clinic corner. Through the open boundary arch, Mikka was beside a low cot. Isel Ferrow lay on it, wrapped against a fever that had come up over the night, too weak to stand. The clinic’s low return vent shared a channel with the anteroom’s air baffle, and the channel was beginning to draw backward.

“We cannot empty the anteroom and leave the clinic without a plan,” Mikka called.

Nera was already at the shared baffle. “The pump chamber is isolated. The outer service door is the relief path. I need the clinic return open until I move the patient.”

“Then move her,” Mikka said. “Do not close the room’s air because the anteroom has a problem.”

Marek saw the collision in the plan. If Nera shut the shared baffle to protect the pump, the clinic would lose its clean return. If she left it open, the wet seam and the stair pressure could pull dust and stale air through the boundary. The room could not be made safe by choosing one side and calling the other a neighboring problem.

“Move the cot into the kitchen pass,” Mikka said. “Not the clinic. The clinic stays open for the next person. The kitchen has two exits and no open flame.”

Nera pointed to the upper landing. “Move the patient there first, then to the kitchen pass. Keep the clinic return open.”

Mikka and Jo reached the cot. Oren took the far side; Tams kept the lever locked. Isel was small enough that the cot could be moved without a stretcher, but the floor route narrowed at the clinic threshold. Jo had to turn the cot sideways, and its lower leg caught the wash basin.

Marek moved toward it, then stopped. He could lift with his left arm, but the cot needed a coordinated turn, not a stronger person taking over.

“Take the rail,” Jo said. “I have the lower corner. Mikka, keep the patient’s head level. Oren, take the weight when I say.”

They moved the cot to the upper landing and then into the kitchen pass. The resident’s breathing remained quick but even. The clinic room sat empty, its washable floor exposed, the low return still moving air.

Nera watched the cloth. “The stair is changing pressure again.”

Marek placed his left palm on the bearing strip. The floor gave a shallow answer from the side wall, not the pump. The load was traveling through the common beam and into the service shaft. A thin seam of grit appeared along the threshold between the anteroom and clinic.

Iven crouched beside the service hatch. “We can open the lower bypass. It would equalize the pressure and pull the dirty air down the shaft.”

“No,” Nera said. “The stair is already pushing through that shaft. Open it and you give the pressure a shorter path.”

Marek looked at the hatch. The bypass was continuous stone, not a magical vent. The old map showed it ending at the pump gallery, but the returning-stair pressure cycle had made the air path harder to predict. They had used the hatch to reach the pump, not as an air control. Opening it would be a shortcut with a physical counter-load somewhere below.

“It is the only open line to the lower pressure,” Iven said.

“It is the only line that connects the stair pressure to the pump bay,” Marek said. “We do not know where the displaced air goes.”

Nera put two fingers beside the air valve. “I do know that the bypass shares the wet seam. If we open it while the pump is stopped, the seam can pull room air down. If we open it while the pump runs, the delivery check can carry grit into the return. Neither is a test we can undo.”

“Then we leave it closed,” Marek said. “No shortcut.”

He said it to himself as much as to Iven. The old route map was still useful, but usefulness was not the same as permission to turn every opening into a solution.

The wheel turned again below. This time the sound was close enough that the floor trembled beneath the grate. The water bead at the seam widened into a thin run. It did not reach the sill. The air cloth, however, darkened at one corner.

Nera pulled the stop handle. “Pump stopped. Anteroom empty. Upper landing only.”

Sefu opened the outer service door the full permitted width. Renn moved to the landing with the lantern and watched the stair-side darkness below it. The anteroom emptied in stages: Oren and Tams with the litter, Jo and Mikka with Isel on the clinic cot, Iven carrying the work sheet, Leena calling the turn from where they had set her down, Pell staying above the service grate with the case board.

Marek remained near the bearing strip until the last of them crossed. He wanted to feel the pressure one more time. The brief impression came and went, carrying no direction he could trust.

“Move,” Leena said.

“I am moving.”

“You are staying to listen to stone.”

“The stone is part of the plan.”

“The plan is not more important than your knees.”

He left with the others.

The outer landing was not a room. It was a broad stone step beneath the slope, open to the upper shaft and wide enough for the litter to turn if no one stood in the turning space. Renn placed the lantern where the air could be watched without blocking the escape. Nera kept the air cloth in view. Sefu held the civic wheel at the boundary, ready to close the deeper gate if the pressure reached it.

For several minutes, nothing moved but dust and the patient’s shallow breathing.

Then the returning-stair wheel gave a heavy half-turn. The air cloth snapped outward. A dark line appeared in the wet seam under the pump housing and spread toward the service well. The pump delivery check answered with a hard knock even though the lever was locked.

Tams looked down. “The pump moved.”

“Do not touch it,” Nera said. “The frame is taking pressure.”

Marek moved to the edge of the landing, keeping the outer door between himself and the anteroom. He saw the floor rise a fraction near the service well, then settle. The movement was not a failure. It was a load change traveling through a wet mineral seam and the old stair pressure cycle. The pump, air baffle, and floor were sharing more than they had during the successful test.

“If we leave the pump running through that change, the check will take grit,” he said.

“If we stop it, the water rises,” Tams replied.

Nera looked at the water mark. “The water can rise to the next mark without reaching the sill. We can afford a short stop.”

“For how long?” Sefu asked.

“Not long enough to leave the route unattended. Long enough for the air to clear.”

Marek watched the service well. “The wet seam is at the front edge. If the pressure returns, it will push into the anteroom first.”

“Then the anteroom cannot be used while the stair cycle is active,” Nera said. “The pump is an attended tool, not a reason to occupy the room through every change.”

Sefu looked at the resident on the cot, then at the six outsiders. “The boundary can remain open for the landing. The anteroom cannot hold people during the pressure change. New admission waits.”

Mikka looked toward the clinic. “The clinic needs to remain usable. We cannot turn every air problem into a reason to empty the rooms behind the gate.”

“It is usable if its return stays open,” Nera said. “That requires someone at the clinic vent to report the cloth. I cannot watch the pump, the outer door, and the clinic at once.”

Renn lifted the enclosed lantern. “I can take the upper landing.”

“I can take the clinic return,” Mikka said. “If the cloth changes, I call Dalla. Dalla rings the work bell once for the clinic and twice for the outer valve. When both are clear, she calls the all-clear from the threshold.”

“Put me on the bell line,” Dalla said, moving to the threshold. “I can hear both sides without taking the pump or the civic wheel.”

Nera kept the air valve. Sefu kept the civic wheel. Jo stayed with Isel after Mikka moved the cot to the kitchen pass. Renn took the upper landing, Mikka took the clinic return, and Dalla watched the threshold for either bell.

Marek watched the arrangement form. The pressure change had exposed a new labor obligation that no room test had named. A route could be kept only if someone watched the air at the places it moved, not only at the mechanism that controlled it.

After a long interval, the wheel sound faded. Nera held the dyed cloth over the return. Its color remained unchanged. She opened the pump air isolation by a finger’s width, waited, then closed it again. The air cloth trembled and settled.

“The lower bypass stays latched,” she said. “The service hatch is not an air control. The pump can be primed while the room is empty, but it runs only while the outer cloth and the clinic return agree.”

Marek looked at the water mark. “The pump still has to lower the water.”

“Jo runs it in short attended intervals,” Nera said. “No one sleeps here. No one treats the lower landing as a second room. The stair cycle is not a clock we understand yet, so every cycle is a new condition.”

Mikka called from the clinic. “Cloth unchanged. Patient breathing steady.”

Dalla called from the threshold, “Clinic and outer clear.”

Her voice carried through the gate and faded.

Sefu opened the anteroom for a single attended shift. Ten people could use the room under the old working limit, including one stretcher, for no more than two hours. The deeper gate was closed again once Mikka’s clinic check ended, leaving only the named clinic return under Nera’s air watch. Nera added the new air rule. Sefu added the clinic watch and the stop bell. Dalla wrote the wet seam’s location on the route record, and Iven marked the pump as stopped during every pressure turn until the check could be cleaned.

Marek looked at the old return route in his copied map. It now had three obligations beside it: keep the hatch closed, keep the clinic return watched, and do not treat the returning stair as a passive bypass. The route had become less like a line on a sheet and more like a set of people standing at different points along a changing path.

The pump started again beneath them. Its delivery check knocked once and settled.

The anteroom was open, but only under conditions that had just been revised.
