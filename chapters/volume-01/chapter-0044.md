# Chapter 44 — The Line About Leaving

The tally board had had four columns on it a fortnight ago and had five now, and the fifth one was Tams Quill's own invention, and it was the column that got read first and it was the column with one item in it.

He read it in the anteroom, standing, with the board turned round, because the man who keeps the arithmetic has to read the last line of it himself and nobody has ever been able to make him do it sitting down.

"Spent," he said. "One frayed nine-metre line, on a ladder foot, in six weeks of standing water. That is all it has got in it and that is all it is going to have."

He let that stand for a second, because it was a line about a piece of rope and it was also a line about the only thing the six of them had ever owned.

"And the hours," he said. "Forty-eight crew hours on one side and forty-nine and a half on the other, which I read out in the anteroom nine days ago and which nobody has beaten since, and which is now out of date on both sides because nine days is a fortnight of nothing in this building."

He had the new figures and he had them in his left hand, because his right was in a sling, and he had got into the habit of reading the tally with his left because it slowed him down by about a second and he wanted the second.

"Step one, the mouth, was seven days and three of them were Ilsa's. The floor is laid. That was Ilsa seven, Mikka three and a half, the wash rooms eleven cover checks at a quarter of an hour, Jo two half-days of a route that pays her and is now a day and a half late, and me and Isel on the mark every hour for nine days, which is not a labour, it is a rota, and I am putting it in the column it belongs in and not the one it flatters."

"Nineteen," said Jo Verrick. "It is on the board as nineteen and it was seventeen at the counting, and the two hours in between are mine, and I am not having them in your column either, and I would like that written down before anybody agrees with me."

"Seventeen," Tams said, "and you were there for six of them, and your own hours go on your own board or Dalla's and not in my hand. That is fair, and it is annoying, and it is fair."

"Forty-one and a half on our side," he said. "And on theirs: Ilsa nine, Mikka five and a half, Dalla eleven, Renn four hours offered and not charged for, Jo four, the wash rooms three, and the boundary's five watch-holders across two days of plan and four intervals of draw, which is nine and a half. Forty-six."

"Forty-one and a half and forty-six."

"Forty-one and a half and forty-six," Tams said, "and I have been carrying a figure in my head since the thirty-ninth day that I now have to strike out, and I am going to say why I was wrong and then I am going to say it once and never again. The crew's hours came *down*. Four weeks ago they were forty-eight and the other side was forty-nine and a half and I said to a full room that the crew's labour was the half you can see. It is not the half you can see. It is the half that gets counted." He turned the board a little so the room could see the top of it. "The boundary's forty-six is six named people and a rota, and it is the hours they lost to rooms and watches in nine weeks, and it does not count the nineteen days of meals, because those are days and not hours, and it does not count the shutter route, because that is a route and not a shift, and it does not count a school day, and it does not count a flue wall a cook has chosen to watch instead of brace. And a thing that is countable gets believed and a thing that is not does not, and that is the whole of the arithmetic and the whole of the finding, and I would like somebody in this room to notice that the crew has spent nine weeks being praised for a number."

He put the chalk down and picked it up again, which was how Tams ended a figure.

"And there is one more line and it is not an hour," he said, "and I have been putting it off for a fortnight and Isel is going to ask me about it in a month, so I will say it. The record has six names in a column and it has never once had a line in it that says what happens to the six of us."

The anteroom went the quiet that this room went at, which was not a silence and was not an atmosphere. It was about fifteen people not looking at each other.

"They will want it in the tally," Leena said, from the frame, "and it is not a tally item, because a tally is hours and this is not hours and if you put it in the tally it will be read as a debt, and it is not a debt, and the crew's hours are given and not owed, and that is on a page already."

"Then where does it go," Tams said.

"It goes on the page that says what the six of them are for," said Pell Roke, from the bench.

He had said it in the flat voice he used for a number he had checked twice, and nobody had expected it from him, and every person in the anteroom turned round, and he did not stop, and he had the crutch across his knees and his splinted ankle up on the crate.

"No," he said. "Before anybody answers. It is not going in the ledger and it is not going in Jo's board and it is not going to be a line under the tally. It is going to be said out loud in this room by somebody who is one of the six, once, and then it is going to be written down, because the whole reason the six of us are any use is that we keep the record and we are the only people in this building who do, and the record has forty-one years of a school in it that kept records about itself and it never once wrote down that it was going to close."

He looked at Marek, and then at all of them, and he was fourteen.

"Someone had better say it before somebody else says it for us," Pell said, "and then we can answer it instead of having it answered at us."

---

Nobody said it for about a minute and a half, which is a long time in a room with a pump in it.

Marek said it, and he said it badly, and Jo made him say it again, and the second one was not better and was the one that went in.

"We are going to leave," he said. "Not today. Not this month, and I do not know when, and I am not going to give you a season, because a season is a promise and I have watched this room refuse six good promises in nine weeks and be right about all six. We are going to leave because we are six people from above who came out of a hole and we have never once been told we may live here, and because there is a surface office with a form on it that has my name on it, and because my hands are not what they were and one day they will be worse and there is a place above where a man with two good hands is a man and a man with one is a man who is helped. So we are going to leave. That is not a decision anybody in this room is entitled to stop, and I am not asking to be stopped, and I am saying it here so that the record has it in a mouth and not in a gap."

Nobody stopped him, and Jo did not write it down for a while, and when she did she wrote it in his words and read it back and made him say it a second time to the room, and he did, and the second reading was when it stopped being a thing he was confessing and started being a thing that was in the record.

Sefu Aran said, "No."

"Understood," Marek said.

"You have not understood anything. Say what I am refusing, because you have done the thing where a man hears a refusal as a wall and goes and finds another door." Sefu came off the wheel, which he did not do, and stood with his back to it. "I am not refusing your leaving. I have no standing to refuse a man walking up a shaft, and I said in the thirty-eighth day that I have no standing to bind a stranger to an obligation, and I am not going to spend nine weeks getting that right and then un-spend it because a stranger has finally said the word."

"Then—"

"I am refusing the sentence you put in your own mouth two minutes after it. *Some day they will be worse.*" He said it flatly. "You have written six plans in nine weeks and four of them had a sentence in them about what a room will become, and I have watched Nera take a word out of one of them, and I watched you take it out yourself the second time. And you have just written yourself out of this building on a sentence about a future, and the future has not happened, and if you want to write it then write it in December when your hand is what it is in December. Do not do it in a room where four people are holding your opinion up because you have gone strange about leaving."

Nobody argued with Sefu Aran about a boundary, and Jo wrote the correction in and made Marek put his name under it, and Dalla read both back.

"And now the part that is mine," Sefu said, "and Jo, do not let this be written in my words. Write it in yours and read it back and I will hear it."

Jo wrote, and read it back.

*That a departure is a route. That a route is the one thing in this building the guilds have an instrument for. That six people leaving, on a named day, by the service bypass, is the first route that anybody has ever put in writing in this Hearthhold, and the first document a hearing could ask for, and the crew would be the party to it. That this is not a reason to refuse six people their leave, and it is a reason not to write it down as a day.*

"Write it as a condition instead," Nera said. "I am the last person in this room who should say this and I am going to say it, because the air plan I wrote last week was a method for going into a room once, and I wrote *once* on it on purpose, and I have been thinking about it since and I do not think it was the wrong word."

"A condition," Dalla said, with her pencil ready.

"Two," Nera said. "One: nobody in this boundary is asked to plan around the six of them being here, in the sense of building a rota with their names in it, and the crew are not on the rota, and that has not changed and I would like it said now in the same breath as the leaving, because in three months somebody is going to want to add a sixth to the waist and the reason they will want to is that two of the six are leaving."

"And the second."

"The second is harder and it is not mine." Nera stopped, and started again, which she did perhaps twice in nine weeks. "The school corner's mouth was shut on Friday and the fall is cut and the floor is laid and the grit has not come up once in six days. And the air at that corner has changed. I have one cloth reading that I cannot account for and I have had it for two days, and what I am telling you is that the shut has moved something in a shaft I cannot see into, and the only thing in this building that is reading it for me is the pump gallery, and the pump gallery has four hours of standing air left in it as of this morning. So: somebody has to be able to keep reading it, and it will have to be a person who is here, and I cannot write a valve board that assumes a man with one good hand is standing at it in February."

"So the leaving is a condition on the air," Marek said.

"No. The leaving is not a condition on anything. The leaving is six people telling a room a true thing at the correct time, and my sentence is that the air does not know and does not care and will be read by whoever is here, and that the correct time to say the rest of it is not today." She hung the cloth on its hook. "Say it again in the spring. Say it to Ilsa and to Renn and to the woman who lifts the cover, and let them write down what they need, and then do it. And the condition is that the reading gets handed to a person before the six of them go, and not after, and I will not be the one who decides that it was all right afterwards."

---

Iven Tallo had been standing by the anteroom bench with a length of timber across his shoulder, and he put it down, and the sound it made on the floor was the sound the rest of that morning was about.

"Three lengths, five wedges, a jack, and a pry bar," he said. "And I have been turning that over since the second day and I have got to the bottom of it and the bottom of it is that we have a shore and no anchor, and that we have a shore made out of the wrong timber."

The room came round.

"A shore is a piece of wood leaning on something," Iven said. "That is all a shore is. Foot on the floor, head on the face of the thing, and the wedges take the slack, and a shore is the oldest work there is and it is what you put up when you have a wall and no time. Now: what I have is three foot four, two foot nine, and a stub. Six foot one of timber if I scarf them, and the one I want is about nine, and the nine is on a shelf under six feet of water with a number chalked on it. So the shore is three pieces."

He put a hand flat on the bench, the way a man does with a thing he is about to be unkind about.

"And I want that written somewhere, because I am a carpenter and this is a carpenter's shame and it is going to be the reason somebody has to hold the head of that shore with his hands every single time a rope comes tight. A built-up head is a good head until it is loaded, and then it is three pieces doing a job and two of them are not in the same piece of wood, and the joint between them is a place where the wood can fold. It is not a bad shore. It is a shore that has to be *tended*, and tending it is a person, and a person is the one thing in this building that we have never once had enough of and have never once had enough of for free." He straightened up. "The jack goes under the foot and packs it to bearing, and that is the one job on this earth a screw jack is good at, and anybody in this room who thinks I am going to jack a pump frame on one foot can go and read the twelfth day again. The pry bar is for the haunch of the step where the shore's heel wants something to push against, and there is a haunch there, and it has been under a foot for a hundred years and it is loose."

"And the anchor," Marek said.

"The anchor is what stops the head coming out." Iven took his hand off the timber. "A raking shore, head bearing on a wall, foot wedged on a floor. The wall is the near wall of that stair, and the wall is one stone and the wall is not going anywhere, and if the head comes out it comes out into the throat. And you stop a shore's head coming out with something behind it. Something on the other side of the wall, or something over the top of it, or a line off it. And I have been in this room nine weeks and I know all three of those, and I want to say them out loud so that the six of you have them in front of you the way I have had them in front of me since Tuesday."

He held up a finger.

"One: the other side of that wall is eleven feet of shaft with a floor nobody has seen, and I am not going to be a man who puts a line into a hole on the strength of a guess, and neither are you, and if anybody in this room has been thinking about that it is a thought to be written down and labelled and left alone."

"A second finger."

"Two: the top of the wall. There is nothing on it. I have looked at it from the fixed landing twice and it is dressed stone and no ring, and the school's wall is a hundred years of a school and a school does not ring its walls, and a hundred years of nothing is not a place to hang forty feet of line on a guess either."

"Third."

"Three: the east stair." Iven's voice did not change, which is how it changed. "The fixed rail. It is the only piece of iron I know of in this building that somebody put there on purpose, and it is there because somebody at some point wanted a hand on it going down a stair in the dark, and it is the one thing in this room with a maker's intent in it. The cord comes off the shore's head, up over the wall, onto the fixed rail, and down to two rings in the common landing's floor."

"Which does not exist," Dalla said.

"Which does not exist," Iven agreed. "And that is the whole of the problem and it has been the whole of the problem for three days, and it is not a carpenter's problem, and I have been waiting to see whether anybody in this room was going to say so, and nobody has, and so I am going to say it and then I am going to go and wash, because I have been carrying a piece of timber about this room since the second day and I would like somebody else to carry it for ten minutes."

---

The black cord was forty feet on a hook in the boundary stores.

It had been there since the eighteenth day, when Sefu Aran had taken a length of it and tied it across the fixed upper approach of a stair nobody was going to cross. It had not been touched since. Iven had asked about it in the second week and been told no and had not asked again, which was in the record in Nera's hand on a valve board, and which was therefore, by the standards of this room, a fact.

Nera had written it there so that the next person would know it had been asked once and not twice.

Sefu said it out loud before anybody could ask, which was his way, and which had been his way since the ninth week.

"The black cord," he said. "It is ours. It is not the crew's and it is not the stair's, and it is not in the stores of a building that keeps things for anybody in particular, it is on my hook because I put it there. I will release it for a named work and I will release it now, and I am going to say the price before I say yes, because I have watched six of you work for nine weeks and every one of you who is any use has learned that way."

He put his hand on the wheel and left it there, which meant he was at his post, and said it anyway.

"The price is a lead wears. You take a line and you lay it in a joint and the joint cuts it, and the day the joint has cut it through, the thing on the other end of that line stops being held. And on that day somebody has to stand on that stair with a new line and hold the two ends while it is done, and that is not a thing I am going to be able to ask Renn Vale for, and it is not a thing I am going to be able to ask anybody for, because it is a watch, and Renn has said in front of nine people that she will not stand a watch on a stair day, and she said it wider than the wet seam and she was right."

"Then the page says it wears and the page says we will find out who does that," Dalla said.

"No," Sefu said. "The page says it wears, and the page says who has to hold it, and the answer to the second one is that we do not have one yet, and I am not going to let a page in this building carry a line with nobody's name under it, because that is the ninth clause of a terms page in this very project and I will not have it twice. So the page carries the wear as a known condition, and it carries the answer as *not yet*, and *not yet* is in Renn's hand and not mine, and I will be asking her in the morning and not in front of this room."

"You have never once asked her in front of the room," Jo said.

"I have asked her in front of the room exactly once," Sefu said, "on the thirty-eighth day, and she refused twice, and it was the correct use of a public room and I would do it again. I am not doing it in a room where the person I am asking has just been told that the six of them are leaving, because that is not an information she needs while she is being asked to hold a rope."

And that was the night, and the tally was read out again at the fourth bell by Tams Quill, and the *spent* column was read out first and still had one item in it, and the *they* column was forty-six hours and belonged to six named people and had not gone down by one hour in a fortnight of days that Ilsa Norr had lost.

And in the stores, on a hook, forty feet of black cord came off the hook, and Dalla Sen wrote the time on it and the reason and the keeper's name, because that is what a keeper is, and because in eleven weeks somebody was going to read it and would find out that the anchor on a returning stair in a Hearthhold came out of a boundary's own store on a night when six people told the truth in a room with eleven people in it.
