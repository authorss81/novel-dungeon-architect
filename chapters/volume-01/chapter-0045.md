# Chapter 45 — A Shore on the Common Landing

The geography of that end of the boundary had never been written down in one place, and on the morning the crew went to work on it, Jo Verrick made a person say it out loud and made herself write it, because everything they were about to do happened in a corridor, a doorway, a landing, three steps, and a hole.

Jo said it with the chalk and a hand's span of board, and Iven Tallo said it, because Iven had been walking it for nine weeks.

"The corridor runs from the kitchen line, past the hearth, past the clinic door, through the dip, through the waist at two foot eight, and ends at the school door," he said. "The school door is two foot eight at the head under a cracked lintel held by two shoring lengths and three wedges and a hand line, and it wants four and has not got four. Through it is the common landing, which is the head of the boundary stair. The stair goes down three steps to the service bend, and past the bend is the fixed first landing, and that landing is a lip over eleven feet of shaft, and the shaft is under the common landing, which is why the corridor's low end stands on top of it. The anteroom is a level up with the pump under its floor, and you get from the anteroom down to the common landing by nine steps."

"Nine," Leena said.

"Nine this morning. Nine is a number, and a number in a record is a thing to be checked, and I have counted it this morning and you can go and count it," Iven said, "and I am not writing a plan that says *a flight*."

Jo wrote the nine and underlined it, and it was the first time since the beginning that a number had got into a record because a person had said it out loud in advance of anybody needing it.

---

The marks were the first work of the day, and they were done by the wrong person on purpose.

Marek Venn could hold a tape, read a level, stand a plumb, and say what a number meant. He could not put a mark on a floor and have it stay a mark, and everyone in the room had known that for eight weeks, and the reason the marks were taken on the common landing and not at the shore's head was not an accommodation and was a decision.

"A chalk line is a reference," Leena Orr said, "and a reference does not move, and the whole of the trouble with this building is that everybody in it wants a mark to mean where a thing is *going* to be. So the marks are made where the person making them can stand, and they are read by a person going to work, and they are not to be chased. If the mark is not where you are, the mark is right and you are wrong, and you go and look again instead of adjusting the floor."

She was on the common landing with a plumb, a chalk, a rule, and a piece of the three-foot four that Iven had cut off the end of a length, and she marked four things and only four things.

The foot of the shore, on the common landing's floor, four foot seven back from the wall, because the angle of a shore is the shore, and you do not move a shore's foot to suit a mark; you move the mark to suit the shore. Six foot one of timber at a four-foot head stands there and nowhere else, and Iven had done it in his head twice on the walk up and was not going to be asked why.

The head, on the wall's face, at the height the line will arrive, which was four feet above the landing's floor and level with the second step of the east stair's masonry.

The haunch, on the third step down, where the pry bar goes.

The fourth was not a place. It was a depth, chalked on the wall by the head mark, and it was the only one of the four she spent four minutes on, and she made them all wait.

"It is how far the wall's face leans out from the floor over six feet of height," she said, "and it is a hand's breadth and a bit, and if you had a square and you set it on the floor against the wall and you came up the wall with it, it would stand a foot clear at the top. So the head of a shore does not touch the wall flat. It touches it on a face, and the face has to be made, and the face has to be made *once* and by a person who knows what a face is, and if it is made badly then the shore is a wedge with a bit of wood on it."

"Iven," Marek said.

"Iven," Leena said. "And I am not coming down the three steps to watch it, because I do not go down three steps, and because the face is cut from the common landing side, and because Jo is going to carry the marks down to the slate and you are going to work off the slate and not off my chalk, and if the slate and my chalk ever disagree then the slate is a copy and mine is the original, and you will come up nine steps and look at mine and you will not touch my floor."

And Jo carried the marks down. She copied them onto the stair slate in her own hand, larger than the rest of the writing, which is what she did with anything she wanted a person to be able to read in the dark, and read them back on the common landing before anybody went down, and read them back again at the service bend, and the second reading was the one the crew worked from and Jo said so.

---

The terms were read at the threshold by Jo in her own hand and it took a quarter of an hour, and it is the fifth time that has happened, and every time it has taken longer.

The first was the old one, and nobody argued with it: the stair stays corded, nothing in this project opens it, and no person is put on the slab.

The second: a named period, a day the room has agreed, an end set by the cord or the cloth or a watcher's word, the party out of the step in under a minute, and the near side as found.

"Read the last four words again," Renn said, from above.

"The near side as found," Jo said.

"I want the clerk to write under it, and I want it in the keeper's hand and not in the crew's," Renn said, "and I want it read back: *and except for the shore, which is the work, which is the one thing this project is for, and which is going to be standing here after you have gone, and I have not decided about it and I will decide it after I have seen it.*"

Everybody looked at Sefu, because that is what a boundary keeper is for.

"In nine weeks nobody has asked to write an exception into clause two," Sefu said. "So: the answer is no, and the exception is right anyway, and the difference goes down with both words in it and the date on it, so that in a year nobody has to guess which of us said which. Jo — refused as a clause, granted as a fact, and both of them in your hand and not in mine."

The third was Mikka's, in her own words, and it was in and nobody had tried to move it: no work while the covers are being lifted, at the second bell and the fifth, and if a person is not on the stair then nobody is in that wash room.

The fourth: no work on a day the corridor is open, none on a day the school runs, and none between the second bell and the fourth.

The fifth was the new one and it was Renn's, and it was about the black cord, and it went on the page before a single length of timber was cut.

"I have read what Sefu said about the lead wearing," Renn said. "I am not going to stand a watch on a stair day. I said it in the thirty-eighth day in front of nine people and I said it was wider than the wet seam and I have not withdrawn a syllable of it and I am not going to now because a man has found a use for my line. So the cord goes over the wall and onto the fixed rail and it comes back down, and where it goes over the wall it is six feet below my landing's floor and it does not come near my door, and I will see to that myself tonight and I will not be told about it. And when the joint has cut it, which it will, then there is a day coming when somebody has to hold two ends of a line on a stair, and I am not that somebody, and I am not going to pretend now that I might be. That goes on the page with my name under it as *not yet*, and *not yet* is a word in this building and it is not a promise."

---

They worked on a day the school was not running, which meant the corridor was shut, which meant the crew got to the common landing through a school door with a cracked head over it that is one wedge short of what it wants.

Everybody thought about it on the way in and said nothing, and Oren Sill stood under the head of it for longer than he had to, with his hand flat on the jamb, the way a man does with a thing he has carried, and then went through.

"Price," Iven said, before anybody started, because a price said late is a price said because somebody asked. "Two days of a carpenter that nobody in this building is going to pay me for, and I have stopped counting those hours out loud, and the number in the ledger is Dalla's business and not mine. Five hardwood wedges off a public-works register that the keeper of this boundary has to receive in a ledger. Forty feet of the boundary's own line, released by its owner, on a road she did not ask for. And one fifth thing, which nobody can price, which is that I came into this building a stranger in a room and that there is now a piece of my work under a woman's floor and she has not asked me a single question about it."

"Noted," said Dalla Sen, and wrote it, and Iven looked at her and she said, "The wedges and the line and the two days, and the fifth thing in your own words, which is the way you said it, and I am not improving it."

The bell at the threshold belonged to Dalla Sen, who was in the anteroom with the ledger, and Dalla could not be in two places, and Sefu Aran was going to be on the common landing with the cord, and so the bell needed a deputy, and the deputy had to be somebody who had been asked and was not one of the four people who keep a room.

Sefu asked Isel Ferrow at the water mark, in front of four people, and told him what he was being given, which no one had done for the last person who stood at that bell.

"It is not a watch," Sefu said. "It is a bell. You pull it if the cloth moves or if I say the word or if anybody shouts, and then you go up the anteroom steps and you stand at the top of them, and you do not come down them. I am telling you now so that nobody can say afterwards that you were given it. Do you want to know what it is for?"

"Yes," said Isel Ferrow, and stopped, and started again. "Yes. Because the other day Mikka said the rota is a list of people and not a list of jobs, and I have been at the water mark for nine days and I am not to be first at anything for a month, and I do not want to be at a bell and not to be told what a bell is, because that is how you end up first at something by accident."

Sefu Aran, who had not been asked a question by a resident in nine weeks, took that without moving a muscle.

"The bell is the stop," he said. "The cloth is the stop. The cord is the stop. Any of the four of them saying the word is the stop. And the bell is how a stop gets from the stair to the anteroom, because I will be on the common landing with a rope and I will not be able to see the cloth. Pull it, go up the nine steps, stand at the top. That is the whole of it. And the moment you have pulled it, you are not the man on the mark, you are the man on the bell, and the mark waits for Isel to come back, and I will not have a person doing both because a person doing both is doing neither."

"Isel is me," said Isel Ferrow.

"Which is the difficulty," Sefu said, "and I have thought about it, and I am not going to have you at the bell and the mark, and I have asked Mikka, and the mark is hers for the length of this period, and she has said yes, and I am telling you that she has said yes so that you do not have to go and ask her and wonder."

Isel Ferrow stood at the water mark with a lamp in one hand and an expression nobody in the room could read and went up the nine steps to the anteroom to find the bell rope.

---

They did the crossing test first, before anything was cut, and it was Oren's job and nobody voted on it.

Jo gave him a length of the three-foot-four and told him what the four marks were and in what order, and he carried it down the three steps with the cord about two feet of slack and a lamp in his other hand, and he walked the near landing once from the bend to the lip and back and up the three steps and off.

It came back at the fourth stroke of the cord, and the cord went tight, and the slab started, and it was not fast and it was not slow and it was over about a second, and the whole party was up the three steps and off the landing in fifty-two seconds, which is the slowest figure in the record and which Jo wrote down and read back, and she said why she had written it down.

"Fifty-two," she said, "and everybody who reads that ledger in a year is going to think somebody was slow. It is in here because it is the one that proves the party can carry a length over that cord and come back, and it is slower than the two times before it because the times before it were a party going out with their hands empty and this one was a party going out with the end of a three-foot-four in its hands, and a man cannot run over a lip he cannot see. Write it and leave it, because the next person who wants to hurry a crossing will read it."

Pell Roke had it off the plinth on the bench at the top of the anteroom steps, with his ear on the stone and his crutch across his knees, and he had called the interval before the wheel, and he had called it eight seconds, and the wheel came about four seconds behind him, as it always had, and eight had not been the number before.

"Eight seconds, this time," he said, and then, before anybody could build anything on it, "eight seconds this time. I am not going to say eight. I am going to say that I called it and I called it right, and I do not know what I get."

"Write that," Jo said, "and write *I do not know what I get* in his words and in his own line, because a boy who has been right once and told the room he does not know what he gets is the only reader this building has ever had."

---

The face on the wall took a day and a half and it was the only skilled work anybody in this building has done since the collapse that a person from outside it could have done at all.

Iven cut it from the common landing side with a chisel he had made out of the old flat file's tang and a hammer Jo's neighbour had lent and taken back the same afternoon, and he did it standing up, and he talked the whole time in the voice he used for teaching, which nobody had ever heard him use on purpose before.

"You are not cutting a shape, you are cutting a bearing," he said. "A bearing is a face the wood can push against without the wood splitting and without the wall splitting. So the face is not flat, it is *set* — it leans back about a hand's breadth over six feet, and the top of it is a little proud and the bottom of it a little hollow, and the head sits in the hollow and the proud bit at the top is what stops it climbing. Cut it flat and the head rides up the wall the first time the line comes tight, and a shore that climbs is not a shore, it is firewood leaning on a wall for a while."

"And you will not show me how to do that," Marek said.

"I will show you how to do it exactly the same way I showed Jo the lift bar," Iven said. "Which is that I will not. You have a hand that cannot hold a chisel and a body that does not know my trade, and if I put you on a wall face with a chisel I will be doing two things: making a mistake, and teaching a man who will be gone in six months that somebody stood here once. And the second one is worse than the first, and I have watched the first kind of thing kill people in galleries and I have never once watched the second kind kill anybody and it has ruined four good carpenters that I know of."

Marek wrote *null* on the corner of the slate and did not hand it over and did not keep it, which was what he had started doing, and Jo took it off the bench at the end of the period without being asked and pocketed it, and it was the sixth, and nothing had come back.

Oren Sill packed the foot to bearing, and it is worth saying how, because it is the whole of what the crew is going to lose in the forty-eighth.

"You do not pack a shore with wedges," Oren said. "Wedges take up slack. A shore is packed with a *face* and a face is made by putting the right piece of wood in the right place and then leaning on it until it goes in. So: a piece of the packing offcut, sized, set at the toe, and then a man on the head of the timber with his whole weight and his own knee, and the wood goes in under him or it does not go in at all, and if it does not go in you take it out and cut a different one, because a shore packed with wood that was not sized is a shore that has a spring in it, and a shore with a spring in it puts its head out four months later and takes a person's hand off at the knuckle."

That did not go on the page. It went into the tally under Tams's own column, headed *carried*, and Tams wrote it out longhand at the fifth bell, in his left hand, slowly, on purpose, because it is the single sentence in nine weeks that he was most frightened of losing, and he said so when he wrote it, and nobody took the slate off him.

Then the pry bar, into the haunch of the third step, and the haunch came up about an inch and gave a dead flat sound, and Iven looked at the face the step made and said *that* and went to cut another one, and Oren packed that too, and the two of them worked a full day on a step nobody in this building had looked at since it was cut, and at the end of it the step was doing what a step does.

And the turn came at the twenty-second minute of the second period, and Nera's voice went up the string in two words, and the party was off the near landing and up the three steps and out of the cord in forty-four seconds, which Jo wrote down, and the shore stood.

That was the part that stopped the room.

The near side was not as found. There was a piece of timber on the common landing's floor, a face cut in a wall, two wedges driven in a haunch, a pry bar's mark, and a jack under a foot, and the clause said *as found*, and the exception said *the shore, which is the work*.

"Then we find out what a thing that stands is," Dalla said from the anteroom, "or we take it out, and I am telling you now that I know which of those two is going to happen and so do you, and the reason I am saying it out loud is that I have watched six people in this building put things where they can be pointed at, and every single time the point has come due somebody has had to have an answer written in a hurry, and in a hurry it is always written badly."

"Write it honestly then," Sefu said. "Write *the shore stands, and it is the work, and the work is a proposal, and a proposal that stands is a thing with a keeper or it is a thing that gets taken out in a year by a person with a bar and no memory of why.*"

"Whose keeper," said Renn.

And nobody answered for about as long as it takes to boil a pot.

"The crew is not," said Jo, in the end, flatly, because nobody else would say it and it was her board and she was going to be the one who had to hold it. "The crew cannot be the keeper of it. The crew are not on the rota, the crew are not a maintenance party, the crew are six people who told this room twenty minutes ago that they are going to leave. If a thing in this boundary stands on the crew's word, then in six months it is a thing on nobody's word, and the first person to find it will be a person with a bar, and he will be entirely right to take it out."

"Then it has to be one of us," said Mikka Hale, "and it is not going to be me. The kitchen brace was stopped on the twenty-fourth day and I have been watching a flue wall ever since and I have exactly nothing left, and I would rather say that now than in six weeks."

"It is not going to be Jo," said Jo.

"It is not going to be me," said Dalla Sen, "because I keep the ledger, and a keeper who keeps the ledger is a keeper who is asked to write down that a thing failed, and the first time a person has to do that about a thing they hold, they will find a reason not to have held it."

"It is not going to be the woman from the wash rooms," said Renn, before anybody could, "and I will tell you why and I will tell you at once so that nobody spends a morning working round it. She has a room and a mother and a morning in which she lifts a cover. That is everything she has and I am not going to be the one who takes a morning off it. If I want to find out what she would say I will go to her door and ask her and stand there while she says no, and I have not done that and I am not going to do it in a room."

"It will be me," said Renn Vale.

And nobody said anything, and it was Ilsa Norr who asked the question, and she asked it in the flat voice she used for a thing she had decided on the way into the room.

"What is it," she said. "I want to know what the thing actually is before I let anybody answer for me, because if it is a thing under my floor then it is a different thing, and if it is a thing under a man's landing then it is a different thing again, and I have been in this room for nine weeks and I have watched everybody here take a decision that was not theirs and I have decided this morning that I am not doing it."

Renn said it, in her own words, and Dalla wrote it in her own words, and Renn made her put her name under it.

"The shore stands on the common landing, four foot seven back from the wall, with its head against the wall's face and its foot wedged, and it is tied by a line that comes up over the wall and through the fixed rail and back down to its own foot. The line is the boundary's and it is on my hook and it is released to the work and not to the crew, and when the joint has cut it, somebody has to hold two ends, and I have said no to that and I have said it wider than the seam, and the line will want replacing and the man who replaces it will be whoever I ask on the day. That is a road, not a wall. So: the shore is mine, and the road is mine, and nobody in this room may come up and look at it."

"You are the keeper of a shore and a lead in a wall you will not let them look at," Iven said.

"I am the keeper of a thing under my own floor that a stranger put in my house," Renn said, "and I have decided, out loud, in a room, today, and if it is wrong then in six months it comes out and it comes out on my word and not on anybody else's, and that is a thing I can live with, and a stranger's shore that four of us are quietly responsible for is a thing none of us can live with."

And Jo wrote it down, and read it back, and it went into the ledger in Renn's words, and the tally that night had a new column heading on it that Tams Quill had to invent again, and the heading was *kept*, and there was one item in it, and one, and it was a shore with a cut face in a wall, on a landing under a door with four people asleep over it, in a building with no sound timber over six feet and seven sound wedges and a scored bronze seat with two cleanings on it or fewer.

The five public-works wedges were driven into the shore's head, four of them, and the fifth into its foot, and the school-door head did not get its fourth, and Renn refused it in her own words on the first morning, out loud, to four people, and Dalla wrote it down with her name under it.

"That head wants a fourth wedge and it is the crew's way into that landing and I would like it, and I am going to say no, and here is the reason and it is not that I am hard. That head has been one short for a fortnight and it has come down a finger and been packed and it has not hurt anybody, because nobody in this building has come through it in the dark. If I put a fourth wedge in that head then the next thing to want a wedge is the clinic sill, and the thing after that is the kitchen, and there is a queue of things in this building that want a wedge and the queue is longer than the seven. So the head goes short. And if it comes down, it comes down on me, and I will be standing under it, because that is where the door I keep is."

Nobody argued with her. The fifth wedge went into the shore's foot, and the school-door head ended the day with three wedges and two shoring lengths and a hand line, and one wedge short of what it wanted, and no fourth to be found in this building or any other.
